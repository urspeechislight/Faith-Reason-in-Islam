The Qur'an and Its Critics

Does Allah Pray to Muhammad?

A Christian charge says the verb yuṣallūna in Qur'an 33:56 means prayer, so Allah prays upon the Prophet Muhammad ﷺ, and it asks why the verse avoided the plain word for blessing. The dictionaries, the poets, and the Qur'an's own verses show the verb carries mercy, praise, and honor. A Twelver Shia answer from al-Kafī to al-Mīzān.

The Charge Under Examination

The Charge

Christian polemicists in Arabic debates and videos read one verb of one verse literally. The verse states that God and His angels yuṣallūna upon the Prophet ﷺ. The charge takes that verb to mean the ritual prayer of worship. Then it asks a trap question. To whom does Allah pray? To Muhammad ﷺ, which would make a creature the addressee of God's worship, or to someone above Allah, which no Muslim accepts. The page speaks to two readers at once. It builds the school's reading for readers who accept the Qur'an as God's word, and it answers the charge on the Arabic evidence that any reader of the language can weigh.

The Answer in Outline

The answer moves in layers, and each layer feeds the next. The dictionaries of classical Arabic print the mercy sense inside their entries for this very root. Pre-Islamic poets used the construction as an invocation for a person, with God as the addressee of the prayer. The Qur'an itself fixes the divine verb with a purpose clause in the same sura, and the formula the verse produced joins the two verbs the charge tries to split. The Imams of the Ahl al-Bayt ﵈ defined the verse agent by agent, and the scholars of the school built one rule for verbs said of God and of creatures together.

Premise 1: The dictionaries define the word as prayer

The lexicon entries for ṣalāt, from the root ṣ-l-w, begin with supplication and the ritual prayer. Supplication, the charge says, is addressed upward to a god. So a verb of prayer, said of Allah, makes Allah the one who prays. The evidence this premise leans on is real. The dictionaries do say it. The page quotes them in full below.

Premise 2: The preposition aims the act at the Prophet

The verse reads yuṣallūna ʿalā al-nabiyy, upon the Prophet. The charge reads the Arabic preposition ʿalā as direction toward a person, as though the Prophet stood at the receiving end of the act the way an addressee receives speech.

Premise 3: The word choice hides the real sense

Arabic owns a plain verb for blessing, bāraka, and the Qur'an uses it of Abraham's household. The charge argues that an eloquent Book would say yubārik if blessing were the meaning, and that the choice of the prayer verb betrays the prayer sense.

Premise 4: The mercy gloss is a patch

Muslim interpreters explain that God's ṣalāt means mercy and praise. The charge calls this late damage control, invented to hide an embarrassment that the early Arabs would have heard plainly.

Conclusion: The charge collapses at every step

The same dictionary pages the charge cites also print the agent by agent senses, mercy from God, invocation from creatures. The poets show the construction as a prayer for a person, addressed to God, in verse older than Islam. The Qur'an's own usage fixes the divine verb as mercy with a stated purpose. The formula the verse produced uses both verbs together and addresses Allah alone. The Imams of the Ahl al-Bayt ﵈ defined the verse in reports, and their scholars answered the strongest form of the grammar objection centuries before modern polemics. Allah does not pray to Muhammad ﷺ, and the verse never says He does.

1. What the Arabic Word Actually Says

Contested words. Yuṣallūna ʿalā, the second stem of the root ṣ-l-w with the preposition ʿalā and a human object. The noun ṣalāt and its plural ṣalawāt, the noun duʿāʾ, the verb bāraka, and the clause sallimū taslīmā carry the adjoining claims.

Method. Construction first, then the dictionaries, then the poets, then the Qur'anic usage.

The dispute turns on a word as built and as placed. The root in the abstract decides nothing, because the root carries several branches. The verse uses one branch, the second stem with ʿalā and a person, and Arabic uses that branch for invocation and honor upon the person named.

The Word as Built and as Placed

Verse: Qur'an 33:56

Locus: the second stem of ṣ-l-w, with the preposition ʿalā and a human object

Claim: the construction marks the Prophet ﷺ as the beneficiary of the act, and Arabic gives the preposition that job

Two Roles the Charge Collapses Into One

An invocation has an addressee, the one spoken to, and a beneficiary, the one spoken for. Arabic keeps the two apart with its prepositions. The second stem of ṣ-l-w with ʿalā and a person marks that person as the beneficiary of good, the one the prayer is for. Every funeral in the Arabic world still uses this exact construction, ṣalāt ʿalā al-mayyit, the prayer over the dead. No one on earth, of any confession, takes the funeral prayer to be worship addressed to a corpse.

The addressee of the blessing has its own marker, the vocative. The formula this verse produced opens with Allāhumma, O Allah. The charge gives the preposition the work of a vocative. That one collapsed role is the whole argument. The dictionaries below show what the construction carried, and they show it inside the entries the charge itself quotes.

The preposition marks the beneficiary. The vocative names the addressee. The charge swaps the two roles, and the swap is the whole argument.

The Dictionaries Print the Mercy Sense

Source: Ibn Sīda (d. 458 AH), al-Muḥkam wa-al-Muḥīṭ al-Aʿẓam, vol. 8, p. 372

Claim: the entry defines God's ṣalāt as His mercy and His praise, inside the dictionary itself

Ibn Sīda's Entry

Ibn Sīda stands among the two greatest lexicographers of classical Arabic. His entry for the root lists the ritual prayer first, because his dictionary reflects the usage of his day, and then keeps unwrapping the word.

والصلاةُ : الدُّعاءُ والاسْتِغفارُ .

وصلاةُ اللّهِ على رسولِه : رَحْمتُه له وحُسنُ ثَنائِه عليه .

Wa-ṣ-ṣalātu ad-duʿāʾu wa-al-istighfār. Wa-ṣalātu Allāhi ʿalā rasūlihi raḥmatuhu lahu wa-ḥusnu thanāʾihi ʿalayh.

"And al-ṣalāt is invocation and the seeking of forgiveness."

"And God's ṣalāt upon His Messenger is His mercy for him and His beautiful praise of him."

- Ibn Sīda (d. 458 AH), al-Muḥkam wa-al-Muḥīṭ al-Aʿẓam, vol. 8, p. 372

The gloss the charge calls a late patch sits inside a dictionary, in the entry for the root, printed nine centuries before any modern polemic. The dictionaries never deny the prayer sense. They index the whole word, and the whole word includes the divine sense, mercy and praise.

What the Ṣiḥāḥ Headed the Entry With

Al-Zabīdī's Tāj al-ʿArūs gathers the classical lexicons, Lisān al-ʿArab among them, and he credits al-Jawharī's Ṣiḥāḥ for the headline of the entry.

وأَمَّا مَعْناها : فقيلَ : الدُّعاءُ ، وهو أَصْلُ مَعانِيها ، وبه صَدَّرَ الجوهريُّ التَّرْجمة .

يقالُ : صَلَّى على فلانٍ إذا دَعَا لَهُ وزَكَّاهُ .

Wa-ammā maʿnāhā fa-qīla ad-duʿāʾ, wa-huwa aṣl maʿānīhā, wa-bihi ṣaddara al-Jawharī al-tarjamah. Yuqālu ṣallā ʿalā fulānin idhā daʿā lahu wa-zakkāh.

"As for its meaning, it is said to be invocation, which is the origin of its senses, and with this al-Jawharī headed the entry."

"One says ṣallā ʿalā fulān, upon so and so, when he invoked for him and magnified him."

- al-Zabīdī (d. 1205 AH), Tāj al-ʿArūs, vol. 19, p. 606

The formula sentence settles the construction question on its own. Ṣallā ʿalā fulān means he invoked for him. The preposition ʿalā marks the beneficiary of the invocation. The charge needs the preposition to mark an addressee, and the dictionary hands the preposition the other job.

Ibn al-Aʿrabī on the Very Verse's Verb

وقال ابنُ الأعرابيّ : الصَّلاةُ مِن اللَّهِ الرَّحْمةُ ؛ ومنه : (هو الذي يُصَلِّي عَلَيْكم) ، أَي يَرْحَمُ .

Wa-qāla Ibn al-Aʿrabī, aṣ-ṣalātu mina Allāhi ar-raḥmah, wa-minhu huwa lladhī yuṣallī ʿalaykum, ay yarḥam.

"Ibn al-Aʿrabī said, the ṣalāt from God is mercy, and from it is His word He it is who yuṣallī upon you, that is, He has mercy upon you."

- al-Zabīdī (d. 1205 AH), Tāj al-ʿArūs, vol. 19, p. 607, from Ibn al-Aʿrabī (d. 231 AH)

A third century authority reads the same verb, in the same sura, with the same preposition, as mercy. Al-Jawāliqī, the language teacher of Baghdad, hands the full rule to students in one line. His rule reads, ṣalāt from God is mercy, from the angels invocation and seeking of forgiveness, from people the prayer with bowing and prostration.

والصلاة من الله الرحمة ومن الملائكة دعاء واستغفار ومن الناس التي فيها الركوع والسجود .

Wa-ṣ-ṣalātumina Allāhi ar-raḥmatu wa-mina al-malāʾikati duʿāʾun wa-stighfārun wa-mina an-nāsi allatī fīhā ar-rukūʿu wa-as-sujūd.

"Ṣalāt from God is mercy, from the angels invocation and seeking of forgiveness, and from people the one containing bowing and prostration."

- al-Jawāliqī (d. 539 AH), Sharḥ Adab al-Kātib, p. 15

The dictionaries therefore answer the first premise on their own ground. The entries the charge cites for the prayer sense also print the divine sense, mercy and praise, at the dictionary level, with no tafsīr needed at all.

The Invocation Came First

Source: Ibn Qutayba (d. 276 AH), Gharīb al-Ḥadīth, vol. 1, p. 15

Claim: the invocation is the root sense, and the ritual prayer received its name from the invocation inside it

Ibn Qutayba's Direction of Derivation

The charge derives the meaning of the verse from the institution of the five daily prayers. The direction of derivation runs the other way, and the third century philologists state it plainly.

أصل الصلاة الدعاء . قال الله ﷿ " وصل عليهم إن صلاتك سكن لهم " أي أدع لهم .

وقال تعالى " ومن الأعراب من يؤمن بالله واليوم الآخر ويتخذ ما ينفق قربات عند الله وصلوات الرسول " أي دعاؤه .

فسميت الصلاة بذلك لأنهم كانوا يدعون فيها ويدلك على ذلك الصلاة على الميت إنما هي دعاء له ليس فها ركوع ولا سجود .

"The origin of ṣalāt is invocation. God said, and invoke upon them, your invocation is tranquility for them, meaning call for good for them."

"And He said, and the ṣalawāt of the Messenger, meaning his supplication. Ṣalāt was named from this because they used to invoke within it."

"What shows you this is the prayer over the dead. It is only invocation for him. It contains no bowing and no prostration."

- Ibn Qutayba (d. 276 AH), Gharīb al-Ḥadīth, vol. 1, p. 15

The Qur'an itself supports the naming, since it defines the ritual as an act of remembrance in Qur'an 20:14. The Kufan grammarian al-Farrāʾ, founder of Qur'anic grammar, read the divine verb of this very sura the same way, through Nashwān's dictionary. His gloss moves the trap question one step and leaves it shut. Forgiveness is a good God grants, and a grant has no addressee above God.

وقال الفراء : قوله تعالى : هُوَ الَّذِي يُصَلِّي عَلَيْكُمْ وَمَلائِكَتُهُ أي يستغفر لكم وملائكته . والأصل في الصلاة : الدعاء .

Wa-qāla al-Farrāʾ, qawluhu taʿālā huwa lladhī yuṣallī ʿalaykum wa-malāʾikatuhu, ay yastaghfiru lakum wa-malāʾikatuhu. Wa-al-aṣlu fī aṣ-ṣalāti ad-duʿāʾ.

"Al-Farrāʾ said, His word He it is who yuṣallī upon you, He and His angels, means He seeks forgiveness for you, He and His angels. The origin of ṣalāt is invocation."

- Nashwān b. Saʿīd al-Ḥimyarī (d. 573 AH), Shams al-ʿUlūm, vol. 6, p. 505, from al-Farrāʾ (d. 207 AH)

The first premise of the charge inverts the lexicographers. The ritual is the named derivative. The invocation is the origin. A derivative sense cannot be forced onto a word whose invocation sense already lived in the language.

The Question Was Asked of Moses First

Source: al-Suddī (d. 127 AH), carried by Ibn ʿAdīl, al-Lubāb fī ʿUlūm al-Kitāb, vol. 15, p. 560

Claim: the Children of Israel asked Moses whether his Lord yuṣallī, and God answered that His ṣalāt is His mercy

The Report and Its Weight

The invocation came first, and the question this raises is old. The tradition preserved the polemic's own question, asked of Moses, together with God's reported answer.

قال السدي : قالت بنو إسرائيل لموسى : أيصلي ربنا ؟ فكبر هذا الكلام على موسى فأوحى اللّه إليه قل لهم : إنّي أصلي وإن صلاتي رحمتي وقد وسعت رحمتي كل شيء .

"Al-Suddī said, the Children of Israel said to Moses, does our Lord yuṣallī? The words weighed heavily on Moses, and God revealed to him, say to them, I do ṣallī, and My ṣalāt is My mercy, and My mercy has encompassed all things."

- Ibn ʿAdīl al-Dimashqī (d. 880 AH), al-Lubāb fī ʿUlūm al-Kitāb, vol. 15, p. 560, from al-Suddī (d. 127 AH)

The report travels on al-Suddī's name with its chain as printed, and it is cited here with that status. Its value for the argument is historical. The embarrassment the polemic claims to have discovered was voiced inside revelation's own history, and the answer, mercy, was fixed there. A gloss that answers a question this old cannot be a modern patch.

The question the polemic presents as a discovery was already asked of Moses, and the answer was already fixed. Mercy is the meaning the dictionaries print.

2. The Poets Show the Word in Use

The dictionaries define the word. The poets prove the definition lived in the language before Islam. Each witness below carries the verb in the disputed sense, invocation for a person with God as the addressee, and each names its transmitter.

Al-Aʿshā's Daughter and Her Prayer

Speaker: al-Aʿshā Maymūn b. Qays, pre-Islamic poet

Claim: the daughter's prayer for her father uses the verb for invocation, and the poet answers with the same verb

The Verse Older Than Islam

Al-Aʿshā, a poet of the pre-Islamic age, departing on a journey, hears his daughter pray for him. His answer is the construction of Qur'an 33:56 in miniature, one creature invoking upon another, with God addressed.

تقول بنتي وقد قربت مرتحلا * يا رب جنب أبي الأوصاب والوجعا

عليك مثل الذي صليت فاغتمضي * نوماً فإن لجنب المرء مضطجعا

Taqūlu buntī wa-qad qarrabta murtahilan, yā rabba jannib abī al-awṣāba wa-al-wajaʿā. ʿAlayka mithlu lladhī ṣallayti fa-ghtamidī, nawman fa-inna li-janbi al-marʾi muḍṭajaʿā.

"My daughter says, as I saddle up to leave, O Lord, keep hardship and pain from my father. Upon you the like of what you have invoked. So sleep, for a man's side needs a place to lie."

- al-Aʿshā (pre-Islamic), transmitted in al-Jawāliqī (d. 539 AH), Sharḥ Adab al-Kātib, p. 15, and Ibn Sīda (d. 458 AH), al-Muḥkam, vol. 8, p. 372

The dictionaries gloss the line on the spot. Ibn Sīda writes that the poet orders her to invoke for him the like of her own invocation. The addressee is God, yā rabb. The beneficiary stands after the preposition. The verb is the verse's verb, and nothing in the scene worships the father.

The oldest witness on the page shows the whole grammar of the verse in one family scene. The daughter prays to God, and the father is the one prayed for.

The Elegies and the Blessing Upon the Dead

Speaker: Ḥassān b. Thābit (d. 54 AH), poet of the Prophet ﷺ

Claim: the companion poet predicates ṣalāt of God upon martyrs, as blessing upon the dead

Two Elegies

From a father's blessing upon a journey to God's blessing upon the slain of the community, the construction holds. The elegy for the men killed at al-Rajīʿ opens by asking God's ṣalāt upon them.

صلّى الإله على الذين تتابعوا * يوم الرجيع فأكرموا وأثيبوا

Ṣallā al-ilāhu ʿalā lladhīna tātābaʿū, yawma ar-rajīʿi fa-akramū wa-uthībū.

"May God bless those who followed one another forth on the day of al-Rajīʿ. They were honored, and they were rewarded."

- Ḥassān b. Thābit (d. 54 AH), Dīwān, vol. 1, p. 227

The elegy for al-Mundhir b. ʿAmr opens the same way.

صلّى الإله على ابن عمرو إنّه * صدق اللقاء وصدق ذلك أوفق

Ṣallā al-ilāhu ʿalā bni ʿAmrin innahu, ṣadaqa al-liqāʾa wa-ṣadaqa dhālika awfiqu.

"May God bless Ibn ʿAmr. He was true at the encounter, and true with it, most fitting."

- Ḥassān b. Thābit (d. 54 AH), Dīwān, vol. 1, p. 545

The first generation heard the verb of the verse upon its martyrs and understood blessing. Under the charge's reading, Ḥassān would be commanding God to worship corpses. The Umayyad poet Kathīr ʿAzza runs the same verb over a woman and her neighbors, may the Raḥmān bless ʿAzza and her daughter Laylā. Al-Murtaḍā, the Imami master, asks God's ṣalāt upon the grave of the one he mourns.

صلّى الإله على الذي * قرحت لمصرعه الجفون

Ṣallā al-ilāhu ʿalā lladhī, qaraḥat li-maṣriʿihi al-jufūn.

"May God bless the one for whom the eyelids were torn with grief."

- al-Sharīf al-Murtaḍā (d. 436 AH), Dīwān, vol. 2, p. 512

One construction runs from the pre-Islamic age through the companions into the school's own poetry, and it never once worships its object.

صلّى على عزّة الرّحمن وابنتها ليلى ، وصلّى على جاراتها الاخر .

Ṣallā ʿalā ʿAzza ar-Raḥmānu wa-bnatuhā Laylā, wa-ṣallā ʿalā jārātihā al-ukhrā.

"May the Raḥmān bless ʿAzza and her daughter Laylā, and bless her other neighbors."

- Kathīr ʿAzza (d. 105 AH), quoted in ʿAlī b. Zayd al-Bayhaqī (d. 565 AH), Maʿārij Nahj al-Balāgha, p. 352

The poetic record closes the second premise. The construction as spoken Arabic, across pagans, companions, and Imami scholars, is invocation and blessing upon a person. The poets hand the verb to the Qur'an exactly as the dictionaries defined it.

3. The Qur'an Reads Its Own Verb

The dictionaries defined the word and the poets spoke it. The Qur'an now fixes the divine verb by usage, in the same sura and across the Book, with three verses that leave the prayer reading no room.

The Verse Itself

Verse: Qur'an 33:56

Claim: the verse announces God's blessing upon the Prophet and commands the believers' blessing and greeting

إِنَّ ٱللَّهَ وَمَلَٰٓئِكَتَهُۥ يُصَلُّونَ عَلَى ٱلنَّبِىِّ ۚ يَٰٓأَيُّهَا ٱلَّذِينَ ءَامَنُوا۟ صَلُّوا۟ عَلَيْهِ وَسَلِّمُوا۟ تَسْلِيمًا

Inna llāha wa-malāʾikatahu yuṣallūna ʿalā n-nabiyy, yā ayyuhā lladhīna āmanū ṣallū ʿalayhi wa-sallimū taslīmā.

"God and His angels bless the Prophet. O you who believe, bless him and greet him with peace."

- Qur'an 33:56, Uthmani text

Two acts fill the verse. God and the angels bless, and the believers are told to bless and to greet. The greeting half gives the frame for the whole, since a greeting runs toward a person one honors, and the Qur'an commands the same greeting among believers themselves. The English word bless in that rendering is the very point the charge contests. The dictionaries, the poets, and the usage verses below are the test it has to pass.

The greeting half of the verse sets a frame of honor between creatures. The blessing half is the disputed part, and the Qur'an itself now defines it.

The Purpose Clause of the Same Sura

Verse: Qur'an 33:43

Claim: God's ṣalāt carries a stated purpose, and its object is all believers

Mercy With a Stated Aim

هُوَ ٱلَّذِى يُصَلِّى عَلَيْكُمْ وَمَلَٰٓئِكَتُهُۥ لِيُخْرِجَكُم مِّنَ ٱلظُّلُمَٰتِ إِلَى ٱلنُّورِ ۚ وَكَانَ بِٱلْمُؤْمِنِينَ رَحِيمًۭا

Huwa lladhī yuṣallī ʿalaykum wa-malāʾikatuhu li-yukhrijakum mina aẓ-ẓulumāti ilā an-nūr, wa-kāna bi-l-muʾminīna raḥīmā.

"It is He who blesses you, He and His angels, that He may bring you out of the darknesses into the light, and He is ever merciful to the believers."

- Qur'an 33:43, Uthmani text

Two facts in one verse end the argument. God's ṣalāt has a purpose clause, to bring believers from darkness to light. A ritual prayer has no purpose clause of this shape, and mercy and guidance demand exactly this one. And the object of God's and the angels' ṣalāt here is every believer. If the object of ṣalāt were the addressee of worship, every believer would be divine. The occasion report from Mujāhid shows the first generation heard it so plainly that Abu Bakr asked to receive the same ṣalāt, and the verse came down for him and the rest.

وقوله تعالى : (هو الذي يصلي عليكم وملائكته) قال مجاهد : لما نزلت - إن الله وملائكته يصلون على النبي - الآية قال أبو بكر : ما أعطاك الله تعالى من خير إلا أشركنا فيه ، فنزلت - هو الذي يصلى عليكم وملائكته -

"Mujāhid said, when the verse God and His angels yuṣallūna upon the Prophet came down, Abu Bakr said, whatever good God has given you, let us share in it. So He it is who yuṣallī upon you, He and His angels, came down."

- al-Wāḥidī (d. 468 AH), Asbāb al-Nuzūl, p. 244, from Mujāhid (d. 104 AH)

The purpose clause makes the divine ṣalāt an act of rescue and mercy upon every believer, and the first generation asked to share in it. An honor asked for is an honor received, never worship offered to a creature.

Ṣalawāt From the Lord Upon Ordinary Believers

Verse: Qur'an 2:157

Claim: plural ṣalawāt from the Lord rest upon afflicted believers, joined to mercy as a second gift

Paired With Mercy, Distinct From It

أُو۟لَٰٓئِكَ عَلَيْهِمْ صَلَوَٰتٌۭ مِّن رَّبِّهِمْ وَرَحْمَةٌۭ ۖ وَأُو۟لَٰٓئِكَ هُمُ ٱلْمُهْتَدُونَ

Ulāʾika ʿalayhim ṣalawātun min rabbihim wa-raḥmatun, wa-ulāʾika humu l-muhtadūn.

"Upon those are ṣalawāt from their Lord, and mercy, and those are the rightly guided."

- Qur'an 2:157, Uthmani text

The purpose clause fixed the divine verb as an act of rescue and mercy. The pairing with mercy now fixes it a second way. The sufferers of 2:156, who say we belong to God and to Him we return, receive ṣalawāt from their Lord. The ritual reading makes the sentence die on the page. The mercy reading carries its own proof, since the next noun, mercy, stands beside the ṣalawāt as a second, distinct gift. The dictionaries give the kind, mercy, and al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī fixes the relation in al-Mīzān. The ṣalāt from God is one special mercy of the believers beside His mercy's wider flow.

لكن الذي نسب من الصلاة إلى الله سبحانه في القرآن هو الصلاة بمعنى الرحمة الخاصة بالمؤمنين وهي التي تترتب عليها سعادة العقبى والفلاح المؤبد .

"But the ṣalāt attributed to God in the Qur'an is the ṣalāt in the sense of the special mercy of the believers, the one on which the happiness of the hereafter and lasting success depend."

- al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī (d. 1402 AH), al-Mīzān fī Tafsīr al-Qurʾān, vol. 16, p. 329

The noun names a bestowal of the mercy kind upon ordinary believers, beside God's wider mercy. A rite is nowhere in the sentence. The identity with mercy is a kind, and the verse names one bestowal of that kind beside mercy's wider flow.

The Prophet's Ṣalāt Is Supplication for People

Verse: Qur'an 9:84 with Qur'an 9:103

Claim: the same verb with the same preposition, from the Prophet ﷺ toward people, is invocation for them and is withheld as punishment

The Prayer Over the Dead Hypocrite

وَلَا تُصَلِّ عَلَىٰٓ أَحَدٍۢ مِّنْهُم مَّاتَ أَبَدًۭا وَلَا تَقُمْ عَلَىٰ قَبْرِهِۦٓ ۖ إِنَّهُمْ كَفَرُوا۟ بِٱللَّهِ وَرَسُولِهِۦ وَمَاتُوا۟ وَهُمْ فَٰسِقُونَ

Wa-lā tuṣallī ʿalā aḥadin minhum māta abadan wa-lā taqum ʿalā qabrihi, innahum kafarū bi-llāhi wa-rasūlihi wa-mātū wa-hum fāsiqūn.

"And never pray over any of them who dies, nor stand at his grave. They disbelieved in God and His Messenger and died in defiance."

- Qur'an 9:84, Uthmani text

The ṣalawāt of 2:157 rest on ordinary believers as a distinct gift beside mercy. The creature's verb now shows the same spread of agents. The withheld act is the funeral prayer over a corpse, the same verb, the same second stem, the same preposition and human object. No reader takes this verse to forbid worship of a dead hypocrite. The ṣalāt is invocation for him, withheld as punishment, and its twin in Qur'an 9:103 is the Prophet's ṣalāt upon desert Arabs whose alms he accepted.

خُذْ مِنْ أَمْوَٰلِهِمْ صَدَقَةًۭ تُطَهِّرُهُمْ وَتُزَكِّيهِم بِهَا وَصَلِّ عَلَيْهِمْ ۖ إِنَّ صَلَوٰتَكَ سَكَنٌۭ لَّهُمْ ۗ

Khudh min amwālihim ṣadaqatan tuṭahhiruhum wa-tuzakkīhim bihā wa-ṣalli ʿalayhim, inna ṣalātaka sakunun lahum.

"Take from their wealth a charity, that you may purify them and cause them to grow by it, and invoke blessing upon them. Your invocation is tranquility for them."

- Qur'an 9:103, partial, Uthmani text

An invocation that brought them tranquility. The Qur'an distributes the root across its agents itself. Mercy and bestowal from God, invocation from the Prophet ﷺ and the believers, praise and seeking of forgiveness from the angels.

The Root's Other Branches Inside the Book

The Book itself uses the root's other branches, and this kills the root meaning method on its own ground.

ثُمَّ ٱلْجَحِيمَ صَلُّوهُ

Thumma al-jaḥīma ṣallūh.

"Then the Blaze, burn him in it."

- Qur'an 69:31, Uthmani text

Fire roasting, the middle of the back, the two hip bones, the race horse running second, all live on this root. Al-Ṭurayḥī even records a reading of the word al-muṣallīn, those who follow the leader, in Qur'an 74:43. A method that fetches the root meaning for one verse must fetch it for all. Run on the root's fire branch, that method turns the verse's verb of blessing into a verb of burning, and Qur'an 69:31 shows the branch is real. It yields race horses where the ordinary reading hears worshippers in Qur'an 74:43. The method fails on its own ground.

The Greeting Clause

فَإِذَا دَخَلْتُم بُيُوتًۭا فَسَلِّمُوا۟ عَلَىٰٓ أَنفُسِكُمْ تَحِيَّةًۭ مِّنْ عِندِ ٱللَّهِ مُبَٰرَكَةًۭ طَيِّبَةًۭ

Fa-idhā dakhaltum buyūtan fa-sallimū ʿalā anfusikum taḥiyyatan min ʿindi Allāhi mubārakatan ṭayyibah.

"So when you enter houses, greet yourselves with a greeting from God, blessed and good."

- Qur'an 24:61, partial, Uthmani text

The verse's second command, greet him with peace, uses the same preposition upon believers who greet themselves in Qur'an 24:61. The greeting frame closes the circle. The Qur'an commands with this construction what no one mistakes for worship, honor toward a person, and it forbids in the same breath what worship is, calling on anyone beside God, in Qur'an 72:18.

4. The Imams on the Verse

The Arabic is settled and the Qur'an has read its own verb. The school of the Ahl al-Bayt ﵈ now receives the verse, and its reports define the act agent by agent, from God, from the angels, and from the believers.

Al-Qummī's Reading of the Verse

Source: ʿAlī b. Ibrāhīm al-Qummī (d. 329 AH), Tafsīr, vol. 2, p. 196

Speaker: the explanation carried by al-Qummī's tafsīr

Claim: God's ṣalawāt are purification and praise, the angels' is extolling, the people's is supplication

The Earliest Imami Tafsīr

قال : صلوات الله عليه تزكية له وثناء عليه ، وصلاة الملائكة مدحهم له وصلاة الناس دعاؤهم له والتصديق والاقرار بفضله .

وقوله : (وسلموا تسليما) يعني سلموا له بالولاية وبما جاء به .

"He said, God's ṣalawāt upon him are His purification of him and His praise of him. The angels' ṣalāt is their extolling of him. And the people's ṣalāt is their supplication for him, their affirmation and acknowledgment of his merit."

"And His word, and greet him with peace, means submit to him in the allegiance of love and loyalty, and in everything he brought."

- al-Qummī (d. 329 AH), Tafsīr, vol. 2, p. 196

The preposition in the people's definition is lahu, for him. The supplication runs toward God for the Prophet ﷺ, and even the greeting clause stays inside allegiance to what he brought. The oldest Imami commentary on the verse knows nothing of an addressee Prophet receiving worship.

The school's earliest tafsīr defines the verse with supplication for the Prophet ﷺ and submission to what he brought. The grammar stays Godward from the first line.

The Verse Prayed Over the Prophet's Own Body

Source: al-Kulaynī (d. 329 AH), al-Kāfī, Dār al-Ḥadīth ed., vol. 2, p. 468, no. 35; Nūr al-Thaqalayn, vol. 4, p. 304, no. 229

Speaker: Imam al-Bāqir ﵇

Claim: ʿAlī ﵇ led the blessing over the dead Prophet ﷺ by reciting this verse, and the Prophet ﷺ had said the verse concerned blessing him after his death

The Funeral Scene

عَنْ أَبِي جَعْفَرٍ عَلَيْهِ السَّلَامُ ، قال : قُلْتُ لَهُ : كَيْفَ كَانَتِ الصَّلَاةُ عَلَى النَّبِيِّ صَلَّى اللَّهُ عَلَيْهِ وَآلِهِ ؟

قال : « لَمَّا غَسَّلَهُ (2) أَمِيرُ الْمُؤْمِنِينَ عَلَيْهِ السَّلَامُ وكَفَّنَهُ ، سَجَّاهُ (3) ، ثُمَّ أَدْخَلَ عَلَيْهِ عَشَرَةً ، فَدَارُوا حَوْلَهُ ، ثُمَّ وقَفَ أَمِيرُ الْمُؤْمِنِينَ عَلَيْهِ السَّلَامُ فِي وسَطِهِمْ ، فَقَالَ : « إِنَّ اللَّهَ وَمَلائِكَتَهُ يُصَلُّونَ عَلَى النَّبِيِّ يا أَيُّهَا الَّذِينَ آمَنُوا صَلُّوا عَلَيْهِ وَسَلِّمُوا تَسْلِيماً » فَيَقُولُ الْقَوْمُ كَمَا يَقُولُ حَتَّى صَلَّى عَلَيْهِ أَهْلُ الْمَدِينَةِ وأَهْلُ الْعَوَالِي (5) »

"From Abū Jaʿfar, peace be upon him. I said to him, how was the blessing upon the Prophet performed?"

"He said, when the Commander of the Faithful washed him and shrouded him and laid him out, he brought ten men in, and they circled around him. Then the Commander of the Faithful stood in their midst and said, God and His angels bless the Prophet, O you who believe, bless him and greet him with peace. The group repeated it after him, until the people of Medina and of the outlying districts had prayed over him."

- al-Kulaynī (d. 329 AH), al-Kāfī, Dār al-Ḥadīth ed., vol. 2, p. 468, no. 35

The chain carries its caveat. Sahl b. Ziyād is a disputed transmitter, and the report is cited with that status. Its content is the school's earliest answer to the how question. The verse was recited over the Prophet's ﷺ shrouded body, group after group. The act performed over a grave is the funeral blessing, and every canon of Arabic, every confession's funeral rite included, calls that an invocation for the dead. The tripartite reading al-Qummī carried now stands confirmed by practice.

Revealed for After His Death

قال : وقال أمير المؤمنين ﵇ : سمعت رسول الله ﵌ يقول في صحته وسلامته : انما أنزلت هذه الآية على بعد قبض الله لي : (ان الله وملائكته يصلون على النبي يا أيها الذين آمنوا صلوا عليه وسلموا تسليما) .

"He said, and the Commander of the Faithful said, I heard the Messenger of God say, in his health and wellbeing, this verse was sent down to me concerning the blessing upon me after God has taken me. God and His angels bless the Prophet, O you who believe, bless him and greet him with peace."

- al-Ḥuwayzī (d. 1112 AH), Nūr al-Thaqalayn, vol. 4, p. 304, no. 229, from Jābir, from Imam al-Bāqir

The Prophet ﷺ himself fixed the verse's reference as the blessing after his death. A verse revealed for praying over a grave, by the Prophet's own reported word, cannot mean worship of the one in the grave.

The Imams Define It Agent by Agent

Source: al-Ṣadūq (d. 381 AH), Thawāb al-Aʿmāl, and Maʿānī al-Akhbār, p. 209

Speaker: Imam Abū al-Ḥasan al-Kāẓim ﵇, and Imam al-Ṣādiq ﵇

Claim: God's ṣalāt is mercy, the angels' purification, the believers' supplication

The Question Put to the Imam

في كتاب ثواب الأعمال عن أبي المغرا قال : سمعت أبا الحسن ﵇ يقول : من قال في دبر صلاة الصبح وصلاة المغرب قبل أن يثنى رجليه أو يكلم أحدا : (ان الله وملائكته يصلون على النبي يا أيها الذين آمنوا صلوا عليه وسلموا تسليما اللهم صل على محمد وذريته) قضى الله له مأة حاجة سبعين في الدنيا وثلاثين في الآخرة .

قال : قلت : ما معنى صلاة الله وصلاة ملائكته وصلاة المؤمن ؟ قال : صلاة الله رحمة من الله ، وصلاة الملائكة تزكية منهم له ، وصلاة المؤمنين دعاء منهم له ، والحديث طويل أخذنا منه موضع الحاجة .

"In the Book of Rewards of Works, from Abū al-Mughīra, who said, I heard Abū al-Ḥasan say, whoever says after the dawn and sunset prayers, God and His angels bless the Prophet, O you who believe, bless him and greet him with peace, then O Allah, bless Muḥammad and his offspring, God settles a hundred needs for him, seventy in this world and thirty in the next."

"I said, what is the meaning of God's ṣalāt, the angels' ṣalāt, and the believer's? He said, God's ṣalāt is mercy from God. The angels' ṣalāt is purification from them for him. And the believers' ṣalāt is supplication from them for him. The narration is long, and we have taken the needed part."

- al-Ḥuwayzī (d. 1112 AH), Nūr al-Thaqalayn, vol. 4, p. 302, no. 221, from al-Ṣadūq, Thawāb al-Aʿmāl, from Imam al-Kāẓim

The definition is multiply attested across the Ṣadūq corpus. One chain note is kept, because it names the speaker. Abū al-Mughīra's report reaches the Imam under the name Abū al-Ḥasan, a name that fits two of the Imams, so the narrator's line carries the identification. Al-Fayḍ al-Kāshānī in al-Ṣāfī identifies him as Imam al-Kāẓim ﵇, while al-Mughniya transmits a like report from Imam al-Riḍā ﵇. The final clause says the narration is long and only the needed part was copied, and it is kept as printed. Al-Maʿānī al-Akhbār carries the same tripartite definition from Imam al-Ṣādiq ﵇, mercy, purification, supplication.

The Covenant Meaning of the Believer's Blessing

حدثني موسى بن جعفر ﵉ قال : «قال الصادق جعفر بن محمد ﵉ : من صلى على النبي وآله فمعناه : أني أنا على الميثاق والوفاء الذي قبلت حين قوله : أَلَسْتُ بِرَبِّكُمْ قالُوا بَلى»

"Mūsā b. Jaʿfar said to me, al-Ṣādiq Jaʿfar b. Muḥammad said, whoever blesses the Prophet and his House, its meaning is, I am upon the covenant and the fidelity I accepted when He said, am I not your Lord? They said, yes."

- al-Baḥrānī (d. 1107 AH), al-Burhān fī Tafsīr al-Qurʾān, vol. 4, p. 488, no. 8695, from al-Ṣadūq, Maʿānī al-Akhbār, p. 209

The school reads the believer's blessing as a renewal of the first covenant. The grammar stays Godward from every side. The wilāyah reading of the greeting clause in al-Qummī's tafsīr keeps even the submission aimed at what the Prophet brought, allegiance to God through His messenger.

God Commands Moses to Bless the Coming Prophet

Source: al-Kāfī's monologue of Moses, via al-Māzandarānī, Sharḥ Uṣūl al-Kāfī, vol. 11, p. 335

Speaker: God's reported address to Moses ﵇

Claim: God commands Moses to bless the coming Prophet, for God and the angels bless him

One Sentence, Three Agents

The covenant reading keeps the believer's blessing Godward, and God's own address to Moses now joins every speaker of the act in one sentence.

يا موسى إنه أُميٌّ وهو عبدٌ صدقٌ

"O Moses, he is unlettered, and he is a true servant."

- al-Māzandarānī (d. 1081 AH), Sharḥ Uṣūl al-Kāfī, vol. 11, p. 335, from al-Kāfī, with a chain attributed upward to the Prophet without every link named

فصلِّ عليه يا بن عمران فإنّي أُصلي عليه وملائكتي .

"So bless him, O son of ʿImrān, for I bless him, and My angels."

- al-Māzandarānī (d. 1081 AH), Sharḥ Uṣūl al-Kāfī, vol. 11, p. 335, from al-Kāfī, with a raised chain

One reported sentence names the coming Prophet a servant, commands Moses to bless him, and joins God and the angels in blessing him. Creature invocation and divine bestowal share one verb without confusion, and the school never treated the sharing as an embarrassment. The sharing is the point of the verse's rhetoric. Every order of being directs its own mode of the act at the Prophet ﷺ.

One reported sentence carries servant, command, and joint blessing without confusion. The verse's shared verb teaches the same lesson, God bestows and creatures ask.

At the Grave Itself

Source: al-Kāfī, via Nūr al-Thaqalayn, vol. 4, p. 304, no. 230

Speaker: Imam al-Ṣādiq ﵇, at the Prophet's grave

Claim: the Imam's blessing at the grave is a request addressed to Allah

The Practice at the Most Charged Place

رأيت أبا عبد الله ﵇ انتهى إلى قبر النبي ﵌ فوضع يده عليه وقال : اسأل الله الذي اجتباك واختارك وهداك وهدى بك أن يصلى عليك .

"I saw Abū ʿAbd Allāh come to the grave of the Prophet, place his hand upon it, and say, I ask God, who chose you and selected you and guided you and guided through you, to bless you."

- al-Ḥuwayzī (d. 1112 AH), Nūr al-Thaqalayn, vol. 4, p. 304, no. 230, from al-Kāfī

Under the charge's logic the grave is the most dangerous place a Muslim could stand. The school's primary text answers from the spot itself. The Imam's ṣalāt upon the Prophet ﷺ at the grave is a request addressed to Allah, asking Allah to bless him. The one sentence that joined Moses with God and the angels has become a standing practice, the two roles never trading places.

ʿUyūn Akhbār al-Riḍā seals the same grammar in a session with the caliph al-Maʾmūn. The taught formula is recited there, and the caliph declares the community's agreement upon it.

فقال تقولون اللهم صلي على محمد وآل محمد كما صليت وباركت على إبراهيم وآل إبراهيم إنك حميد مجيد فهل بينكم معاشر الناس في هذا خلاف قالوا لا قال المأمون هذا مما لا خلاف فيه أصلا وعليه إجماع الأمة .

"He said, you say, O Allah, bless Muḥammad and the House of Muḥammad, as You blessed and showered blessing upon Abraham and the House of Abraham, You are Praiseworthy, Glorious. Is there any disagreement among you people over this? They said, no. Al-Maʾmūn said, this is something there is no disagreement over at all, and upon it is the consensus of the community."

- al-Fayḍ al-Kāshānī (d. 1091 AH), al-Ṣāfī, vol. 4, p. 201, from al-Ṣadūq, ʿUyūn Akhbār al-Riḍā

The school's practice answers the liturgy objection before it is raised. Every formula opens with the vocative O Allah, and the one blessed is named after the preposition. The addressee and the beneficiary never trade places.

5. Two Verbs, One Formula

The charge's strongest hold is the word choice. Why did the verse say ṣalli and leave bārik aside? The Prophet's ﷺ own answer already circulates on every Muslim tongue, because the verse produced a formula that uses both verbs in one breath.

The Formula the Verse Produced

Source: Tafsīr Ibn Abī Ḥātim, no. 17769; Majmaʿ al-Bayān, vol. 8, p. 179

Speaker: the Prophet ﷺ, asked by the companions through Kaʿb b. ʿAjra

Claim: the taught blessing addresses Allah and commands both verbs, ṣalli and bārik

Both Verbs, Both Houses

قلنا : يا رسول الله ، قد علمنا السلام عليك فكيف الصلاة عليك ؟

قال : «قولوا : اللهم صلى على محمد وعلى آل محمد ، كما صليت على إبراهيم وعلى آل إبراهيم إنك حميد مجيد ، وبارك على محمد وعلى آل محمد ، كما باركت على إبراهيم وآل إبراهيم ، إنك حميد مجيد»

"We said, O Messenger of God, we know the greeting upon you, so how do we bless you?"

"He said, say, O Allah, bless Muḥammad and the House of Muḥammad, as You blessed Abraham and the House of Abraham, You are Praiseworthy, Glorious. And bestow blessing upon Muḥammad and the House of Muḥammad, as You bestowed blessing upon Abraham and the House of Abraham, You are Praiseworthy, Glorious."

- Ibn Abī Ḥātim (d. 327 AH), Tafsīr, vol. 10, p. 13, no. 17769, from Kaʿb b. ʿAjra

The formula answers the word choice question inside itself. The Prophet ﷺ uses both verbs and keeps them distinct, because they name two acts. The dictionaries fix what bāraka itself means, and al-Jawharī's entry carries it in one line.

والبركة : النماء والزيادة . والتبريك : الدعاء بالبركة . ويقال : بارك الله لك وفيك وعليك ، وباركك .

Wa-al-barakatu an-namāʾu wa-az-ziyādah. Wa-at-tabrīku ad-duʿāʾu bi-al-barakah. Wa-yuqālu bāraka Allāhu laka wa-fīka wa-ʿalayka, wa-bārakak.

"Al-baraka is growth and increase. Tabrīk is the invocation of blessing. One says, may God bless to you, in you, and upon you, and may He bless you."

- al-Jawharī (d. 393 AH), al-Ṣiḥāḥ, vol. 4, p. 263

So bāraka names increase and abundance poured into a thing, and the Qur'an uses it of offspring, provision, places, and times, including the blessing upon Abraham's household that the charge's own premise invokes.

وَبَٰرَكْنَا عَلَيْهِ وَعَلَىٰٓ إِسْحَٰقَ ۚ وَمِن ذُرِّيَّتِهِمَا مُحْسِنٌۭ وَظَالِمٌۭ لِّنَفْسِهِۦ مُبِينٌۭ

Wa-bāraknā ʿalayhi wa-ʿalā Isḥāq.

"And We bestowed blessing upon him and upon Isaac."

- Qur'an 37:113, partial, Uthmani text

The blessing verb takes the same preposition ʿalā upon the person blessed, so the word choice the charge questions carries the same grammar as the verse's own verb. Ṣalāt ʿalā names the personal act of mercy and honor directed upon a person, the act Qur'an 33:43 defines with its purpose clause and Qur'an 2:157 pairs with mercy. The school transmits the formula on its own chains, and Majmaʿ al-Bayān carries it from Abū Ḥamza al-Thumālī with the Household named in every clause. Ibn Masʿūd's version names the three gifts one by one.

اللهم اجعل صلاتك ورحمتك وبركاتك على سيد المرسلين ، وإمام المتقين ، وخاتم النبيين ، محمد عبدك ورسولك ، إمام الدين ، وقائد الخير ، ورسول الرحمة .

"O Allah, place Your ṣalāt, and Your mercy, and Your blessings upon the master of the messengers, the leader of the God conscious, the seal of the prophets, Muḥammad, Your servant and Your messenger, the leader of the religion, the guide of good, and the messenger of mercy."

- al-Ṭabrisī (d. 548 AH), Majmaʿ al-Bayān, vol. 8, p. 179, from Ibn Masʿūd

The formula names ṣalāt, mercy, and blessing as three distinct gifts. A language that keeps three words apart does not make one of them secretly mean a fourth, worship.

The Prophet ﷺ answers the how question with a formula that uses both verbs in one breath and addresses Allah alone. The two premises of the word choice charge meet one formula, and both fail inside it.

The First Gloss of the Verse Equated Them Anyway

Source: al-Ṭabarī (d. 310 AH), Jāmiʿ al-Bayān, vol. 22, p. 53

Speaker: Ibn ʿAbbās, through Ṭabarī's chain

Claim: yuṣallūna at this verse means they bless

The Earliest Exegetical Layer

يقول تعالى ذكره : إن الله وملائكته يبركون على النبي محمد ( ص ) ، كما : 21847 حدثني علي ، قال : ثنا أبو صالح ، قال : ثني معاوية ، عن علي ، عن ابن عباس ، قوله : إن الله وملائكته يصلون على النبي يا أيها الذين آمنوا عليه يقول : يباركون على النبي .

"God's remark means, God and His angels keep blessing the Prophet Muḥammad. As ʿAlī told me, from Abū Ṣāliḥ, from Muʿāwiya, from ʿAlī, from Ibn ʿAbbās, concerning God and His angels yuṣallūna upon the Prophet, O you who believe, upon him, He means, they bless the Prophet."

- al-Ṭabarī (d. 310 AH), Jāmiʿ al-Bayān, vol. 22, p. 53

The chain is a tafsīr chain that ḥadīth critics do not grade sound, and the gloss is cited for what it shows, the earliest exegetical layer read the verb as blessing. The question why the verse avoided the blessing verb was asked and answered fourteen centuries ago, and the answer ran the other way. The first exegetical gloss equates the two at this very verse, yuṣallūna, they bless. Ṭabarī's own running translation of the believers' command reads, invoke for God's Prophet.

The earliest exegetical layer reads the verse's verb as bless. The premise that ṣalli cannot mean bless falls with it, and the word choice question dissolves into a distinction the formula itself draws.

God Blesses Every Believer Ten Times

Source: Sunan al-Tirmidhī, no. 485; Musnad Aḥmad, vol. 11, p. 128

Speaker: the Prophet ﷺ, from Abū Hurayra and ʿAbd Allāh b. ʿAmr

Claim: whoever blesses the Prophet once, God blesses him ten times

The Report That Turns the Charge Around

« مَنْ صَلَّى عَلَيَّ صَلَاةً صَلَّى اللَّه عَلَيْهِ عَشْرًا »

"Whoever blesses me once, God blesses him by it ten times."

- al-Tirmidhī (d. 279 AH), Sunan, vol. 2, p. 355, no. 485, from Abū Hurayra; parallel in Musnad Aḥmad, vol. 11, p. 128

The Ibn ʿAbbās gloss made the two verbs one at this verse. The ten-fold report now turns the same equivalence around. Under the charge's reading, God prays to every ordinary Muslim, ten times over. The report is graded sound in the Sunni canon and carries a Shia parallel in the Mustadrak of al-Nūrī, and the charge's own method leans on that canon for its exhibits. The mercy reading makes it plain. The addressee of the believer's blessing is Allah, and the one who receives God's ṣalāt is any Muslim who says the formula. That closes the last route to reading the object of ṣalāt as an addressee of worship.

6. One Verb, Many Speakers

The dictionaries and the reports fix the senses agent by agent, mercy from God, invocation from creatures. A trained objector has one move left, and it is a real one. Arabic, he says, does not let one verb carry different meanings for different subjects when each subject holds the verb in its plain sense. The verse joins God and the angels under one verb, so the verb has one meaning, the worship prayer. This objection is old, it was raised by the Arab grammarians themselves, and the tradition answered it in writing.

Ibn Hishām's Objection and His Own Answer

Source: Ibn Hishām al-Anṣārī (d. 761 AH), Mughnī al-Labīb, vol. 2, p. 271

Claim: the word has one lexical meaning, beneficent inclining, applied by each agent in its own way

The Strongest Form, in the Grammarian's Own Words

قلت : الصواب عندي أن الصلاة لغة بمعنى واحد ، وهو العطف ، ثم العطف بالنسبة إلى اللَّه ﷾ الرحمة وإلى الملائكة الاستغفار وإلى الآدميين دعاء بعضهم لبعض .

الثانية : أنا لا نعرف في العربية فعلا واحدا يختلف معناه باختلاف المسند إليه إذا كان الإسناد حقيقيا .

"I say, the sound view in my judgment is that ṣalāt has lexically one meaning, beneficent inclining. The inclining, in relation to God, is mercy. In relation to the angels, it is seeking of forgiveness. In relation to human beings, it is the invocation of each for each."

"The second is that we know of no Arabic verb that changes its meaning according to its subject under literal predication."

- Ibn Hishām al-Anṣārī (d. 761 AH), Mughnī al-Labīb, vol. 2, p. 271

Ibn Hishām calls the majority view far fetched for several reasons, and the second of them is quoted above. The polemic borrowed the objection wholesale. Muslim grammarians raised it seven centuries ago and answered it in writing. His own resolution dissolves it, and it dissolves the tension with the dictionaries as well. One meaning, beneficent inclining, exists, and each agent performs it according to its own capacity. The agent by agent senses of the dictionary entries are this one core in the hand of each speaker, so the two positions are one position at two levels. Al-Ḥaytamī answers the same way in the salawat monographs and adds why the verse joins God with His angels under one verb.

وبما قدّمناه من وضعها للقدر المشترك يردّ الأخيران ؛ إذ لا اشتراك حينيذ ، ولا اختلاف باختلاف المسند إليه .

"By what we have advanced, that the word is coined for the shared core, the last two objections fall. There is then no homonymy and no variance with the subject."

- Ibn Ḥajar al-Ḥaytamī (d. 974 AH), al-Durr al-Manḍūd fī al-Ṣalāt ʿalā Ṣāḥib al-Maqām al-Maḥmūd, p. 46

وحكمة التشريك هنا : أن هذا قول من اللّه عزّ وجل شرّف به ملائكته ، فلا يتوهم منه نقص البتّة .

"The wisdom of the shared predication here is that this is a saying of God, with which He honored His angels, so no deficiency can be suspected in it."

- Ibn Ḥajar al-Ḥaytamī (d. 974 AH), al-Durr al-Manḍūd fī al-Ṣalāt ʿalā Ṣāḥib al-Maqām al-Maḥmūd, p. 46

The master exegete of the school distributes the shared verb in exactly this way, and the shared verb magnifies the Prophet's rank without confusing the speakers.

ختم ذلك بالتعظيم الذي ليس يقاربه تعظيم ولا يدانيه فقال : (إن الله وملائكته يصلون على النبي) ، معناه : إن الله يصلي على النبي ﵌ ويثني عليه بالثناء الجميل ويبجله بأعظم التبجيل ، وملائكته يصلون عليه (يثنون عليه) بأحسن الثناء ويدعون له بأزكى الدعاء .

"He sealed it with a magnification no magnification rivals, saying, God and His angels bless the Prophet. Its meaning is that God blesses the Prophet and praises him with beautiful praise and honors him with the greatest honoring, while His angels bless him, praising him with the finest praise and supplicating for him with the purest supplication."

- al-Ṭabrisī (d. 548 AH), Majmaʿ al-Bayān, vol. 8, p. 179

The objection and its answers live inside the Arabic tradition itself. The shared core and the distribution both keep one verb for many speakers without one mode of act. God's half of the verb is praise and honor. Petition belongs to the angels' half and the believers' half, and it aims at God alone.

The Cause Named for Its Effect

Source: Ibn al-Qayyim (d. 751 AH), al-Fawāʾid; al-Shahīd al-Thānī (d. 965 AH), via al-Māzandarānī

Claim: the root sense is invocation literally for creatures, and for God the verb names the granting of what invocation asks

The Sunni and Shia Solutions Meet

The shared core keeps one meaning for the verb, and the cause and effect solution now explains how the two halves of the verse carry it.

الصلاة حقيقة في الدعاء مجاز في إجابة الدعاء ، لأن الإجابة مسببة عن الدعاء فصلاة الملائكة حقيقة لأنها دعاء ، وصلاة اللَّه من مجاز التعبير بلفظ السبب الذي هو الدعاء عن المسبب الذي هو الإجابة .

"Ṣalāt is literal for invocation and figurative for the answering of invocation, since the answer results from the invocation. The angels' ṣalāt is literal, for it is invocation. God's ṣalāt is the figure of expressing the cause, the invocation, for its effect, the granting."

- Ibn al-Qayyim (d. 751 AH), al-Fawāʾid al-Mūshawiqa, p. 126

المشهور أن الصلاة من الله الرحمة ومن الملائكة الاستغفار ومن المؤمنين الدعاء وهو طلب الرحمة ، وقال الشهيد الثاني : أصل الصلاة الدعاء إلاّ أنها من الله تعالى الرحمة مجازاً ورجحه على المشهور بأن المجاز خير من الاشتراك كما بيّن في الأصول .

"The famous view, ṣalāt from God is mercy, from the angels seeking of forgiveness, from the believers invocation, which is the request for mercy. Al-Shahīd al-Thānī said, the origin of ṣalāt is invocation, and from God it is mercy by extension. He preferred this over the famous view on the ground that extension is better than homonymy, as established in legal theory."

- al-Māzandarānī (d. 1081 AH), Sharḥ Uṣūl al-Kāfī, vol. 11, p. 345, from al-Shahīd al-Thānī (d. 965 AH)

The Sunni polymath and the Shia martyr arrive at this solution from two traditions. The legal theorists of the school, its uṣūlīs, even used this verse as their test case for whether one word can carry two senses at once. Al-Māzandarānī records the uṣūlīs' two answers. One keeps a single sense for the verb, one care in showing honor that each speaker performs in its own way. The other gives each subject its own half of the sentence, so the reader hears, God blesses, and His angels bless.

Two traditions arrive at one solution. The verb is invocation in a creature's mouth, and in God's mouth it names the answering bestowal, so one verb serves many speakers with no creaturely posture in God.

Arabic Already Runs Verbs Both Ways

Source: Ibn Abī al-Ḥadīd (d. 656 AH), Sharḥ Nahj al-Balāgha, vol. 6, p. 144

Claim: the grammarian's own parallel, God keep you alive against I invoked life for you

A Parallel From Everyday Arabic

قلت : الصلاة من الله تعالى هي الاكرام والتبجيل ورفع المنزلة ، والصلاة منا على النبي ﵌ هي الدعاء له بذلك

"I say, ṣalāt from God is honor, veneration, and the raising of rank. And our ṣalāt upon the Prophet is the invocation for that."

- Ibn Abī al-Ḥadīd (d. 656 AH), Sharḥ Nahj al-Balāgha, vol. 6, p. 144

ونظيره قوله (حياك الله) أي أحياك الله وأبقاك ، وحييتك أي دعوت لك بأن يحييك

"Its like is the saying, God keep you alive, meaning God actually kept you alive and preserved you, against I invoked life for you, meaning I asked God to keep you alive."

- Ibn Abī al-Ḥadīd (d. 656 AH), Sharḥ Nahj al-Balāgha, vol. 6, p. 144

The parallel closes the grammar objection. Arabic speakers bless each other with God keep you alive every day, and no one hears the speaker claiming to give life. One verb runs across the divine reality and the creature's prayer for that reality, and the language carries both easily. The verse's shared verb is the same structure. God's mercy stands on one side, the angels' and the believers' invocation on the other, one act of inclining toward the Prophet ﷺ performed by every order of being. The four accounts above are one reading at four depths, and the Imams' own word, mercy, is the anchor the school holds. Al-Ṭabāṭabāʾī gathers the whole line in al-Mīzān. The unifying meaning of ṣalāt is inclining. God's ṣalāt in the Qur'an is the special mercy that carries believers from darkness to light. It is His remembering them with mercy, as the Qur'an says in 2:152, remember Me and I remember you.

7. The Christian Canon on Blessing

The grammar objection is closed, and the method that pressed it remains. The Christian canon now faces that same method.

Source: the Torah, the Psalms, and the Epistle to the Ephesians, in the editions cited below

Claim: Scripture predicates the blessing verb of God toward creatures, with the same verb family creatures use toward God

Method: the charge's own method, run against the charge's own canon

The LORD Blesses Israel

The Priestly Blessing God Commanded

The Torah commands the priests of Israel to place a blessing on the people, and the words of that blessing name the LORD as the one who blesses.

יְבָרֶכְךָ֥ יְהוָ֖ה וְיִשְׁמְרֶֽךָ׃

יָאֵ֨ר יְהוָ֧ה ׀ פָּנָיו֙ אֵלֶ֖יךָ וִֽיחֻנֶּֽךָּ׃

יִשָּׂ֨א יְהוָ֤ה ׀ פָּנָיו֙ אֵלֶ֔יךָ וְיָשֵׂ֥ם לְךָ֖ שָׁלֹֽום׃

يُبَارِكُكَ الرَّبُّ وَيَحْرُسُكَ.

يُضِيءُ الرَّبُّ بِوَجْهِهِ عَلَيْكَ وَيَرْحَمُكَ.

يَرْفَعُ الرَّبُّ وَجْهَهُ عَلَيْكَ وَيَمْنَحُكَ سَلاَمًا.

Yevārekhkā YHWH viyishmerkhā. Yāʾer YHWH pānāw elaykā viḥunnekkā. Yissā YHWH pānāw elaykā viyāsēm lekhā shālōm.

"The LORD bless thee, and keep thee. The LORD make his face shine upon thee, and be gracious unto thee. The LORD lift up his countenance upon thee, and give thee peace."

- Numbers 6:24-26, Westminster Leningrad Codex, Smith and Van Dyck Arabic (1865), King James Version

The command closes with God's own word on what the priests' blessing does. They place His name on Israel, and He blesses them.

וְשָׂמ֥וּ אֶת־שְׁמִ֖י עַל־בְּנֵ֣י יִשְׂרָאֵ֑ל וַאֲנִ֖י אֲבָרֲכֵֽם׃ פ

Vesāmū et-shemī al-benē Yisraʾel, va-anī ʾavarekhēm.

فَيَجْعَلُونَ اسْمِي عَلَى بَنِي إِسْرَائِيلَ، وَأَنَا أُبَارِكُهُمْ

"And they shall put my name upon the children of Israel; and I will bless them."

- Numbers 6:27, Westminster Leningrad Codex, Smith and Van Dyck Arabic (1865), King James Version

The Hebrew verb is barakh, bless, the same verb the page's fifth section met in the Qur'an's bāraka. In the Psalm of David the same verb runs upward, bless the LORD, O my soul, and the Genesis creation account runs it from God to the first couple, and God blessed them.

בָּרֲכִ֣י נַ֭פְשִׁי אֶת־יְהוָ֑ה וְכָל־קְ֝רָבַ֗י אֶת־שֵׁ֥ם קָדְשֹֽׁו׃

بَارِكِي يَا نَفْسِي الرَّبَّ، وَكُلُّ مَا فِي بَاطِنِي لِيُبَارِكِ اسْمَهُ الْقُدُّوسَ.

Barakhī nafshī et-YHWH, ve-khol qerāvay et-shem qodshō.

"Bless the LORD, O my soul, and all that is within me, bless his holy name."

- Psalm 103:1, Westminster Leningrad Codex, Smith and Van Dyck Arabic (1865), King James Version

The Epistle to the Ephesians runs the verb both ways in one sentence. The letter opens by blessing God, who has blessed the believers.

ευλογητος ο θεος και πατηρ του κυριου ημων ιησου χριστου ο ευλογησας ημας εν παση ευλογια πνευματικη εν τοις επουρανιοις εν χριστω

Eulogētos ho theos kai patēr tou kyriou hēmōn Iēsou Christou, ho eulogēsas hēmas en pasē eulogia pneumastikē en tois epouraniois.

مُبَارَكٌ اللهُ أَبُو رَبِّنَا يَسُوعَ الْمَسِيحِ، الَّذِي بَارَكَنَا بِكُلِّ بَرَكَةٍ رُوحِيَّةٍ فِي السَّمَاوِيَّاتِ فِي الْمَسِيحِ،

"Blessed be the God and Father of our Lord Jesus Christ, who hath blessed us with all spiritual blessings in heavenly places."

- Ephesians 1:3, Elzevir Textus Receptus (1624), Smith and Van Dyck Arabic (1865), King James Version

The creation account then runs the verb from God to the first human couple.

וַיְבָ֣רֶךְ אֹתָם֮ אֱלֹהִים֒ וַיֹּ֨אמֶר לָהֶ֜ם אֱלֹהִ֗ים פְּר֥וּ וּרְב֛וּ וּמִלְא֥וּ אֶת־הָאָ֖רֶץ

وَبَارَكَهُمُ اللهُ وَقَالَ لَهُمْ: «أَثْمِرُوا وَاكْثُرُوا وَامْلأُوا الأَرْضَ

Va-yevārekh otām Elohim, va-yomer lahem Elohim, perū urevū umilʾū et-hāʾāreṣ.

"And God blessed them, and God said unto them, Be fruitful, and multiply, and replenish the earth."

- Genesis 1:28, partial, Westminster Leningrad Codex, Smith and Van Dyck Arabic (1865), King James Version

The canon's blessing verb runs from God to Israel in the Torah and from Israel to God in the Psalms, and the Epistle runs it both ways in one sentence. The method that reads one direction into the Qur'an reads one direction into the canon too.

The Four Tests, Run Both Ways

What Each Side Asks a Believer to Accept

The page has shown what the Qur'an says. God's ṣalāt is mercy, praise, and the attentive raising of the Prophet's ﷺ rank, and the believer's ṣalāt is supplication addressed to Allah. The Christian canon says the LORD blesses Israel with the verb Israel's own psalms use to bless the LORD. On the ethical test, the Qur'an asks a believer to accept that God honors His messenger with mercy and asks the believers to join in asking for that mercy. The canon asks a believer to accept that God places His name on Israel through priests who bless with His verb. Each view gives God a bestowal that creatures also perform. Each school explains the sharing with the same move. In both, the divine act is the reality, and the creature's act of blessing, praise or request, looks to God.

Coherence With Each Scripture

On the rational test, the Qur'an's usage is consistent across its pages. Qur'an 33:43 fixes the divine verb with a purpose clause, and Qur'an 2:157 pairs the ṣalawāt with mercy. Qur'an 9:84 fixes the creature's verb as invocation for the dead, and the formula of blessing addresses Allah alone. The canon's usage is coherent the same way once the reader allows context to fix the sense of bless. The strongest reply is a claimed difference in kind, Hebrew barakh never names a ritual act, while Arabic ṣalāt is the name of the daily prayer. The answer sits in the derivation the dictionaries fixed. Arabic named the ritual from the invocation at its core, so the shared name is a line of descent. Descent explains why one language's blessing word never became a rite's name while the other's did. The present day answers with facts the page has already shown. The funeral construction still blesses the dead, and the formula still opens with O Allah. The purpose clause of Qur'an 33:43 still reads mercy into the verb. The charge refuses that allowance to the Qur'an while depending on it for Numbers and the Psalms.

Fidelity to the Text and Economy

On textual fidelity, the mercy reading tracks the dictionaries, the poets, the purpose clause, and the liturgy. The prayer reading tracks the first line of a dictionary entry with the rest of the entry removed. On economy, the mercy reading asks the verse to mean what its construction meant in the language it was revealed in. Everywhere else in Arabic, this construction means blessing upon its object. The charge needs it to mean worship of its object in this one verse alone. The charge also needs the formula the verse produced, O Allah, bless, to address the wrong party. The method that produces the charge against Qur'an 33:56 produces the same charge against Numbers 6 and Psalm 103. A reader who keeps the method owes an answer for the Aaronic blessing. A reader who keeps context owes the Qur'an the same hearing.

8. The Exits, Closed

Three doors remain open for a determined objector. Each one closes on the sources already on the table.

Only the Root Sense Was Meant

To Whom Does Allah Supplicate

The retreat runs as follows. The argument only meant that duʿāʾ needs an addressee above the speaker, so to whom does Allah supplicate? The retreat builds human prayer's grammar into God. The school's legal theorists answered it in writing. For God the verb names the effect, the granting of what invocation requests, as Ibn al-Qayyim said and al-Shahīd al-Thānī said with him. Or it names the shared core, bestowal of good, in the words of the school's theologians. The same question aimed at the Qur'an's word for God loving the believers, in Qur'an 5:54, would demand passion and passivity in God. The schools of theology, Shīʿī, Ashʿarī, and Muʿtazilī, answer act verbs said of God with one shared rule, and the school's own lexicon commentary states the rule for this very word.

فمن حملها بالنسبة اليه تعالى على ( الرحمة ) إنما أراد معناها الذي يليق به عز شأنه ، وهو : افاضة الخير والاحسان .

"Whoever applies it to Him in the sense of mercy intends only the sense that befits Him, exalted is His majesty, and that is the outpouring of good and kindness."

- al-Mudarris al-Afghānī (d. 1406 AH), al-Madras al-Afḍal, vol. 1, p. 150

The rule covers Ibn Hishām's inclining as well. Said of God, the inclining is an outpouring of good, and a bodily leaning belongs to creatures. The shared core obeys the same law as the mercy gloss.

Act verbs said of God name His effects, and the effect here is the outpouring of good. The question to whom does Allah supplicate asks a human posture of a divine bestowal, and the rule of effects is the answer.

The Poetry Is Too Late

Four Independent Lines

The rule for God's verbs is settled, and the retreat tries the dating instead. The retreat discounts the poets as poetry of the Islamic age. Al-Aʿshā's daughter verse is Jāhiliyya, and its scene, a daughter praying for a departing father, owes nothing to Islam. The case never rests on the poets. A deeper retreat borrows a word history instead, ṣalāt, it says, came into Arabic from the Aramaic ṣlōtā, which already named the ritual prayer. A loanword's birth fixes nothing about its later life in the borrowing language. The Arabic record above is the evidence for the Arabic sense, and the construction, the funeral rite, and the liturgy all speak for it. The dictionaries carry the agent by agent senses in their own entries. The Qur'an fixes the divine verb in Qur'an 33:43 and Qur'an 2:157, and the creature's verb in Qur'an 9:84 and Qur'an 9:103. The funeral construction of every Arabic confession carries the preposition's sense of good done upon a person. Four lines stand, and the objection has to break all four with one method.

Four lines stand on four kinds of ground, dictionaries, scripture, rite, and liturgy. One method must break all four, and the only method on offer breaks the objector's own canon first.

The Verse Exalts Muhammad Too Much

Honor Without Worship

The final retreat grants the mercy reading and then argues that a verse this exalting makes the Prophet ﷺ a quasi divine figure.

The exaltation is real and intended, and the school's own lexicon of the Qur'an ranks the honor above the prostration of the angels before Adam.

ومن هنا قال بعضهم : تشريف لله محمدا ( ص ) بقوله : إن الله وملائكته يصلون على النبي أبلغ من تشريف آدم بالسجود .

"From here some said, God's honoring of Muḥammad with His word, God and His angels bless the Prophet, is more exalting than Adam's honor through the prostration."

- al-Ṭurayḥī (d. 1085 AH), Majmaʿ al-Baḥrayn, vol. 1, p. 283

The school holds the highest station for the Prophet ﷺ and his House, and its doctrine keeps the boundary fixed at the same time. The Qur'an calls him God's servant at the peak of the honor. The boundary verse of the same Book reads, it is not for any mortal whom God has given the Book, and the wisdom, and the prophethood, to say, be servants of me beside God.

مَا كَانَ لِبَشَرٍ أَن يُؤْتِيَهُ ٱللَّهُ ٱلْكِتَٰبَ وَٱلْحُكْمَ وَٱلنُّبُوَّةَ ثُمَّ يَقُولَ لِلنَّاسِ كُونُوا۟ عِبَادًۭا لِّى مِن دُونِ ٱللَّهِ

Mā kāna libasharin an yuʾtiyahū Allāhu al-kitāba wa-al-ḥukma wa-al-nubuwwata thumma yaqūla li-nnāsi kūnū ʿibādan lī min dūni Allāh.

"It is not for any mortal whom God has given the Book, and the wisdom, and the prophethood, to say to the people, be servants of me beside God."

- Qur'an 3:79, partial, Uthmani text

A rise in degree within one kind of honor never becomes worship. The inference from uniquely honored to secretly worshipped is the charge's own conclusion dressed as an observation. The verse the school prays every day reads, O Allah, bless Muḥammad and the House of Muḥammad. It addresses Allah alone from first word to last.

Glossary of Key Terms

Ṣalāt, with plural ṣalawāt, is the noun of the root ṣ-l-w. In the dictionaries its origin is invocation. The ritual prayer received the name from the invocation at its core. Said of God, it is mercy and praise upon a person.

Duʿāʾ is invocation or calling. The addressee is fixed by context, God when the call is worship, and the one spoken for stands after the preposition.

Bāraka is blessing as increase and abundance poured into a thing. The Qur'an uses it of offspring, provision, places, and times.

Taslīm is the greeting of peace, and by extension submission to what the Prophet ﷺ brought.

Allāhumma means O Allah, the vocative that opens every invocation of blessing. It is yā Allāh with the mīm replacing the yā.

Ṣalāt ʿalā al-mayyit is the funeral prayer over the dead. It is invocation for the deceased and contains no bowing or prostration.

Uṣūlīs are the legal theorists of the school, scholars of the roots of law, the discipline that studies how words carry meanings and how proofs work.

Wilāyah is allegiance of love and loyalty, the school's term for the authority of the Prophet ﷺ and the Imams ﵈.

Majāz is extension, a word naming a related sense, and ishtirāk is homonymy, one word for unrelated senses. The legal theorists prefer extension because it keeps one thread of meaning.

Final Verdict

The charge fails on every ground it claims. The dictionaries it cites print the mercy and praise senses inside the same entries. The preposition it leans on marks the beneficiary of a blessing, and the funeral prayer of every Arabic confession proves the construction daily. The Qur'an fixes the divine verb with a purpose clause in the same sura and lays ṣalawāt from the Lord on ordinary believers. It withholds the Prophet's ﷺ ṣalāt from a dead hypocrite as a punishment no one reads as worship. The formula the verse produced addresses Allah and commands both verbs, ṣalli and bārik, in one breath, and the earliest gloss of the verse said the two verbs mean the same thing here.

The school of the Ahl al-Bayt ﵈ receives the verse with defined senses. God's ṣalāt is mercy, praise, and the raising of the Prophet's ﷺ rank. The angels purify and extol. The believers supplicate, and their supplication renews the first covenant. The strongest form of the grammar objection belongs to Ibn Hishām, and the grammarians and the theologians of both traditions answered it with the shared core and the cause named for its effect.

Allah does not pray to Muhammad ﷺ. The verse announces mercy upon him from God and His angels, and it teaches the believers to ask Allah for that mercy with the Household named in the request. The method that produces the charge, applied to the Christian's own canon, makes the LORD of Numbers and the Psalms pray to Israel. A reader who keeps that method must accept what it says about Numbers and the Psalms as well.
