Suppose a person stands before two options, A and B. He considers both and finally chooses A.

What finally explains why he chose A and never B? The verses of decree, the sealed hearts, and the written portions of every creature press that question home from Islam's own scripture.

The Imams of the Ahl al-Bayt ﵈ answered with one sentence. God causes the servant to be a determiner, and He does not determine the servant's determination for him. The choice depends on God for its entire existence without being settled by God as A and never B.

1. The Problem: If God Is Sovereign, How Can We Be Free?

The claim does not argue that free will is impossible in the abstract. It argues that the Qur'an and the reports of the Prophet ﷺ teach that Allah settled every human act before the actor existed.

The Qur'an brings the will itself under Allah's willing, and no one wills except as He wills (Qur'an 76:30). Guidance and misguidance are His to give. He guides whom He wills and leads astray whom He wills (Qur'an 35:8), and had He willed, everyone on earth would have believed (Qur'an 10:99).

The verses reach the heart directly. Allah set a seal on the hearts of the defiant (Qur'an 2:7). Nothing befalls the servants except what Allah has written for them (Qur'an 9:51). Of the idolaters and the idols they carve, Allah created them and what they make (Qur'an 37:96).

The reports carry the same teaching. Before creating the heavens and the earth, Allah wrote the portions of every creature, its term, its provision, its deeds, its joy and its misery, and the ink has dried. The hearts of the children of Adam lie between two of Allah's fingers, and He turns them wherever He wills. The Prophet ﷺ himself prayed to the Turner of hearts to keep his heart firm on obedience.

On these texts the claim stands complete. Every thought, desire, intention, and act was written before its author existed, and what is written comes to pass. Islam then weighs those same acts on the scales of Paradise and Fire. The punishment falls on what the Writer wrote, and the person becomes the place where the writing happens.

The difficult point is the content of what was written, and God's knowledge does not answer it. Knowledge does not cause, and the claim never needed it to. Suppose you choose A, and never B. Was your choosing A written from eternity?

If it was, you could not have chosen B while the writing stayed true. If it was not, then something about you contributed a fact the decree of God did not contain. Writing the word freely inside the decree does not close the gap, because freely choosing A was itself written from eternity.

Creating your powers does not close it either. God made your mind, your desires, your memory, your circumstances, and the alternatives in front of you, and the question is what settled the use of all that, why A and never B. Who determined your determining?

If God determined it, compulsion returns, and with it the injustice of punishing a choice God made. If you determined it on your own, something in creation slipped outside His rule. If neither determined it, the choice is brute.

The objection has a sharper form, and the article meets it there. God eternally and infallibly knows that the servant will choose A. What accounts for A being the servant's act, and never God's determination in the servant's place?

If God settles it, B was closed before the servant existed. If the servant settles it, the content of the eternal writing depends on the creature, and the objector asks whether God has become a scribe, recording what the creature authors. If nothing settles it, A is brute.

Saying that God knows the servant will freely choose A does not answer this. The question is what makes His eternal knowledge be knowledge of A.

The objection may grant that knowledge is not causation. It may grant that God gives power and does not coerce it. It may even grant that the servant is a real secondary cause. Its question remains the hardest one. The question is not whether God already knew which choice the servant would make. He did. The question is whether His knowing the choice is what makes the choice His.

The claim has a modern face, and it deserves to be met at full strength. A philosopher sharpens it with a story. A neuroscientist secretly programs a man's desires so that at noon he gladly commits a crime.

The man feels free, deliberates, and chooses, yet his choice was manufactured. The philosopher then asks what changes when the programmer moves from a laboratory to heaven. If nothing changes, every human choice is manufactured, and praise and blame are theater.

The Imami answer begins by rejecting an assumption hidden inside the dilemma. The dilemma assumes that God's causation and human causation compete with one another. It pictures God and the human being as two causes fighting over the same seat.

If God causes the act, the human being cannot. If the human being causes it, God cannot. The whole objection lives inside that picture.

The reports of the school reject the picture itself. On the reading they teach, the two causes stand on different levels, and the lower cause acts only through the power the higher cause gives it. The sections that follow establish this step by step, first from the Imam's own parable, then from the definitions, then from the conditions of responsibility, and then from the causal structure.

Before the last question is asked, one thing still needs settling, what the divine decree actually does.

2. The Imam's Answer: Allowing an Act Is Not Forcing It

Report. Imam Jaʿfar al-Ṣādiq ﵇

Source. al-Kafi, vol.1, p.631, report 413, and vol.1, p.208, Dar al-Hadith ed.

Compiled. al-Kulayni (d.329 AH)

The Imam's answer to that hidden assumption is also the doctrine's clearest explanation, and it is the earliest. A narrator brought the formula to Imam Jaʿfar al-Ṣādiq ﵇ and asked what it means. The Imam answered with a story any listener can test against his own experience.

Show the original Arabic

عَنْ أَبِي عَبْدِ اللَّهِ عَلَيْهِ السَّلَامُ ، قَالَ : « لَا جَبْرَ وَلَا تَفْوِيضَ ، وَلكِنْ أَمْرٌ بَيْنَ أَمْرَيْنِ (2) » .

قَالَ : قُلْتُ : وَمَا أَمْرٌ بَيْنَ أَمْرَيْنِ ؟

قَالَ : « مَثَلُ ذلِكَ : رَجُلٌ رَأَيْتَهُ عَلى مَعْصِيَةٍ ، فَنَهَيْتَهُ ، فَلَمْ يَنْتَهِ ، فَتَرَكْتَهُ ، فَفَعَلَ تِلْكَ الْمَعْصِيَةَ ؛ فَلَيْسَ (3) حَيْثُ لَمْ يَقْبَلْ مِنْكَ فَتَرَكْتَهُ (4) كُنْتَ أَنْتَ الَّذِي أَمَرْتَهُ بِالْمَعْصِيَةِ » . (5)

"From Imam Jaʿfar al-Ṣādiq. He said, there is no compulsion and no delegation, but a matter between two extremes.

The narrator asked, and what is a matter between two extremes?

The Imam replied, suppose you see a man committing a sin and tell him to stop. He refuses, so you leave him alone, and he goes on to commit the sin.

The fact that he ignored you and you did not physically stop him does not mean that you commanded him to commit the sin."

- al-Kulayni (d.329 AH), al-Kafi, Dar al-Hadith ed., vol.1, p.631

You told the man not to commit the act. You had the power to physically prevent him. You chose not to force him. He nevertheless chose to commit it.

Your refusal to physically control him does not make his choice your choice. That is the whole point, and it carries over to God directly. Not preventing a choice is not the same thing as causing that choice. God commands the servant not to sin. God gives guidance and warning.

God has the power to prevent the servant from acting. When God permits the servant to exercise his choice, that permission is not God choosing the sin for him.

The classical commentator al-Mazandarani (d.1081 AH) drew the mapping out in his commentary on this report.

Show the original Arabic

(ولكن أمر بين أمرين ، قال : قلت : وما أمر بين أمرين ؟

قال : مثل ذلك رجل رأيته على معصيته فنهيته) عنها (فلم ينته فتركته) بحاله وما زجرته عنها جبراً وقهراً (ففعل تلك المعصية) بقدرته واختياره (فليس حيث لم يقبل منك فتركته) مع قدرتك (1) على زجره عنها جبراً (كنت أنت الَّذي أمرته بالمعصية) أي جبّرته عليها ، أطلق الأمر على الجبر مجازاً فكما أنّك لمّا منعته منها بالزَّواجر والنصائح ما فوّضت الأمر إليه ولمّا رأيته أنّه يفعلها فتركته وما منعته منعاً يوجب تركه ما أجبرته عليها ، كذلك صنع الله بالنسبة إلى أفعال العباد فهذا أمر بين أمرين

"He committed that sin through his own power and his own choice. You left him as he was and did not drive him from it by compulsion and overpowering. It is not because he refused your warning and you left him alone, though you had the power to stop him by force, that you were the one who commanded him to commit the sin. To command it here means to compel him to it.

In the same way, when you held him back with warnings and counsel, you did not hand the affair over to him. And when you saw that he would commit it and left him without a restraint that would force him to stop, you did not compel him to it. That is how Allah deals with the actions of His servants. This is the matter between two extremes."

- al-Mazandarani (d.1081 AH), Sharh Usul al-Kafi, vol.5, p.33

One example already excludes both extremes. God has not delegated everything to the servant, because the servant remains dependent upon God and receives guidance, power, opportunity, and existence from Him at every moment. God has not compelled the servant, because the servant himself chooses how to act.

The warning was real, and so was the freedom to ignore it.

The parable also settles the neuroscientist's story. The programmed man in the laboratory never received a warning he could refuse. No one showed him the road and then stepped back.

The servant in the parable receives guidance, refuses it, and acts on his refusal. The difference between the two cases is exactly the difference the doctrine needs.

3. What Jabr and Tafwid Actually Mean

Contested words. jabr, tafwid, and qada, the two named errors and the decree the dialogue defines

Lexicons quoted. Lisan al-Arab of Ibn Manzur (d.711 AH) and al-Ayn of al-Khalil ibn Ahmad (d.175 AH), with the poet al-Ajjaj and the grammarian al-Azhari inside the entries

The two errors have names because the Imams named them, and the reports define them before the dictionaries confirm them. Jabr means that the servant's act is forced upon him, so that God performs the action and then punishes the servant for what He Himself did. Tafwid means that the servant, or another created being, has been handed an independent power by which he manages an affair apart from God, so that the affair leaves God's control.

Both errors can be stated with the needed precision. Jabr says that God determines the choice for the servant, settling A on his behalf. Tafwid says that the servant determines the choice through a power independent of God.

The middle says that the servant determines the choice through a power whose existence and operation depend on God at every moment. A dependent cause can genuinely determine an effect without becoming an independent cause.

The parable of the previous section already showed God acting in neither sense, because God warned, and God did not chain the hand. Imam al-Rida ﵇ defined both errors in a dialogue at Merv, quoted in the next section, and the definitions there match the dictionaries exactly.

The classical lexicons confirm the senses. Al-Azhari, quoted in Ibn Manzur's Lisan al-Arab, fixes jabr with two plain words, overpowering and coercion.

ابن الأَنباري : الجبار في صفة الله ﷿ الذي لا يُنالُ ، ومنه جَبَّارُ النخل .

Al-Azhari said, jabbar in the description of Allah, or of the servants, comes from ijbar, compulsion, which is overpowering and coercion, and never from jabara, the restoring."

- Ibn Manzur (d.711 AH), Lisan al-Arab, vol.4, p.113

Tafwid has its own entry, and the entry is one sentence long. To delegate an affair is to hand it over so that the recipient runs it.

فوض : فوضت إليه الأمر أي جعلته إليه . [ وقال الله - جل وعز - : وأفوض أمري إلى الله ، أي أتكل عليه ] .

"Fawad (fawad, fawadtu ilayhi al-amr) means I handed the affair over to him, that is, I made it his (ayy jaʿaltuhu ilayh).

And Allah, mighty and majestic, says, and I commit my affair to Allah (wa-afawdu amri ila Allah), meaning I trust in Him (ayy atakkilu ʿalayh)."

- al-Khalil ibn Ahmad (d.175 AH), al-Ayn, vol.7, p.64

One more word needs its dictionary before the reports use it. Qada, the decree, is the word the Merv dialogue will define over human acts, and the lexicons hold two families of sense apart for it.

Show the original Arabic

وقَضاء الشيء : إِحْكامُه وإِمْضاؤُه والفراغ منه فيكون بمعنى الخَلْق .

وقَضَى أَي حَكَمَ ، ومنه القضاء والقَدر . وقوله تعالى : وقَضَى ربُّك أَن لا تعبدوا إِلَّا إِياه ؛ أَي أَمَر ربك وحَتم ، وهو أَمر قاطع حَتْم .

"Qada, when said of a thing (qadaʾu al-shayʾi), is its completion, its execution, and the finishing of it, so that it can carry the meaning of creation.

Qada, when said of a judge (qada, ayy hakama), means he judged, and from this comes the decree joined to the measure (al-qada wa-l-qadar).

Allah's word, and your Lord decreed that you worship none but Him, means your Lord commanded and bindingly decreed, a cutting command."

- Ibn Manzur (d.711 AH), Lisan al-Arab, vol.15, p.186

The two families sit side by side in the entry, the judgment and the command on one side, the completing and creating of a thing on the other. The dialogue at Merv picks the judgment sense when it defines the decree over human acts, and the argument returns to that choice when it reaches the report.

Two short lexical notes complete the ground, because the root jabara carries a second, unrelated life in the dictionaries. The same verb names the setting of a broken bone and the restoring of loss, and the poets show that sense in use.

Show the original Arabic

ويقال : جَبَرْتُ العظم جَبْراً وجَبَرَ العظمُ بنفسه جُبُوراً أَي انجَبَر ؛ وقد جمع العجاج بين المتعدي واللازم فقال :

قد جَبَر الدِّينَ الإِله فَجَبَرْ

"It is said, I set the broken bone (jabartu al-ʿazma jabran), and the bone sets and heals by itself (juburan). Al-Ajjaj joined the transitive and the intransitive in one verse (qad jabara al-dina al-ilahu fa-jabar) when he said, God restored the religion, and it stood firm."

- Ibn Manzur (d.711 AH), Lisan al-Arab, vol.4, p.115

The doctrine's negation targets the coercion sense alone. No report of the school ever denied that God heals, restores, and mends, and the divine name al-Jabbār, the Compeller, belongs to that discussion. The lexicons gloss the name as dominion over creation, the One who subdues what He wills of command and prohibition, and the school reads the name as rule over things and over existence itself.

The reports distinguish things from acts, and the subduing of a thing is a different matter from the manufacturing of a servant's choice. The name and the doctrine meet on that line, and neither cancels the other.

4. Imam al-Rida Defines the Middle Position

Report. Imam Ali ibn Musa al-Rida ﵇, in a dialogue at Merv

Source. Bihar al-Anwar, vol.5, p.14, relaying al-Saduq (d.381 AH), Uyun Akhbar al-Rida, and Tafsir al-Burhan, vol.3, p.516, relaying al-Tabrisi, al-Ihtijaj

The definitions come from a dialogue recorded at Merv. A questioner brought the formula of Imam Jaʿfar al-Ṣādiq ﵇ to Imam al-Rida ﵇ and asked for its meaning, and the Imam defined both errors and then the middle.

Show the original Arabic

روي لنا عن الصادق جعفر بن محمد ﵇ أنه قال : لا جبر ولا تفويض بل أمر بين أمرين فما معناه ؟ فقال : من زعم أن الله يفعل أفعالنا ثم يعذبنا عليها فقد قال بالجبر ومن زعم أن الله ﷿ فوض أمر الخلق والرزق إلى حججه ﵈ فقد قال بالتفويض فالقائل بالجبر كافر والقائل بالتفويض مشرك .

"It was related to us from al-Sadiq Jaʿfar ibn Muhammad that he said, there is no compulsion and no delegation, but a matter between two extremes. So what does it mean?

Imam al-Rida said, whoever claims that Allah performs our actions and then punishes us for them has affirmed compulsion.

And whoever claims that Allah handed the affairs of creation and provision over to His proofs has affirmed delegation.

The one who affirms compulsion is an unbeliever, and the one who affirms delegation is an idolater."

- al-Majlisi (d.1111 AH), Bihar al-Anwar, vol.5, p.14, relaying al-Saduq (d.381 AH), Uyun Akhbar al-Rida

The two definitions matter, and each one names a precise error. The first position makes God the performer of the human act, and punishment then falls on a person who did nothing. The second position hands a created being an independent sphere of divine power, creation and provision themselves, and God's rule ends where that sphere begins.

The Imam's words name the extreme case, handing creation and provision to His proofs, meaning the Imams ﵈ themselves, and delegating divine affairs to any created being is the same error. It sets up a second manager inside creation, and the second horn of the dilemma is the philosophical form of it.

The Imam rejects both errors, and the verdicts attached to them, unbelief and idolatry, attach to the positions exactly as he defined them. The definitions fix the target, never the labels.

فقال : «وجود السبيل إلى إتيان ما أمروا به ، وترك ما نهوا عنه» .

قلت له : وهل لله مشيئة وإرادة في ذلك ؟ فقال : «أما الطاعات فإرادة الله تعالى ومشيئته فيها الأمر بها ، والرضا لها ، والمعاونة عليها ، وإرادته ومشيئته في المعاصي النهي عنها ، والسخط لها ، والخذلان عليها» .

قلت : فلله ﷿ [فيها] القضاء ؟ قال : «نعم ، ما من فعل يفعله العباد من خير أو شر إلا ولله فيه قضاء» . قلت : فما معنى هذا القضاء ؟ قال : «الحكم عليهم بما يستحقونه من الثواب والعقاب في الدنيا والآخرة» .

"He said, it means that people have a real way to do what they have been commanded to do and to avoid what they have been forbidden to do.

The questioner asked, does Allah have a will concerning that? He said, as for obedience, Allah's will concerning it is that He commands it, is pleased with it, and helps the servant toward it.

His will concerning sins is that He forbids them, is angry at them, and withholds His help from the one who persists in them.

The questioner asked, does Allah make a decree concerning them? He said, yes. There is no act the servants do, of good or of evil, in which Allah has no decree.

He asked, and what does this decree mean? He said, it is the judgment upon them of what they deserve, of reward and punishment, in this world and the next."

- al-Bahrani (d.1107 AH), Tafsir al-Burhan, vol.3, p.516, relaying al-Tabrisi, al-Ihtijaj

The middle is defined first as a real way that stands open. People can actually do what they are commanded to do and avoid what they are forbidden to do. The way would not be real if the act were forced, and it would not be commanded if the servant had no hand in it.

The most important distinction in the whole doctrine concerns God's will for obedience and His relation to sin. God wills obedience in the sense that He commands it, approves of it, and helps the servant toward it. God's relation to sin is different.

He forbids it and disapproves of it. Yet He may still permit the sinner to possess the power and opportunity through which the sin becomes possible. The distinction comes first in plain English, and the technical names follow it.

The school calls God's will of command, approval, and prohibition the legislative will, irada tashriʿiyya, and calls His will by which a thing receives existence the existential or creative will, irada takwiniyya. The names label a distinction the report already made, and they never carry the explanation by themselves.

The decree, qada, receives its own definition in the same dialogue. Over human acts, good and evil alike, the decree is the judgment of what the act deserves, reward or punishment, in this world and the next. The decree that touches every act is a decree of reckoning, and the report keeps it apart from any manufacture of the act from inside the servant.

Al-Hurr al-Amili carries the same definitions in his legal corpus, which shows the school received the doctrine as law.

5. What Makes a Person Responsible?

Reports. Imam al-ʿAskari ﵇, in his letter to the people of al-Ahwaz, and Imam al-Rida ﵇, relaying a divine speech from Ali ibn al-Husayn ﵇

Sources. Bihar al-Anwar, vol.5, p.24, relaying al-Ihtijaj, and Sharh Usul al-Kafi, vol.5, p.33, report 12, also carried in al-Kafi, vol.1, p.208

The middle receives its fullest definition in a letter. Asked about the two errors, Imam al-ʿAskari ﵇ listed what must be present before an act can be laid to the servant's account.

Show the original Arabic

فقال : صحة العقل ، وتخلية السرب ، والمهلة في الوقت ، والزاد من قبل الراحلة ، والسبب المهيج للفاعل على فعله ، فهذه خمسة أشياء فإذا نقص العبد منها خلة كان العمل عنه مطرحا بحسبه

"He said, sound reason, freedom from restraint, enough time to act, the necessary means, and the motive that moves the doer to his deed.

These are five things, and whenever one of them is missing, the act falls from the servant's account to that extent."

- al-Majlisi (d.1111 AH), Bihar al-Anwar, vol.5, p.24, relaying al-Ihtijaj

Sound reason means the person can understand what he is doing. Freedom from restraint means no one is forcing his hand or locking his path. Enough time means the moment of action is genuinely available to him. The necessary means cover the ability, the tools, and the provisions the act requires. A motive is a reason that moves him toward the act at all.

All of these conditions are received. The servant did not create his own existence. He did not create his basic faculties.

He does not create time. He does not independently sustain his power. Yet none of these gifts, by themselves, determines what he chooses to do with them.

A person may possess intelligence, money, time, opportunity, and a strong desire to steal. Those things make theft possible. They do not make theft compulsory.

The moral question begins with what the person does with what he has been given.

One distinction from this list will matter in the closing sections of the argument. A motive influences a choice. It does not necessarily determine it. The letter itself counts the motive among the conditions of accountability, so the school treats motives as ingredients of responsible action, never as forces that switch the person off.

A companion of Imam al-Rida ﵇ told him that some of their fellows affirmed compulsion and others affirmed an independent ability. The Imam told him to write down a divine speech transmitted from Ali ibn al-Husayn ﵇, Imam Zayn al-Abidin. Al-Kulayni carries the report in al-Kafi, and al-Mazandarani quotes it in his commentary.

The theological idea is unmistakable. The ability comes from God. The use of that ability belongs to the servant. The willing self exists by God's will, the duties are carried out by God's power, and even the strength a person spends on sin is a strength God gave him. The good is credited to God because He is its giver and its arranger, nearer to it than the servant himself.

The evil is laid to the servant because he is the one who chose its direction. The closing line of the speech reads I have arranged for you everything you want, and it means the means are arranged, the hearing, the sight, the power, and the path to whatever is wanted.

What gets wanted is decided by the one who hears and sees. One speech carries all of these truths without competition.

6. Our Power Is Real, but It Comes From God

Reports. Imam al-Rida ﵇, Imam Ali ﵇, and Imam al-ʿAskari ﵇

Source. Ilm al-Yaqin, vol.1, p.383, and vol.2, p.266, compiled by al-Kashani (d.1091 AH). The first report is recorded earlier by al-Saduq (d.381 AH) in al-Tawhid and Uyun Akhbar al-Rida

The divine speech traced every gift to God, and one report gathers the whole structure of those gifts into a few lines. Al-Saduq records it with his chain from Sulayman ibn Jaʿfar al-Jaʿfari, from Imam al-Rida ﵇, and al-Kashani carries it in Ilm al-Yaqin.

Show the original Arabic

فقال : - «إنَّ اللَّه - عزَّ وجلَّ - لم يطع بإكراه ، ولم يعص بغلبة ، ولم يهمل العباد في ملكه ، وهو المالك لما ملَّكهم ، والقادر على ما أقدرهم عليه ، فإن ائتمر العباد بطاعته لم يكن اللَّه عنها صادّا ، ولا منها مانعا ،

وإن ائتمروا بمعصيته فشاء أن يحول بينه (1) وبين ذلك لفعل ، وإن لم يحل وفعلوه ، فليس هو الذي أدخلهم فيه» .

"He said, Allah, mighty and majestic, is not obeyed because He forces people to obey, and He is not disobeyed because anyone overpowers Him. He has not abandoned His servants within His kingdom. He is the Owner of whatever He has allowed them to possess, and He has power over whatever power He has given them.

If the servants choose to obey Him, Allah does not stand in their way and does not prevent them.

And if they choose to disobey Him, He can, if He wills, come between them and the sin. But if He does not come between them and they commit it, He is not the One who made them enter into it."

- al-Kashani (d.1091 AH), Ilm al-Yaqin, vol.1, p.383

One sentence in this report is among the clearest formulations in the entire doctrine. He is the Owner of what He has allowed them to own, and He has power over the power He has given them. Human power is real. Human power is not independent. The servant possesses power from God, never beside God.

In plain English, whatever you own, you own because God let you own it, and whatever you can do, you can do because God keeps the ability alive in you. Ownership through God is the formula, and ownership apart from God is what the doctrine denies.

The report also settles the question of physical prevention. God can come between a servant and the sin he resolved on, and sometimes He does. God can block the outcome without touching the choice.

A servant whose hand is stopped still wanted the sin, and the wanting was his own. A servant whose hand is left free is not forced by that freedom to want anything. The doctrine concerns the choice, and the reports keep the choice and the outcome distinct.

A report from Imam Ali ﵇ gives the same structure for ability itself. A man asked him how to speak about what he owns, and the answer teaches the ownership formula and then explains the most recited phrase in the matter, la hawla wa-la quwwata illa bi-llah.

Show the original Arabic

قال : (5) «تقول : تملكها باللَّه الذي يملكها من دونك ؛ فإن تملَّككها ، كان ذلك من عطائه ، وإن سلبكها ، كان ذلك من بلائه ؛ هو المالك لما

ملَّكك ، والمالك لما عليه أقدرك ؛ أما سمعت الناس يسألون الحول والقوَّة ، حيث يقولون : لا حول ولا قوَّة إلَّا باللَّه » ؟ .

"He said, you say, I own her through Allah, who owns her apart from me. If He grants her to me, that is from His gift, and if He takes her from me, that is from His trial.

He is the Owner of whatever He has let me own, and He has power over whatever power He has given me. Have you not heard people ask for strength and ability when they say, there is no power and no strength except through Allah?

The man asked, what does it mean, Commander of the Faithful?

- al-Kashani (d.1091 AH), Ilm al-Yaqin, vol.1, p.383

La hawla wa-la quwwata illa bi-llah is often heard as a denial of human agency, and the report forbids that hearing. Its point here is that every ability we possess depends upon God's continuing aid and preservation. The phrase names dependence, and dependence is not passivity.

A person who has no strength to obey except by God's aid still obeys, and the obedience is genuinely his.

The report of the hearts carries the positive side of the same coin, and it answers a man who asked about the doctrine of the delegators.

وجئت تسأل عن مقالة المفوّضة ؟ كذبوا ؛ بل قلوبنا أوعية لمشيَّة اللَّه ، فإذا شاء اللَّه شئنا ، واللَّه يقول : وَما تَشاؤُنَ إِلَّا أَنْ يَشاءَ اللَّهُ [ 76 / 30 ]

"You have come to ask about the doctrine of the delegators. They lie.

Our hearts are vessels of Allah's will, so when Allah wills, we will.

Allah says, and you do not will, except that Allah wills (Qur'an 76:30)."

- al-Kashani (d.1091 AH), Ilm al-Yaqin, vol.2, p.266

A philosopher reads that verse as the death of freedom, because he reads every act of willing as one more event competing for space beside God's will. The school reads it through everything already established above. The heart is a vessel, a capacity held in being by God, and when He grants being, power, reason, time, and occasion, the person wills.

The verse names the dependence of the will upon God's will, and the reports have already separated that dependence from coercion. A vessel does not compete with what fills it.

7. God Sustains the Act Without Choosing the Sin

Reports. Imam al-Hadi ﵇, and Imam Jaʿfar al-Ṣādiq ﵇

Sources. Bihar al-Anwar, vol.5, p.24, relaying al-Saduq (d.381 AH), al-Iʿtiqadat, and al-Kafi, vol.1, p.208

The hearts are vessels held by God, and the hardest idea in the doctrine begins with an ordinary distinction. There is a difference between explaining why an act exists at all and explaining why the person chose that act and not some other. The first explanation traces bodies, minds, power, and opportunity to God, who sustains them.

The second explanation names the chooser, and the chooser is the servant.

When a person commits a sin, his body exists because God sustains it. His mind exists because God sustains it. His physical power exists because God sustains it.

The moment and the opportunity in which he acts exist because God sustains creation. None of that means God selected the sinful direction in which the servant used those gifts.

Show the original Arabic

وقد روي عن أبي الحسن الثالث ﵇ أنه سئل عن أفعال العباد أهي مخلوقة لله تعالى ؟ فقال ﵇ لو كان خالقا لها لما تبرأ منها وقد قال سبحانه : " إن الله برئ من المشركين " ولم يرد البراءة من خلق ذواتهم ، وإنما تبرأ من شركهم وقبائحهم

"It is related from Imam al-Hadi that he was asked, are the actions of the servants created by Allah?

He said, if He were their creator, He would not have declared Himself clear of them. The Exalted said, Allah is clear of the idolaters.

He did not mean that He is clear of creating their bodies. He meant that He is clear of their idolatry and their wicked deeds."

- al-Majlisi (d.1111 AH), Bihar al-Anwar, vol.5, p.24

A note on the attribution, because the epithet in the Arabic needs resolving. The Arabic calls him Abu al-Hasan al-Thalith, the third Imam who bore the epithet Abu al-Hasan, and al-Saduq transmits the report under that epithet in al-Iʿtiqadat. The epithet belongs to Ali ibn Muhammad al-Hadi ﵇, the tenth Imam.

Bihar al-Anwar opens his biography with this very designation, and al-Tusi's Rijal lists his companions under it, so the speaker is Imam al-Hadi ﵇.

The argument of the report needs care, because two different claims travel under the words God did not create our acts. One claim says God created nothing in the act at all, and that claim cannot stand, since the sinner, his limbs, his power, and his moment all exist by God's continuous grant. The precise claim is narrower and stronger.

God declared Himself clear of the idolaters' shirk itself while He sustains the idolaters, so the sin, as sin, traces to the servant. God sustains the existence, the faculties, and the power involved in an evil act, and He is not the selector or the moral author of its sinful direction. That is the claim the reports carry, and every section of the argument stays inside it.

Show the original Arabic

عدة من أصحابنا ، عن أحمد بن محمد البرقي ، عن علي بن الحكم ، عن هشام بن سالم ، عن أبي عبد الله ﵇ قال : الله أكرم من أن يكلف الناس ما لا يطيقون والله أعز من أن يكون في سلطانه ما لا يريد .

"Imam al-Sadiq said, Allah is too generous to burden people with what they cannot bear, and Allah is too mighty for anything in His dominion to exist against His will."

- al-Kulayni (d.329 AH), al-Kafi, vol.1, p.208

Two sayings close the frame. The first keeps God clear of authorship of evil. The second keeps His dominion without escape, and it adds that God never burdens a person beyond what He gave him the strength to bear.

Nothing occurs in the kingdom against the existential will of God, the will by which things receive their being. The kingdom itself, and the power in the servant's hand, and the moment in which he acts, all exist by continuous divine grant. The logic matches the divine speech of the fifth section, the good to God, the evil to the self.

The two claims look incompatible only inside the picture of competing causes, and that picture is the one thing the school refuses to grant.

8. The Qur'an Places the Choice With the Human Being

The previous section ended with a dominion in which nothing escapes God and a sin that traces to the servant himself. The verses of the Qur'an draw the same line, and each one establishes one part of it. God assigns the guidance, the showing, and the forming to Himself. The response, the walking, and the purifying are attributed to the human being.

Show the original Arabic

إِنّا هَدَيْناهُ السَّبِيلَ إِمّا شاكِراً وإِمّا كَفُوراً

inna hadaynahu al-sabila, imma shakiran wa-imma kafuran

"We showed him the way, whether he becomes grateful or ungrateful." (Qur'an 76:3)

- Qur'an 76:3, relayed in al-Masabih, p.12

The showing belongs to God, and the becoming belongs to the servant. The verse names both halves in one line, gratitude and ingratitude held open after the way has been shown.

Show the original Arabic

وَهَدَيْناهُ النَّجْدَيْنِ

wa-hadaynahu al-najdayn

"And We showed him the two paths." (Qur'an 90:10)

- Qur'an 90:10, cited in al-Masabih, p.12

God shows both roads, the climbing one and the low one. The Qur'an never says that God selects which road the servant walks. The verses gathered here give the walking to the servant himself.

Show the original Arabic

وَقُلِ ٱلْحَقُّ مِن رَّبِّكُمْ ۖ فَمَن شَآءَ فَلْيُؤْمِن وَمَن شَآءَ فَلْيَكْفُرْ

wa-qul al-haqqu min rabbikum, fa-man shaaʾa falyuʾmin wa-man shaaʾa falyakfur

"And say, the truth is from your Lord. Whoever wills, let him believe, and whoever wills, let him disbelieve." (Qur'an 18:29)

- Qur'an 18:29

Belief and disbelief are both addressed to the human will directly. A compatibilist holds that a choice can be both determined and responsible, and on that reading God commands the willing He also grants.

The division of the two wills established at Merv still stands inside that reading, and the imperative addresses the servant's own decision, never replacing it.

These verses consistently address human beings as genuine agents who will, choose, purify themselves, corrupt themselves, believe, disbelieve, and change what is within themselves. Any interpretation of the divine decree must therefore preserve those human attributions and never explain them away.

That is the defensible reading, and it rests on the whole Qur'an.

Show the original Arabic

وَنَفْسٍۢ وَمَا سَوَّىٰهَا * فَأَلْهَمَهَا فُجُورَهَا وَتَقْوَىٰهَا * قَدْ أَفْلَحَ مَن زَكَّىٰهَا * وَقَدْ خَابَ مَن دَسَّىٰهَا

wa-nafsin wa-ma sawwaha, fa-alhamaha fujuraha wa-taqwaha, qad aflaha man zakkaha, wa-qad khaba man dassaha

"And by the soul and He who shaped it. Then He inspired it with its wickedness and its duty.

Successful is the one who purifies it, and ruined is the one who corrupts it." (Qur'an 91:7-10)

- Qur'an 91:7-10

God creates and forms the soul, and He makes moral knowledge available to it, the inspiration of both its wickedness and its duty. Then the outcome changes subject. The one who purifies the soul is the person, and the one who corrupts it is the person.

The enabling is God's, and the purification is ours.

Show the original Arabic

إِنَّ ٱللَّهَ لَا يُغَيِّرُ مَا بِقَوْمٍ حَتَّىٰ يُغَيِّرُوا۟ مَا بِأَنفُسِهِمْ

inna Allaha la yughayyiru ma bi-qawmin hatta yughayyiru ma bi-anfusihim

"Allah does not change the condition of a people until they change what is within themselves." (Qur'an 13:11)

- Qur'an 13:11

This verse deserves special weight. The change God brings about in a people's condition waits on a change inside the people themselves. The verse does not say that Allah changes what is in them first, and it does not say they act apart from Him.

Their inner change is treated as a genuine contribution that belongs to them, and the divine change follows it. That is precisely the relationship the reports call the matter between two extremes, now stated in the grammar of a conditional.

One decree verse outranks all the others in this debate, and it deserves to be met in its own context.

Show the original Arabic

أَ تَعْبُدُونَ ما تَنْحِتُونَ (95) وَاللَّهُ خَلَقَكُمْ وَما تَعْمَلُونَ (96)

a-taʿbuduna ma tanhituna, wa-llahu khalaqakum wa-ma taʿmalun

"Do you worship what you carve, while Allah created you and what you make?" (Qur'an 37:95-96)

- Qur'an 37:95-96, as carried in al-Sharif al-Murtada, Tafsir Nafais al-Taʾwil, vol.1, p.380

The school's own exegete read the verse inside its scene. Abraham was rebuking men who carved idols out of wood and then worshipped their own carving.

Show the original Arabic

يقول خلقكم وخلق الخشب الذي عملتموه صنما ، فسمّى الصنم الذي عملوه عملا لهم وإن كان الذي حلّ فيه من التصوير عملهم

"It means He created you, and He created the wood you carved into an idol. So He named the idol they made their work, even though the designing that resided in it was theirs."

- al-Sharif al-Murtada (d.436 AH), Tafsir Nafais al-Taʾwil, vol.1, p.380

Read in context, the strongest decree verse of the dispute names the idols, and God's creating names the wood. The same tafsir extends the principle across the Qur'an. God created the iron, and David's craft of making mail settled in it.

God created the wood, and Noah's craft of building the ship settled in it. The pattern gives God the existence of the material and gives the servant the direction of the craft. That is the same line the reports have drawn throughout, now stated by the verse the objector counts as his strongest.

قوله (لا إكراه في الدين) اي لا يكره أحد على دينه الا بعد ان قد تبين له الرشد من الغي

qawluhu, la ikraha fi al-din, ayy la yukraha ahadun ʿala dinihi illa baʿda an tabayyana lahu al-rushdu min al-ghayy

The sealing verses form the last family the objector reaches for. Allah sealed their hearts, the Qur'an says of the defiant, and God leads astray whom He wills. Read as God sealing hearts first and people sinning afterward, those verses would teach compulsion.

The school reads them in their own order, and its own report explains the seal.

And 35:8 describes a man whose own evil deed has been made to look fair to him, so the deed is his before the fair-seeming is God's.

The choosing comes first in the verses' own order, and the divine act follows it, which is the order every report on the subject has taught.

Read so, the verses of misguidance presuppose the very choice they respond to, and the two wills of Merv keep God's command apart from His abandonment throughout.

The Qur'an states the negation of compulsion in religion as the standing rule, and the school's own tafsir reads the exception clause as a boundary of clarity. God's method is to make the truth clear first, and the reports gathered here describe that clarification, guidance, warning, and a real way left open. The boundary God draws is the boundary of knowing, never the boundary of force.

9. Two Causes Without Competition

Source. Sirat al-Haqq, vol.2, p.203, p.220, and p.221

Speaker. Shaykh Muhammad Asif al-Muhsini, contemporary

The verses have given every role its owner, and the philosophical objection still imagines God and the human being as two competing causes trying to occupy the same position. If God causes the act, the human being cannot, and if the human being causes it, God cannot. Everything gathered so far points to a different picture.

A person writes a sentence with a pen. The sentence is genuinely attributable to the writer, and the movement is genuinely attributable to the pen, in the sense that the pen really moves and really writes. These two explanations do not compete, because the pen acts through the writer's power.

The pen contributes nothing the writer did not give it, yet the movement on the page is truly the pen's movement. The analogy is limited, and the limit matters. A human being is not an unconscious instrument like a pen.

The servant is a conscious secondary cause whom God has given genuine agency. The relation between God and the servant runs deeper than the relation between a writer and his pen, and the two relations are stacked in the same way.

Show the original Arabic

استناد فعل أو شيء إلى العلتين قد يكون عرضياً وقد يكون طولياً وعلى الأوّل يتمّ تخيّل الأشعري وعلى الثاني يتمّ ما هو الحق من الأمر بين الأمرين ، وقد وقع الماديون وطوايف من المسلمين منهم الأشاعرة في الاشتباه بين القسمين فانحرفوا عن الحق .

"The tracing of an act, or of a thing, to two causes may be lateral, and it may be vertical.

On the lateral reading, the picture of the Asharis holds. On the vertical reading, the truth of the matter between two extremes holds.

The materialists, and groups of Muslims among them the Asharis, confused the two kinds and swerved from the truth."

- al-Muhsini (contemporary), Sirat al-Haqq, vol.2, p.220

Horizontal or lateral causation is two causes competing at the same level, rivals for one effect. Vertical causation is one real cause acting through a power whose existence comes from a higher cause. The Imami claim is that God's causation and human causation are vertical. God causes the servant to exist as an agent, and the servant then genuinely acts as that agent.

The human act is therefore attributable to both, in different respects, to God as the One who gives existence and power, and to the servant as the one who chooses the act.

Al-Muhsini states the structure in detail.

Show the original Arabic

فالواجب الوجود هو الذي يبقي النفس ويعطيها القوة والمكنة والاقدار على إيجادها الأفعال ، بل الشوق الذي هو من مرجحات إرادتها وقصدها نحو الفعل مخلوق لله تعالى بلا مدخلية لاختيار النفس ، بل هي مجبولة ومفطورة على اشتياق ما يلائمها والتنفُّر عمَّا يزاحمها ، وإنَّما للنفس - بإذن الله وإقداره وتمكينه - هو الاختيار في قصدها نحو الفعل وعدم قصدها نحوه ، فلها أن تفعل ، ولها أن لا تفعل ، ولكن إذا فعلت فعلت بقوة الله التي أعطاها .

"The Necessary Being continually sustains the soul and gives it the strength and ability through which it acts.

Even its natural desires and inclinations are created by Allah. By its nature, the soul desires what it sees as suitable and recoils from what it sees as harmful.

What belongs to the soul, through Allah's permission and the ability He gives it, is the choice to pursue an act or to refrain from it.

It can act and it can refrain. But when it acts, it acts through the power Allah has given it."

- al-Muhsini (contemporary), Sirat al-Haqq, vol.2, p.203

In ordinary words, God keeps the soul in existence and hands it its strength. Even the desires that pull at it are His creation, and the school says so plainly. What the soul itself contributes is the decision, to aim at the act or to let it go.

That decision is real. It is the soul's own, and it runs on borrowed power.

Show the original Arabic

والحقُّ أن يقال : إنَّ إرادة الله تعلَّقت بكل شيء حتى بالأفعال الاختيارية من دون أن يستلزم الجبر ! إذ الواجب يفيض على الممكن في بقاء وجوده وصدور أفعاله آناً فآناً ، سواء في حال صلواته أم في حال زناه ، كما بيّناه سابقاً ، وإرادته ليست إلا إفاضته

"The truth is that Allah's will attaches to everything, even to free acts, without this entailing compulsion.

God continually gives the created being its existence and the power through which its actions occur, moment by moment, in its state of prayer and in its state of fornication alike.

His will is nothing other than His giving."

- al-Muhsini (contemporary), Sirat al-Haqq, vol.2, p.221

One admission in that quotation has to be joined to the letter of the five conditions, because the two travel together. God builds the desires, and the desires weigh on the choice. A motive is still different in kind from a necessitating cause.

Fire produces burning wherever it stands, and the same effect always follows the same fire. A desire does not work that way. The same desire, in the same person, is obeyed at one hour and refused at the next, and the refusal is as real as the obedience.

If the God-given desires necessitated the sin infallibly, the sin would return to the One who gave them, and the dialogue at Merv cleared God of exactly that return.

God wills the praying and the sinning in the sense that He grants both acts their being, moment by moment, while commanding the one and forbidding the other. God gives the servant the power to act, but does not thereby determine which action the servant chooses. The decree that Merv defined as judgment still holds its place here, because every act that receives its being also receives its reckoning, and the giving will and the judging decree never compete.

10. What Role Does the Decree Play?

One confusion has to be removed before the last question is asked, why the servant chooses A and never B. The decree is not an additional cause inserted before the human choice. The divine decree is not another cause standing behind the servant and pushing his will toward A.

The reports have already separated God's roles. God gives the servant existence, power, opportunity, and the continuing ability to act. The servant then determines how that power is used.

The decree does not replace this human cause. The dialogue at Merv defined the decree over human acts as the judgment of what they deserve, and the lexicons keep that judgment sense beside the sense of completing and creating. Nothing in either sense manufactures the servant's decision toward A.

So the causal order is drawn wrongly when the objector draws it.

The order the objector pictures.

God decrees A. The decree determines the servant. The servant chooses A.

The order the reports carry.

God creates and sustains a real choosing agent. The servant chooses A. The whole event, including its human cause, falls within God's judgment and sovereignty.

This is why saying that an act is within God's decree does not mean that God selected the act on the servant's behalf. God's existential will gives the act its existence. It does not supply the servant's determination toward obedience or sin.

That determination belongs to the servant.

Much of the remaining disagreement lives inside one word. The objection can use decree to mean an earlier divine determination, one that fixes which choice the servant will make before he makes it. The Merv report, asked directly what God's qada concerning human acts means, answers with the judgment of reward or punishment that they deserve.

The report never defines qada as God choosing the servant's act before the servant chooses it. When the fixing sense is built into the word decree, and that word is then used to prove the fixing sense, the conclusion has been placed inside the starting point. The reports give the word a different content, and the argument stands on their definition.

Two kinds of dependence now have to be held apart. The servant depends on God for everything, existence, power, reason, desires, opportunity, and continued existence during the act itself. That is metaphysical dependence.

The servant does not depend on an antecedent divine selection of A over B. The reports deny that determinative dependence. The decree contains the servant's choosing because the servant chooses, and the servant chooses through power God sustains.

God's eternal knowledge is a further question, and the next section takes it up on its own terms.

The decree therefore includes real human causation. It does not erase it. With the decree in its place, the last question can be asked in its strongest form.

11. What Finally Explains Why We Choose A Rather Than B?

Source. Dirasat fi Ilm al-Usul, vol.1, p.153

Speaker. the school of al-Khoei, 15th century AH

The strongest form of the question is concrete. Suppose a person desires both A and B. Everything about him comes ultimately from God, his existence, his personality, his desires, his circumstances, his intelligence, and his power.

At the final moment he settles on A and never on B. If something outside him determined the answer, he would not be free in any meaningful sense, and if nothing determined it, the choice would be random.

The Imami answer is that the person himself is a genuine cause. A free agent does not need another external cause that chooses on his behalf. His reasons, desires, habits, and character influence his deliberation, and influence is not identical to compulsion.

The act occurs because the agent chooses it for reasons. If another cause necessarily determined the choice after all the deliberation had occurred, that other cause would become the real chooser, and the search for a chooser would begin again behind it.

The doctrine therefore terminates the explanation in the acting person. Why did he choose A? Because, after weighing his reasons and his desires, he chose A.

The shape of that answer sounds strange at first, and the strangeness is deliberate. Every further why hunts for a chooser behind the chooser, and the hunt ends only when it rests in a being who can settle questions, and a person is that kind of being.

The answer also does not say that nothing caused the choice. The person caused it.

Philosophers call this position agent causation, and the definition is short. Agent causation means that a person himself can be the cause of a choice. The person is a real cause, and prior causes never produce the choice through him as an inevitable result.

Nor does this make the person independent of God. The person who caused the choice exists, thinks, desires, and acts only because God gives him existence and power at every moment. The choice does not have an external cause that chooses it for the person. But the chooser himself has a cause, and that cause is God. That is the key philosophical answer, and it applies the vertical structure to one single act.

The determinist's reply is that the refusal at the next hour was itself the victory of a stronger desire, and that nothing was ever weighed at all. That reply can be restated of every act, and it cannot be refuted from inside experience alone, because it re-describes the experience and does not deny it. The school does not pretend otherwise.

What the reports establish is the structure the re-description erases. A desire that operates through weighing can be warned, argued with, and resisted, and the parable, the warnings, and the two wills all treat it so. A cause that bypasses the person cannot be warned at all.

The re-description also carries a cost. It makes the commands, the warnings, and the reproaches address a screen, while the Qur'an itself attributes the believing, the disbelieving, the purifying, and the corrupting to the person. The school rests where its sources rest, in a choosing agent held in being by God, and it leaves the determinist's one extra assumption exactly where it was, an assumption, never a finding.

God's knowledge raises one more question. God has always known that this person would choose A, and what God knows cannot fail to happen, so it can look as though B was never really open. Knowledge stays outside the causal chain, and the point about B has its own answer.

Knowing that a person will choose A is different from choosing A for him. Knowledge is not a cause, and the certainty of the known event does not identify God as the agent who chooses it.

Two statements now have to be held apart. Necessarily, if God knows that the servant chooses A, the servant chooses A, and that follows from infallibility. It is a different claim that the servant necessarily chooses A because God knows it, and the second claim does not follow from the first.

So the first concession is made plainly. Given that God eternally knows A, A is certain and cannot turn out false. B occurs only in a world where the servant chooses B, and in that world God eternally knows B. In neither world is God mistaken or surprised.

Nothing changes in God through time. God eternally knows the servant as freely choosing A, and His knowledge neither precedes the choice as a coercive cause nor follows it as newly acquired information. The temporal act is present to His eternal knowledge as the act of that servant.

The objection takes one more turn. If the servant's choosing settles which act exists, does the creature not determine something about God Himself, so that God becomes a scribe who only records what the creature authors?

He does not. The servant determines which created act exists, and God's eternal knowledge infallibly comprehends that act without receiving information from it. The chooser, his power, his act of choosing, and the existence of the resulting act depend on God continuously. What never comes from God is an antecedent selection of A for the servant.

The critic can still say that something about reality, A and never B, originates in the creature and is not selected by God. Yes, if originating means that the creature is the immediate determining cause of A. No, if it means the creature holds a power or a realm outside God. The dilemma assumes that whatever God does not select must be independent of God, and the doctrine denies exactly that assumption.

The commentator already quoted on the parable answers the deeper form of the question in one line.

Show the original Arabic

وأجاب عنه بأنَّ العلم تابع للمعلوم لا علّة له ،

أنّ علمه الأزلي بالحوادث لا ينافي حدوثها ولا يقتضي قدمها

أنّه تعالى شأنه عالم اليوم بالشيء الّذي يوجد غداً وذلك الشيء حاضر عنده اليوم (2) كما أنه حاضر عنده غداً لا لأنّه موجودٌ اليوم بل لأنّه تعالى لمّا لم يكن زمانيّاً

"He answered that knowledge follows the known, and is not a cause of it.

God's eternal knowledge of temporal things does not contradict their coming to be, and it does not imply that they are eternal.

He knows today the thing that will exist tomorrow, and that thing is present with Him today and present with Him tomorrow, not because it exists today, but because He is not a being inside time."

- al-Mazandarani (d.1081 AH), Sharh Usul al-Kafi, vol.3, p.252

The maxim separates certainty from agency. God's knowledge accounts for the certainty of A. It does not account for A as God's act, because God eternally knows A precisely as the servant's act, never as an act He performs in the servant's place.

The objection moves from two truths to a third it never earns. God knows A. God causes the servant and his power to choose. The conclusion it wants is that God chooses A for the servant, and knowledge is not the proof of that third claim.

The school of al-Khoei grounds the distinction between a tremor and a deliberate movement in direct experience, against the Ashari picture of the act.

أما الأشاعرة فيرد عليهم : أولا : انا بالوجدان نفرِّق بين حركة يد المرتعش وغيرها من الحركات الاختيارية ، وبين حركة النبض وتحريك الإصبع اختيارا ، والوجدان أعظم البراهين

"Against the Asharis it is replied, first, that in direct experience we distinguish between the movement of the trembling hand and other chosen movements, and between the pulse and the deliberate lifting of a finger. Direct experience is the greatest of proofs."

- the school of al-Khoei, Dirasat fi Ilm al-Usul, vol.1, p.153

A trembling hand moves, and the person does not choose the movement. The same hand can deliberately reach for a glass. Everyone experiences these two movements differently, because one happens to him while the other proceeds from him as an intentional act.

The difference matters philosophically, and the reason can be said in one sentence. In the tremor there is no choosing at all, and in the reach the choosing is the very event we are trying to explain. A theory that treats both movements identically has erased the agent, and every person can observe the difference in himself between one heartbeat and one deliberate gesture.

The same page of Dirasat fi Ilm al-Usul sets the Ashari position beside it, and the contrast is instructive.

"On that view, his choice has no effect at all, and the servant is a surface and a location for the act, like the body on which blackness rests.

The only difference is that the creation of the act in the human being accompanies his power, unlike the creation of blackness in the body."

- the school of al-Khoei, Dirasat fi Ilm al-Usul, vol.1, p.153

That picture is the neuroscientist's picture, a human surface on which acts are placed from outside, with the timing arranged to coincide with an inert ability. The Imami school denies it, and the denial has now been established from the parable, the definitions, the five conditions, the ownership of ability, and the verses.

Section conclusion, the answer in three layers

The choice of A over B is made by the servant's own enabled choosing, and nothing outside his choosing makes it.

The choosing self, with its reason, power, desires, time, and occasion, exists from God instant by instant, so nothing escapes the dominion.

The relation between the two layers is vertical, so they never compete.

The third answer of the opening dilemma said that a choice with no external decider must be random, and that does not follow. A choice made for reasons, by a person who weighed them, is the opposite of random. Randomness is what happens when no one settles the question, and here the person settles it.

The Argument in Brief

The argument against the doctrine

Either the decree eternally fixed the choosing, and then the servant could never have done otherwise.

Or the choosing itself fixes part of what the decree contains, and then the creature adds a fact God did not write.

Or nothing settles the choice at all, and then it is random, and a random act is no one's own.

The school rejects all three conclusions. The fork itself is answered by rejecting the assumption hidden inside it, that God's causation and the servant's causation must compete for one seat.

Premise 1: The Imams Named and Rejected Both Extremes

The formula of Imam Jaʿfar al-Ṣādiq ﵇ denies compulsion and delegation in one line, and his parable of the warned sinner explains the denial. Imam al-Rida ﵇ defined both errors at Merv. Compulsion says that God performs our acts and then punishes us for them.

Delegation says that God handed the affairs of creation and provision to a created being. The lexicons confirm both senses, and the school's verdicts attach to the positions as defined, never to the labels. Sections 2 through 4 carry this premise.

Premise 2: Everything the Servant Acts With Is Received

Existence, power, reason, time, opportunity, and motive are all given, and the letter of the five conditions counts them as conditions of responsibility. None of them settles what the servant does with them. The servant possesses power from God, never beside God, and the ownership formula of Imam Ali ﵇ states the same layered ownership for ability itself. Sections 5 and 6 carry this premise.

Premise 3: Sustaining an Act Is Not Selecting Its Sin

God sustains the sinner's body, mind, power, and moment, and He declared Himself clear of the idolaters' act itself. The sin, as sin, traces to the servant, who alone chooses the moral direction in which God-given power is used. Nothing occurs in the kingdom against the will that grants existence.

Section 7 carries this premise.

Premise 4: The Qur'an Places the Choice With the Human Being

God shows the way and shows both roads, and the walking belongs to the servant. Belief and disbelief are commanded to the human will directly. God forms the soul and teaches it its wickedness and its duty, and the purifying and the corrupting belong to the person.

His changing of a people's condition waits on their own inner change. The decree verses, read in their own order, state the warnings refused before the heart is sealed and the deed owned before the fair-seeming. Section 8 carries this premise.

Premise 5: God Causes the Chooser, and the Servant Causes the Choice

The two causes stand on different levels and never compete. God creates and sustains a real choosing agent, the servant chooses, and the whole event falls within God's judgment and sovereignty. The decree is not an additional cause inserted before the human choice, and the report that defines it gives the word the content of judgment, never manufacture. Sections 9 and 10 carry this premise.

Premise 6: The Explanation Ends in the Person

The person himself is the cause of the choice. Reasons and desires influence deliberation without necessitating its outcome. Knowledge follows the known and produces nothing, and the counterfactual holds, since had the servant chosen B, God would have known B.

The choice has no external cause that makes it, and the chooser has a cause, God. Section 11 carries this premise.

Conclusion: Dependence Is Not Compulsion

God gives the servant existence, power, ability, circumstance, and the continuing capacity to act, and the servant determines how that power is used. What settles A, and not B, is the servant's own choosing, held in being by God at every instant. Nothing stands behind the servant pushing his will toward A.

The same act therefore depends completely upon God for its existence while remaining genuinely the servant's own choice. Giving a person the power to choose is not the same thing as choosing on his behalf. God causes the servant to be a determiner, and He does not determine the determination for him. That is the matter between two extremes, and the sections establish it from the reports, the lexicons, and the Qur'an.

Conclusion: Why Human Responsibility Remains Real

The Imams ﵈ reject two extremes, and they reject both by name. God does not force the servant to choose and then punish him for a choice God Himself made. Nor does God give the servant an independent power existing outside His sovereignty.

Compulsion is the first error, delegation is the second, and the school condemned them both in the strongest terms more than twelve centuries ago.

Instead, God creates and continually sustains the human being as a real agent. He gives him reason. He gives him power. He gives him time. He gives him opportunity. He gives him the ability to deliberate and to act.

He guides him toward good and warns him against evil. Within all of those God-given conditions, the servant genuinely chooses.

That is why the same act can depend completely upon God for its existence while belonging genuinely to the servant as a moral choice. The servant is never independent of God. Dependence is real at every instant, and dependence is not compulsion.

God gives the power, and the servant determines how he uses it. That is the meaning of the formula, there is no compulsion and no delegation, but a matter between two extremes.

The philosophical question then stands before the doctrine one final time. What explains why A happens while B never does? The servant chooses A.

Where did the servant come from, with his ability, his reasons, and his power to choose? From God. Did God therefore choose A for him?

No. Giving a person the power to choose is not the same thing as choosing on his behalf. This is the distinction on which the entire doctrine rests.
