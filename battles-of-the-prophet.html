The Chronology

The Prophet Muhammad ﷺ led twenty-seven expeditions in person. They ran from the second year after the Hijra to the ninth. Real fighting happened on nine fields. The rest ended without a battle. Some were marches, and some were sieges that ended in surrender. Two were pilgrimages that a hostile Mecca nearly turned into war.

The dates count the years after the Hijra, the Prophet's ﷺ migration from Mecca to Medina, and use the month names of the Arab calendar.

Muhammad ibn Umar al-Waqidi wrote in Baghdad and died in the year 207 of the Hijra. His al-Maghazi is the earliest full chronicle of these expeditions to survive, and he closes his ordered list with a count. The hadith critics among the Sunnis class him as weak in transmission. The series uses him for the order and the frame, and every page weighs what the Shia chroniclers add.

فكانت مغازي النبي ﵌ التي غزا بنفسه سبعاً وعشرين غزوة . وكان ما قاتل فيها تسعاً : بدر القتال وأحد والمريسيع والخندق وقريظة وخيبر والفتح وحنين والطائف . وكانت السرايا سبعاً وأربعين سرية واعتمر ثلاث عمر

"The Prophet's expeditions that he led himself numbered twenty-seven. He fought in nine of them. These were Badr al-Qital, Uhud, al-Muraysi', the Trench, Qurayza, Khaybar, the Conquest, Hunayn, and al-Ta'if. The detachments numbered forty-seven, and he performed the lesser pilgrimage three times."

- al-Waqidi, al-Maghazi, vol.1, p.11, the closing count of the ordered list

The nine named fields are Badr, Uhud, al-Muraysi', the Khandaq, Qurayza, Khaybar, the Conquest of Mecca, Hunayn, and al-Ta'if. His twenty-seven expeditions include the march to al-Hudaybiyya. He counts the lesser pilgrimages apart, three in all. The series adds the return pilgrimage of the next year, one of those three, as its twenty-eighth part, because the two tell one story.

The order and the dates follow al-Waqidi's list. Where the Shia chroniclers disagree with him on a date or a detail, the page for that expedition carries the disagreement and weighs it.

A linked expedition name leads to its page. Pages for the remaining expeditions are being added in order. Each takes its place here as it lands, so the reader can walk the whole arc from the first march to the last.

What Every Page Records

  • Battle name: the name the sources use, with its source.
  • Nature: defensive or offensive, with the reason for the label.
  • Commander: who commanded, and what role he held.
  • Context: what led to the expedition, and what followed it.
  • Major figures: the named actors on both sides.
  • Companions: who fought, and who is reported to have fled.
  • The killed: who died, and how each death moved the outcome.
  • Outcome: the tactical result and the strategic result, kept apart.
  • Prevention: whether the fighting could have been avoided, and what attempts the sources record before it.
  • Justification: a closing section weighing the expedition against the alliances and enmities of its time.

Every claim on a page grounds in the sources. Quotes appear in Arabic exactly as the sources carry them, each with a citation and a translation. Where the record holds only a thin line about an expedition, the page says so plainly and adds nothing.

The Series

The Sunni spine stands on five works. Al-Waqidi's al-Maghazi comes first. Ibn Hisham's al-Sira al-Nabawiyya stands beside it, with al-Suhayli's Rawd al-Unaf. Al-Tabari's Ta'rikh al-Rusul wa-l-Muluk and al-Mas'udi's Muruj al-Dhahab close the line.

The Shia spine holds four works. Al-Qadi Nu'man's Sharh al-Akhbar leads them. Al-Majlisi's Bihar al-Anwar, volumes 19 to 21, stands next. Al-Qummi's Muntaha al-Amal and al-'Amili's al-Sahih min Sira al-Nabi al-A'zam follow. Each page cites what it actually uses.
