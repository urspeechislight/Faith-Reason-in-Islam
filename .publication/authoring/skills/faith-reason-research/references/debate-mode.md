# Debate mode

The full pressure-test method behind core sections 7 and 8.

## 1. Reconstruct the opponent

Three levels, distinguished explicitly where they differ:

1. **the popular polemical form**: what circulates in debates and
   tracts;
2. **the strongest classical or scholarly form**: the version the
   opponent's own authorities actually teach;
3. **a sophisticated reply to the obvious objection**: the move a
   trained opponent makes when the article's first answer lands.

Rules:

- Use the opponent's own primary or authoritative sources for what they
  believe: the church father, council, creed, confession, or scripture;
  the madhhab, creed text, or named scholar. Finding aids (search
  tools, indexes, aggregator sites) locate sources but never become the
  cited authority.
- Do not attribute one denomination's doctrine to the entire religion.
  A charge built on Ash'ari kalam is a claim about Ash'arism; one built
  on a Calvinist confession is a claim about that confession.
- When the opponent's argument attacks a doctrine our school does not
  hold, make that scope error explicit before answering the broader
  philosophical issue underneath it. Say plainly: this is the doctrine
  of X, the school of Ahl al-Bayt holds otherwise, and here is the
  answer to the doctrine as its holders state it.
- Formalize the strongest case as numbered premises and a conclusion,
  then answer that construction. Quote the premises from the opponent's
  own texts where possible.

## 2. The answer order

1. **Ground the contested language** (see `research-framework.md`,
   lexical grounding): settle what the Arabic, Hebrew, Greek, Latin, or
   English actually says before any doctrine is compared.
2. **Build the school's reading in authority order**: Qur'an, riwa'i
   tafsir, hadith, early scholars, later scholarship.
3. **State what the opponent actually believes**, from their sources,
   at their strongest form.
4. **Weigh comparatively**, when the opponent holds a doctrine on the
   same topic, on four criteria and only these four, argued in both
   directions:
   - **ethical**: what does each view ask a believer to accept about
     God?
   - **rational**: is each view coherent with its own scripture?
   - **textual fidelity**: which view is consistent with the verse's
     and the sources' own words?
   - **economy**: which view demands less of the text?

   The comparison weighs what steps 1-3 established; it invents no new
   evidence, and it argues without ridicule or irony. A canon that
   presses a test against our reading must be shown to face the same
   test in its own pages where it genuinely does.

## 3. The stress tests

Reconstruct each load-bearing inference fairly first, in the opponent's
steel-manned form. Only then test it. Use a named fallacy only when the
label is technically correct; otherwise call the problem what it is.

- **ambiguous middle / equivocation**: a term shifts sense between
  premises.
- **hidden or missing premise**: the inference requires an unstated
  claim; name the claim and ask whether the opponent holds it.
- **invalid inference / non sequitur**: the conclusion does not follow
  from the premises as stated.
- **scope shift**: the premises establish less than the conclusion
  asserts (Ash'arism to Islam, one tafsir to the school).
- **category mistake**: a property of one kind is predicated of
  another.
- **circularity or imported premise**: the conclusion, or an audience
  premise the opponent does not share, does load-bearing work in the
  argument.
- **inconsistency with another accepted premise**: the opponent's own
  scriptures or creed commit them elsewhere to what this inference
  denies.
- **counterexample**: a case satisfying the premises but not the
  conclusion.
- **over-generation**: the test the opponent presses also defeats their
  own position; run it against their canon and report where it fires.
- **reductio consequence**: accepting the premise forces a result the
  opponent rejects.
- **causal-role-to-essence inference**: "X causes, decrees, or uses Y"
  is read as "X is essentially the author of Y" without the connecting
  premise.
- **modal confusion**: necessity, certainty, foreknowledge, decree,
  ability, and compulsion treated as interchangeable; foreknowledge
  read as cause, decree read as removal of ability.
- **conflation of capacity, knowledge, intention, will, act, and
  effect**: each pair is a distinct modal and causal relation; the
  argument must say which one it means.
- **burden shifting**: demanding proof from the wrong side, or treating
  an unanswered question as an established failure.

## 4. The four-part test

For every debate test, give exactly four things:

1. **the inference being tested**, quoted or formalized;
2. **the problem**, in one or two sentences;
3. **the question to ask the opponent**, phrased so a yes or no
   commits them;
4. **what the opponent must establish to continue**.

## 5. Branches

Build branches for likely replies. After each test, tell the reader
where to go when the opponent:

- answers **yes**: the commitment they have taken on, and the next
  test it exposes;
- answers **no**: which premise of their case the denial gives up;
- **introduces a new distinction**: whether it is legitimate, and if
  so what the article's claim narrows to, and if not, why it fails.

A strong debate section is a tree the reader can walk, not a wall of
separate refutations.

## 6. Escape routes and the close

Before handoff, identify the three strongest escape routes an informed
opponent could take: an unaddressed distinction, a scope problem, a
missing premise. Either answer each inside the article or narrow the
conclusion so it no longer defeats the article.

The close concludes only as broadly as the premises justify, and
separates "this argument fails" from "the opposing doctrine is false".
If the article has shown the former, it says exactly that, and says
what would be needed for the latter.
