Hadith Analysis

Do Not Marry al-Hasan: A Fabricated Narration

Chain criticism, arithmetic evidence, and the principle of al-rushd fi khilafihim applied to the "mutallaq" report

The Argument Under Examination

Background

A report found in Ibn Sa'd's Tabaqat (d. 230 AH) claims that Imam Ali ﵇ stood on the minbar in Kufa and told the people not to marry their daughters to his son al-Hasan ﵇, calling him a mutallaq (frequent divorcer). The report circulated widely in Sunni biographical literature and the wife count inflated from 6 to 70 to 300 across subsequent compilers. The narration also appears in Shia primary collections, including al-Kafi, which complicates any simple dismissal.

Premise 1: The Chain Passes Through al-Waqidi

Every variant of the narration in Ibn Sa'd passes through Muhammad b. Umar al-Waqidi (d. 207 AH), graded by Sunni consensus as a liar, fabricator, and abandoned narrator.

سكتوا عنه وتركه أحمد وابن نمير

Sakatu 'anhu wa-taraka-hu Ahmad wa-Ibn Numayr.

"They remained silent about him; Ahmad and Ibn Numayr abandoned him."

- Al-Bukhari, grading al-Waqidi, cited in Ibn 'Asakir, Tarikh Madinat Dimashq, Vol. 54

Premise 2: The Numbers Are Arithmetically Impossible

The inflated wife counts (70, 90, 250, 300) would have produced hundreds of children. Genealogical sources record only 15. Ibn Sa'd's own biography names 6 wives and 4 concubine-mothers.

Premise 3: A Methodological Principle Applies

The principle of al-rushd fi khilafihim (right guidance lies in opposing them), established in multiple Shia narrations from Imam al-Sadiq ﵇ and Imam al-Rida ﵇, directs maximum suspicion toward reports that are popular among the Mukhalifeen and damaging to the Ahl al-Bayt ﵈.

Conclusion: Fabricated on Multiple Independent Grounds

The narration fails on six independent counts: chain deficiency (al-Waqidi), unknown narrators, arithmetic impossibility, argument from silence, theological implausibility, and contradiction with documented character evidence.

The report appears in Ibn Sa'd's Tabaqat as a cluster of three related narrations. Every variant passes through a single narrator whose reliability was destroyed by the greatest Sunni hadith critics.

The Three Variants in Ibn Sa'd

Introduction

Ibn Sa'd groups three reports together in his biography of Imam al-Hasan ﵇. The first records Ali's ﵇ concern about tribal enmity. The second contains the public prohibition. The third characterizes al-Hasan ﵇ as a frequent divorcer whom every wife loved.

The Public Prohibition (Hadith 111)

The core narration reads:

يا أهل الكوفة ، لا تزوجوا الحسن بن علي فإنه رجل مطلاق فقال رجل من همدان : والله لنزوجنه ، فما رضي أمسك وما كره طلق

Ya ahl al-Kufa, la tazawwiju al-Hasan b. Ali fa-innahu rajul mutallaq. Fa-qala rajul min Hamdan: wallahi la-nuzawwijannahu, fa-ma radiya amsaka wa-ma kariha tallaqa.

"O people of Kufa, do not marry [your daughters to] al-Hasan b. Ali, for he is a man who divorces frequently." A man from Hamdan said: "By God, we will marry [our daughters] to him. What pleases him he keeps, and what he dislikes he divorces."

- Ibn Sa'd, Tabaqat, Tarjamat al-Hasan, hadith 111 (ed. al-Tabataba'i, Qum)
The Tribal Concern (Hadith 110)

A parallel report frames the concern in political terms:

ما زال الحسن بن علي يتزوج ويطلق حتى خشيت أن يكون يورثنا عداوة في القبائل

Ma zala al-Hasan b. Ali yatazawwaju wa-yutalliqu hatta khashitu an yakuna yuwarithuna 'adawatan fi al-qaba'il.

"Al-Hasan b. Ali kept marrying and divorcing until I feared it would bring us enmity among the tribes."

- Ibn Sa'd, Tabaqat, Tarjamat al-Hasan, hadith 110
Conclusion

All three variants share the same chain: Ibn Sa'd from Muhammad b. Umar [al-Waqidi] from Hatim b. Isma'il from Ja'far b. Muhammad [al-Sadiq] ﵇ from his father [al-Baqir] ﵇ from Ali ﵇. The sole source is al-Waqidi.

Al-Waqidi: Unanimously Rejected

Introduction

Muhammad b. Umar al-Waqidi (d. 207 AH) was a prolific historian and qadi of Baghdad. His erudition was never in question. His truthfulness was demolished by every major Sunni hadith critic.

Ahmad b. Hanbal

Ahmad b. Hanbal, the founder of the Hanbali school, called al-Waqidi a liar and accused him of fabricating hadith:

هو كذاب

Huwa kadhdhab.

"He is a liar."

- Ahmad b. Hanbal, cited in Ibn 'Asakir, Tarikh Madinat Dimashq, Vol. 54
Al-Shafi'i

Al-Shafi'i, founder of the Shafi'i school, dismissed al-Waqidi's entire corpus:

كتب الواقدي كذب

Kutub al-Waqidi kadhb.

"Al-Waqidi's books are lies."

- Al-Shafi'i, cited in Ibn 'Asakir, Tarikh Madinat Dimashq, Vol. 54
Yahya b. Ma'in

Yahya b. Ma'in, one of the greatest hadith critics, gave two devastating verdicts:

الواقدي ليس بشئ

Al-Waqidi laysa bi-shay'.

"Al-Waqidi is nothing."

- Yahya b. Ma'in, cited in Ibn 'Asakir, Tarikh Madinat Dimashq, Vol. 54

Yahya b. Ma'in also estimated that al-Waqidi forged 20,000 hadiths in the name of the Messenger of Allah ﷺ. Muslim b. al-Hajjaj (compiler of Sahih Muslim) graded him "abandoned in hadith" (matruk al-hadith). Abu Hatim al-Razi said "not trustworthy" (laysa bi-thiqa).

Conclusion

The grades are unanimous: matruk (abandoned), kadhdhab (liar), muttaham (accused of fabrication). No Sunni scholar of hadith accepts al-Waqidi as a reliable narrator. He was famous for fabricating chains and inserting himself into narrations he never heard.

The Second Chain: Unknown Narrators

Introduction

The third variant (hadith 112) uses a different chain: al-Waqidi from Ali b. Umar from his father from Ali b. al-Husayn ﵇. The problem is that Ali b. Umar and his father are completely unknown.

Al-Dhahabi's Verdict

The Qom-era editorial footnote in Ibn Sa'd's text cites al-Dhahabi's Mizan al-I'tidal (3/148) on this narrator:

علي بن عمر الدمشقي ، عن أبيه ، وعنه بقية ، لا يدرى من هو؟!

Ali b. Umar al-Dimashqi, 'an abihi, wa-'anhu Buqayya, la yudra man huwa?!

"Ali b. Umar al-Dimashqi, from his father, from him Bukayr. Nobody knows who he is!"

- Al-Dhahabi, Mizan al-I'tidal, 3/148, cited in Ibn Sa'd, editorial footnote (87)
Conclusion

Both Ali b. Umar and his father are majhul (unknown). They appear nowhere else in hadith literature. A narration whose chain contains a fabricator (al-Waqidi) followed by two unknown narrators cannot serve as evidence for any claim.

A defining feature of this fabrication is the progressive inflation of al-Hasan's ﵇ wife count across sources. What began as a report of several marriages grew to absurd proportions.

The Escalation: 6 to 300

Introduction

Al-Qarashi's Hayat al-Imam al-Hasan traces how the wife count escalated across sources. Each generation of compilers added to the number.

The Escalation Table

The progression is as follows:

  • 6 to 10 wives: Ibn Sa'd's own biographical section. Al-Mada'ini (in Ibn Abi al-Hadid 16/21). This is the historical baseline.
  • 70 wives: Al-Mada'ini (d. 225 AH), a narrator accused of lying (muttaham bil-kidhb). Transmitted via Ibn Abi al-Hadid.
  • 90 wives: Ibn Sa'd marginal note. Astarabadi, al-Barahin al-Qati'a. Escalation.
  • 250 wives: Abu Talib al-Makki, Qut al-Qulub. Further escalation.
  • 300 wives: Al-Maliki, al-Fusul al-Muhimma, citing the escalation chain. Absurd.
The Sources of Fabrication

Al-Hasani (Sira al-A'imma) identifies three principal sources: Ali b. Abd Allah al-Mada'ini (d. 225 AH), al-Shiblanji, and Abu Talib al-Makki (Qut al-Qulub). Al-Dhahabi identified Awanah b. al-Hakam, one of al-Mada'ini's key informants, as a known Umayyad propagandist.

كان يضع الأخبار لبني أمية

Kana yada'u al-akhbara li-Bani Umayya.

"He used to fabricate reports for the Umayyads."

- Al-Dhahabi on Awanah b. al-Hakam, cited in al-Hasani, Sira al-A'imma, Vol. 1
The Political Origin

Al-Maliki (al-Fusul al-Muhimma) traces the fabrication to its political origin:

إن هذه الأباطيل قد افتعلها المنصور الدوانيقي وأخذها عنه المؤرّخون

Inna hadhihi al-abatila qad ifta'ala-ha al-Mansur al-Dawaniqi wa-akhadha-ha 'anhu al-mu'arrikhun.

"These fabrications were invented by [Caliph] al-Mansur al-Dawaniqi, and the historians took them from him."

- Al-Maliki, al-Fusul al-Muhimma, Vol. 2

The Arithmetic Refutation

Introduction

The Qom editorial on Ibn Sa'd's text makes the simplest and most devastating argument.

The Arithmetic

Ibn Sa'd's own biography, in the opening section listing al-Hasan's ﵇ children, names only 6 free wives and 4 concubine-mothers, producing 15 children (5 sons and 10 daughters). This is entirely normal for a man of his era and social standing, comparable to his father Ali ﵇, who had a similar number of wives and children.

The Qom Editorial

The footnote on Ibn Sa'd's text states:

فلو كان أحصن سبعين امرأة أو تسعين لكان أولاده يعدون بالمئات

Fa-law kana ahsana sab'ina imra'atan aw tis'ina la-kana awladuhu ya'udduna bil-mi'at.

"If he had really married seventy or ninety women, his children would number in the hundreds."

- Ibn Sa'd, Tarjamat al-Hasan, editorial footnote (87) by Sayyid Abd al-Aziz al-Tabataba'i

The genealogical record (Ibn Habib's al-Muhabbar, d. 245 AH) lists only three sons-in-law for al-Hasan ﵇. The claim of 300 wives producing 15 children requires a biological impossibility.

Conclusion

If 70, 90, or 300 wives were real, there would be hundreds of descendants recorded across the genealogical literature. There are 15. The numbers are fabricated.

Shia scholars across centuries have rejected this narration on multiple grounds. The arguments range from chain criticism to the argument from silence to character evidence.

The Argument from Silence

Introduction

The Qom editorial on Ibn Sa'd's text raises perhaps the most telling historical point. If al-Hasan ﵇ had truly been marrying and divorcing at the rate of 70 to 300 wives, Mu'awiya, who actively sought al-Hasan's faults, never used it as ammunition.

The Silence

The editorial states:

فطيلة حياته لم نر معاوية ولا واحدا من زبانيته عاب الحسن بذلك ولا بكته بشئ من هذا القبيل وهو الذي كان يتسقط عثرات الحسن فلم يجد فيه ما يشينه

Fa-tilata hayatihi ma ra'ayna Mu'awiya wala wahidan min zubaniyyatihi abba al-Hasana bidhalika wala bakkata-hu bi-shay' min hadha al-qabil wa-huwa alladhi kana yatasqitu athrati al-Hasan fa-lam yajid fihi ma yushinuhu.

"During his entire life, we never saw Mu'awiya or any of his henchmen reproach al-Hasan for this, even though Mu'awiya was actively seeking out al-Hasan's faults and found nothing to shame him with."

- Ibn Sa'd, Tarjamat al-Hasan, editorial footnote (87)

The Damascus court, which employed professional poets and propagandists against Ali's ﵇ family, never raised this charge during al-Hasan's ﵇ lifetime. The stories appeared only decades later.

Conclusion

An enemy who scoured al-Hasan's ﵇ life for faults and found nothing to exploit. If the marriages had been excessive, Mu'awiya would have used the charge. He did not, because the charge did not exist.

Character Evidence

Introduction

Multiple independent descriptions of al-Hasan's ﵇ character directly contradict the "mutallaq" image.

Ibn Sa'd's Own Testimony

Ibn Sa'd's biography, the same text that preserves the "mutallaq" narration, also records:

كان الحسن حليما ، كريما ، ورعا ، ذا سكينة ووقار وحشمة ، ما سمعت منه كلمة فحش قط

Kana al-Hasan haliman, kariman, wara'an, dha sakina wa-waqar wa-hashama, ma sami'tu minhu kalimata fahshin qatt.

"Al-Hasan was forbearing, generous, pious, possessing serenity, dignity, and gravitas. No one ever heard an obscene word from him."

- Ibn Sa'd, Tabaqat, Tarjamat al-Hasan, hadiths 1-25
The Documented Divorces

The historical record documents only three divorces. One was to Umm Kulthum. One was to a woman from Banu Shayban. One was to the Asadiyya, whom al-Hasan ﵇ took back after she said "a small consolation from a departing lover" (mata' qalil min habib mufariq). A story of that emotional depth contradicts the image of a careless divorcer.

Generosity Toward Divorced Wives

Ibn Sa'd records that al-Hasan ﵇ sent each divorced wife generous financial gifts: 10,000 dirhams and containers of honey as a parting gift (mut'a). This is the behavior of a man who honored the women he parted with, not one who discarded them carelessly.

Conclusion

Every independent description of al-Hasan ﵇ emphasizes forbearance, generosity, and piety. The few documented divorces show emotional depth and generous treatment of former wives. The "mutallaq" label contradicts the entire character record.

Scholarly Rejections

Karim (al-Rawd al-Nadhir)

Calls the narration one of "the blatant lies and fabrications" (al-akadhib wa-l-maftirat al-sariha) attributed to Imam Ali ﵇. He argues that whatever multiple marriages occurred were driven by legitimate shar'i circumstances, and that hostile historians inflated the numbers.

Al-Qarashi (Hayat al-Imam al-Hasan)

Frames the report as a deliberate smear campaign:

والصقوا بسيرته الوضاءة انه كان كثير الزواج، والطلاق... والسبب في ذلك كله هو الحط من شأنه، والتقليل من أهميته

Wa-al-saqu bisiratihi al-wadda'i'i annahu kana kathira al-zawaj, wa-l-talaq... wa-al-sabab fi dhalika kullihi huwa al-hattu min sha'nihi, wa-al-taqilul min ahammiyyatihi.

"They smeared his radiant biography with the claim that he was a frequent marrier and divorcer. The whole purpose was to diminish his stature and importance."

- Al-Qarashi, Hayat al-Imam al-Hasan, Vol. 1, p. 1431

He notes that Taha Husayn, the modern Egyptian literateur, uncritically propagated these reports "as established facts" without investigating the chain.

Al-Hasani (Sira al-A'imma)

Identifies the political context of the fabrication:

المدائني والواقدي وغيرهما من المؤرخين القدامى قد كتبوا التاريخ في ظل الحكومات التي كانت تناهض أهل البيت

Al-Mada'ini wa-l-Waqidi wa-gharuhuma min al-mu'arrikhin al-qudama' qad katabu al-tarikha fi zill al-hukumat allati kanat tunahidu Ahl al-Bayt.

"Al-Mada'ini, al-Waqidi, and other early historians wrote history in the shadow of governments that were hostile to the Ahl al-Bayt."

- Al-Hasani, Sira al-A'imma, Vol. 1
Conclusion

Across centuries, Shia scholars have identified this narration as a political fabrication. The fabrication served to diminish al-Hasan's ﵇ stature after his caliphate, justify the Umayyad takeover, undermine the concept of isma (infallibility), and counteract the popularity of his peace treaty.

The narration appears in al-Kafi itself, the most authoritative Shia hadith collection. Shia scholars cannot dismiss it as a Sunni fabrication. Their responses range from arithmetic refutation to chain criticism to the application of methodological principles.

The Narration in al-Kafi

Introduction

Al-Kulayni (d. 329 AH) preserves two versions of the narration in al-Kafi, Vol. 6, within the chapter on divorce. A third version appears in al-Barqi's al-Mahasin.

Chain 1: Ibn Sinan from al-Sadiq

The first chain runs through Abd Allah b. Sinan from Imam al-Sadiq ﵇:

إن عليا صلوات الله عليه قال وهو على المنبر : لا تزوجوا الحسن فإنه رجل مطلاق ، فقام رجل من همدان فقال : بلى والله لنزوجنه ، وهو ابن رسول الله وابن أمير المؤمنين فإن شاء أمسك وإن شاء طلق

Inna Aliyyan salawatu Allahi alayhi qala wa-huwa ala al-minbar: la tazawwiju al-Hasan fa-innahu rajul mutallaq, fa-qama rajul min Hamdan fa-qala: bala wallahi la-nuzawwijannahu, wa-huwa ibn rasuli Allah wa-ibn amir al-mu'minin fa-in shaa amsaka wa-in shaa tallaqa.

"Ali, peace be upon him, said while on the minbar: Do not marry al-Hasan, for he is a man who divorces frequently. A man from Hamdan stood and said: By God, we will marry him. He is the son of the Messenger of Allah and the son of the Commander of the Faithful. If he wishes he keeps, and if he wishes he divorces."

- Al-Kulayni, al-Kafi, Vol. 6, p. 54 (Kitab al-Talaq)
Chain 2: Ibn Faddal from al-Sadiq

The second chain adds the claim of fifty divorces:

إن الحسن بن علي طلق خمسين امرأة ، فقال علي بالكوفة فقال : يا معشر أهل الكوفة لا تنكحوا الحسن فإنه رجل مطلاق

Inna al-Hasan b. Ali tallaqa khamshina imra'ah, fa-qala Ali bil-Kufah fa-qala: ya ma'shara ahli al-Kufah la tankihu al-Hasan fa-innahu rajul mutallaq.

"Al-Hasan b. Ali divorced fifty women. Ali said in Kufa: O people of Kufa, do not marry al-Hasan, for he is a man who divorces frequently."

- Al-Kulayni, al-Kafi, Vol. 6, p. 56
Chain 3: The Consultation (al-Mahasin)

Al-Barqi preserves a version where Ali ﵇ gives private advice instead of a public proclamation:

أتى رجل أمير المؤمنين عليا فقال له : جئتك مستشيرا إن الحسن والحسين وعبد الله بن جعفر خطبوا إلي فقال أمير المؤمنين : المستشار مؤتمن . أما الحسن فإنه مطلاق للنساء ، ولكن زوجها الحسين ، فإنه خير لابنتك

Ata rajul amir al-mu'minin Aliyyan fa-qala lahu: ji'tuka mustashiran inna al-Hasan wa-l-Husayn wa-Abd Allah b. Ja'far khatabu ilayya fa-qala amir al-mu'minin: al-mustashar mu'atthaman. Amma al-Hasan fa-innahu mutallaq lil-nisa', wa-lakin zawwijha al-Husayn fa-innahu khayr li-bintik.

"A man came to the Commander of the Faithful, Ali, and said: I have come to you for consultation. Al-Hasan, al-Husayn, and Abd Allah b. Ja'far have all proposed to me. The Commander of the Faithful said: The one consulted is entrusted. As for al-Hasan, he is a frequent divorcer of women. But marry her to al-Husayn, for that is better for your daughter."

- Al-Barqi, al-Mahasin, p. 601
Conclusion

These narrations were reproduced in Bihar al-Anwar (volumes 43 and 44) by al-Majlisi and in Jami Ahadith al-Shi'a (volume 22) by al-Burujirdi. The presence of the report in Shia primary collections means the rejection must come through other means.

Majlisi's Refutation

Introduction

Al-Majlisi provides the most detailed rebuttal within the Shia tradition in his footnote to Bihar al-Anwar, Vol. 44. He traces the inflated count to Abu Talib al-Makki, a weak narrator.

The Weakness of Abu Talib al-Makki

Al-Majlisi identifies the origin of the 250/300 count:

اشتهر عنه أنه تزوج ثلاث مائة امرأة ، والأصل في ذلك ما ذكره أبو طالب المكي في قوت القلوب كما نقله ابن شهرآشوب فأرسله المؤرخون إرسال المسلمات ونقلوا ذلك في كتبهم بلا تثبت وتحقيق ، مع كون الرجل ضعيف الرواية ، ليس بثبت ولا ثقة

Ishtahara anhu annahu tazawwaja thalath mi'ah imra'ah, wa-l-asl fi dhalika ma dhakara-hu Abu Talib al-Makki fi Qut al-Qulub kama naqala-hu Ibn Shahr Ashub fa-arsala-hu al-mu'arrikhun irsal al-musallamat wa-naqalu dhalika fi kutubihim bala tathabbut wa-tahqiq, ma'a kawn al-rajul da'if al-riwaya, laysa bi-thabat wa-la thiqa.

"It became famous about him that he married 300 women. The origin of that is what Abu Talib al-Makki mentioned in Qut al-Qulub, as transmitted by Ibn Shahr Ashub. The historians then circulated it as established fact without investigation or verification, despite the man being weak in narration, neither reliable nor trustworthy."

- Al-Majlisi, Bihar al-Anwar, Vol. 44, footnote on hadith 27
The Arithmetic Argument

Al-Majlisi then makes the arithmetic argument. If al-Hasan ﵇ had married 250 or 300 women, there would necessarily be more than 200 offspring at minimum. He estimates the real number at between 20 to 30 women and argues the "mutallaq" label arose because al-Hasan ﵇ was never seen with more than four free wives at a time, so he had to divorce one to marry another. Since this was unusual for his time, he became known as a mutallaq, and then the general public exaggerated it wildly.

Conclusion

Majlisi's position: the Kafi narrations have a factual basis (al-Hasan ﵇ married and divorced more than usual because of the four-wife limit), but the inflated numbers are fabricated by a weak narrator and circulated without verification.

Al-Muhsini's Assessment

Introduction

Al-Muhsini takes a notably different approach from Majlisi. He considers the Kafi chains reliable (mu'tabar) and accepts a factual basis for the report.

His Position

He considers the 250/300 count "obvious exaggeration" but accepts that al-Hasan ﵇ married and divorced more than usual. His position is that the narrations have a correct origin in the aggregate. The core of the report (that Ali ﵇ expressed concern) may reflect a real historical moment, but the numbers were wildly inflated by later compilers.

Conclusion

The diversity of scholarly responses to the Kafi narrations shows that Shia scholars do not dismiss the report reflexively. They engage with it through chain criticism, arithmetic evidence, and methodological principles, reaching conclusions that range from partial acceptance of a factual core to outright rejection of the entire report.

A well-established principle in Shia hadith methodology holds that when a report is popular among the Mukhalifeen and serves an anti-Ahl al-Bayt agenda, the truth lies in its opposite. The principle is called al-rushd fi khilafihim: right guidance lies in opposing them.

The Maqbula of Umar b. Hanzala

Introduction

The foundational source is the Maqbula of Umar b. Hanzala, recorded in al-Kafi (Vol. 1, p. 67) and al-Tusi's Tahdhib (Vol. 6, p. 301). It is called "maqbula" because the Shia scholarly community received it with acceptance (qubul).

The Core Instruction

Imam al-Sadiq ﵇ gave a hierarchy of tie-breakers for conflicting reports. When all other criteria are exhausted, the final criterion is:

ما خالف العامة ففيه الرشاد

Ma khalafa al-amma fa-fihi al-rushd.

"What contradicts the general public, in it is right guidance."

- Imam al-Sadiq, Maqbula of Umar b. Hanzala, al-Kafi, Vol. 1, p. 67

The Imam ﵇ further instructed: if both reports agree with the al-amma, look to what their judges and rulers prefer, leave that, and take the other. If both agree with their judges, then wait until you meet your Imam, "for standing at the threshold of doubt is better than plunging into destruction."

Conclusion

The Maqbula establishes a hierarchy: first the Book and the Sunna, then scholarly consensus, then narrator reliability, then opposition to the al-amma as a final tie-breaker.

Parallel Narrations

Introduction

The principle appears in multiple independent narrations from Imam al-Sadiq ﵇ and Imam al-Rida ﵇ across different Shia collections.

The Marfua of Zurara

Ibn Abi Jumhur al-Ahsa'i transmits in Ghawali al-La'ali from al-Allama, raised to Zurara, who asked Imam al-Baqir ﵇ about conflicting hadiths:

انظر ما وافق منهما العامة فاتركه وخذ بما خالف فان الحق فيما خالفهم

Uzur ma wafaqa minhuma al-amma fa-udrukuhu wa-khudh bi-ma khalafa fa-inna al-haqqa fi ma khalafahum.

"Look at what agrees with the general public, leave it, and take what contradicts them. For the truth lies in what contradicts them."

- Imam al-Baqir, Marfua of Zurara, Ghawali al-La'ali (via al-Allama)
Imam al-Rida's Instruction

Ibn Babawayh records in Uyun al-Akhbar a striking version from Imam al-Rida ﵇:

ايت قاضي البلد فما أفتاك بشئ فخذ بخلافه فان الرشد في خلافه

I'ti qadi al-balad fa-ma aftaka bi-shay' fa-khudh bi-khilafihi fa-inna al-rushda fi khilafihi.

"Go to the qadi of the town. Whatever he gives you a fatwa on, take the opposite, for right guidance lies in his opposite."

- Imam al-Rida, Ibn Babawayh, Uyun al-Akhbar, Vol. 2, p. 20
The Rawandi Narration

Al-Qutb al-Rawandi transmits from Imam al-Sadiq ﵇ the most explicit version:

إذا ورد عليكم حديثان مختلفان فاعرضوهما على كتاب الله ، فما وافق كتاب الله فخذوه وما خالف كتاب الله فردوه وان لم تجدوهما في كتاب الله فاعرضوهما على أخبار العامة ، فما وافق أخبارهم فذروه وما خالف أخبارهم فخذوه

Idha warada alaykum hadithani mukhtalifani fa-ariduhuma ala kitabi Allah, fa-ma wafaqa kitaba Allah fa-khudhuhu wa-ma khalafa kitaba Allah fa-rudduhu wa-in lam tajiduhuma fi kitabi Allah fa-ariduhuma ala akhbari al-amma, fa-ma wafaqa akhbarahum fa-dhuruha wa-ma khalafa akhbarahum fa-khudhuhu.

"If two conflicting hadiths come to you, present them to the Book of Allah. What agrees with the Book of Allah, take it. What contradicts the Book of Allah, reject it. If you do not find them in the Book of Allah, then present them to the reports of the general public. What agrees with their reports, discard it. What contradicts their reports, take it."

- Imam al-Sadiq, al-Qutb al-Rawandi, cited in Wasa'il al-Shi'a, Vol. 18, p. 84
Conclusion

Six independent narrations from Imam al-Sadiq ﵇ and Imam al-Rida ﵇ establish the same principle across different Shia collections. The principle is not a blanket rejection of all Sunni reports. It is a murajjih (tie-breaker) used when other criteria are exhausted.

Application to the Mutallaq Narration

Introduction

The application of this principle to the "mutallaq" narration is one of the clearest cases imaginable.

Four Converging Grounds

The report satisfies every condition for maximum suspicion under the principle:

  • The "mutallaq" label is overwhelmingly popular in the Sunni corpus. It appears in Ibn Sa'd, Ibn Asakir, Ibn Abi al-Hadid, Abu Talib al-Makki, al-Shiblanji, and virtually every Sunni biographical source.
  • It serves the anti-Ahl al-Bayt ﵈ narrative by diminishing al-Hasan ﵇, the Prophet's ﷺ grandson whom he called "sayyid shabab ahl al-janna" (master of the youth of Paradise).
  • The Sunni version has a weak chain (al-Waqidi) and arithmetic problems (300 wives with no corresponding children).
  • There is an argument from silence: Mu'awiya never raised the charge during al-Hasan's ﵇ lifetime.
Conclusion

By the principle of al-rushd fi khilafihim, the opposite position should be preferred: al-Hasan ﵇ was a man of exemplary character whose marriages were few and motivated by legitimate reasons. The principle provides an additional independent ground for rejection, on top of the chain, arithmetic, and historical arguments.

Scholarly Debate About the Principle

Introduction

The principle itself is not uncontested within Shia scholarship. Several major scholars have debated its scope, its chain reliability, and its proper application.

Al-Khurāsānī (Kifayat al-Usul)

Al-Khurāsānī analyzes four possible interpretations of why contradicting the amma is a valid murajjih: (a) it is a ta'abbudi instruction that must be followed without understanding the reason, (b) it reflects the intrinsic goodness of opposing them, (c) truth is ghalib (predominant) in what contradicts them, or (d) reports that agree with them are suspected of being taqiyya-motivated distortions. The fourth interpretation is the most widely adopted: reports aligning with Sunni positions may have been distorted under taqiyya or political pressure.

Al-Khu'i

Al-Khu'i discusses the principle extensively and treats it as a valid tie-breaker. He cautions against dogmatic application, noting that the principle operates within the specific framework of resolving conflicting reports, not as a standalone method for evaluating individual narrations.

Al-Isfahani (contemporary)

Al-Isfahani argues that the principle should be applied narrowly. He makes the critical observation that "not a single narration of 'take what contradicts the amma' has a sound chain" (lim tasihh ay riwaya min riwayat al-akhdh bi-ma khalafa al-amma sanadan). Even al-Rawandi's narration, which al-Sadr graded as sahih, is disputed. Despite this, the principle remains widely accepted in Shia jurisprudence as a valid murajjih.

Conclusion

Despite ongoing debate about the principle's chain and scope, its application to the "mutallaq" narration remains compelling. The convergence of five independent grounds (chain weakness, arithmetic impossibility, argument from silence, character evidence, and the al-rushd fi khilafihim principle) makes this one of the strongest cases for rejection in the hadith literature.

Glossary of Key Terms

Mutallaq: A man who divorces frequently. The term comes from the Arabic root t-l-q, meaning to release or set free. In the context of this narration, it carries a pejorative sense of someone who marries and discards women carelessly.

Al-Waqidi: Muhammad b. Umar al-Waqidi (d. 207 AH), a prolific historian and qadi of Baghdad. Unanimously rejected as a hadith narrator by Sunni critics (Ahmad b. Hanbal, al-Shafi'i, Yahya b. Ma'in, Muslim, al-Bukhari) for fabricating chains and forging hadiths.

Matruk: "Abandoned." A technical grade in hadith criticism meaning that scholars have unanimously abandoned narrating from this person due to established fabrications or persistent lying.

Majhul: "Unknown." A narrator who appears in only one or two chains and about whom no biographical information exists. Reports from majhul narrators are not accepted as evidence.

Murajjih: A "preponderant" or tie-breaker. In Shia hadith methodology, a criterion used to prefer one report over another when they conflict. The principle of al-rushd fi khilafihim is one such murajjih.

Al-rushd fi khilafihim: "Right guidance lies in opposing them." A principle established in multiple Shia narrations directing that when a report is popular among the Mukhalifeen (the general Sunni public), the truth is more likely found in its opposite.

Al-amma: "The general public." In Shia hadith terminology, this refers to the Sunni majority. The term appears throughout the narrations establishing the al-rushd fi khilafihim principle.

Maqbula: "Accepted." A narration that the Shia scholarly community received with unanimous acceptance. The Maqbula of Umar b. Hanzala is the foundational source for the hierarchy of tie-breakers in Shia hadith methodology.

Taqiyya: Religious dissimulation under threat. One interpretation of the al-rushd fi khilafihim principle is that reports agreeing with Sunni positions may have been issued under taqiyya and thus do not reflect the true teaching of the Imams ﵈.

Final Verdict

The "do not marry al-Hasan, he is a mutallaq" narration is fabricated on six independent grounds: the chain passes through al-Waqidi (a unanimous liar by Sunni consensus), the second chain contains unknown narrators, the inflated wife counts (70 to 300) are arithmetically impossible given the 15 documented children, Mu'awiya never raised the charge during al-Hasan's ﵇ lifetime, the report contradicts every independent description of al-Hasan's ﵇ character, and the principle of al-rushd fi khilafihim directs maximum suspicion toward reports that are popular among the Mukhalifeen and damaging to the Ahl al-Bayt ﵈. The narration is a product of the anti-Ahl al-Bayt ﵈ historiographical school that operated under Umayyad and early Abbasid patronage.
