The War of Forts

Khaybar was the fortress of the men who had bought the Trench. The exiled clan of al-Nadir held it alongside the other Jewish tribes of the oasis, and when the Muslim army marched in the opening the truce had made, they found the ground already sold. Kinana ibn Abi al-Haqiq had gone to Ghatafan with fourteen riders, offering half of Khaybar's produce for a year to whoever came to destroy Medina's army. The campaign that followed was a war of forts, and its decisive hour came at the citadel gate, in a duel.▼ commentary▼ commentary

"The Jews used to stand to arms every night before dawn. Kinana ibn Abi al-Haqiq went out to Ghatafan with fourteen men, calling them to aid them, and theirs would be half the produce of Khaybar for a year. When the Messenger of Allah camped on their ground, they stirred that night no more, and no rooster crowed for them until the sun rose. They woke with their hearts pounding, opened their forts, and went out to their work with the hoes and the baskets and the panniers, and when they saw the Muslims they cried, 'Muhammad and the army,' and ran back into their forts, while the Messenger of Allah said, 'Allah is greater. Khaybar is ruined. When we alight in the courtyard of a people, evil is the morning of the warned.' He fought the men of al-Nata that day until night, and at night he moved the people to al-Rajic. He would go out with the Muslims under their banners, and their cry was, 'Mansur, strike them down.' He ordered the cutting of their palms, and the Muslims fell to it until they had cut four hundred clusters, and then he called out forbidding it. It is related that when the Messenger of Allah came down at Khaybar the migraine took him, and he did not go out to the people."

- al-Maqrizi, Imta' al-Asma', vol.1, p.353

Commentary

The Sources for Khaybar

Source spine: al-Maqrizi, Imta' al-Asma', vol.1, p.353

Shia lines: al-'Amili, al-Sahih min Sira al-Nabi al-A'zam, vol.17, p.294, on the duel. Al-Mar'ashi, Sharh Ihqaq al-Haq, vol.8, p.400, collecting the Sunni attestations

Poetry: the Haydara rajaz, carried by both communities, with al-Nawawi's note from the two Sahihs, from Salama ibn al-Akwa

Both lines carry the same frame. They agree on the Ghatafan bribe, the terror of the morning the army arrived, the fighting at al-Nata, the palms ordered cut and then spared, and the duel that broke the citadel.

The Duel at the Citadel

The challenger: Marhab the Khaybari, the fortress champion, out with his rajaz

The answer: Ali ﵇, answered verse with verse and blow with blow

The blow: Dhu l-Fiqar split stone, helmet, and head to the teeth, and the camp heard it

The result: the stronghold taken, and the fort line broken

Read the two poets' exchange as the armies heard it. Marhab's verses boasted strength. The answer Ali ﵇ gave was his mother's name for him, Haydara, the lion, and a merchant's image turned to war, paying the full measure with the sword. The sources of both communities carry the verses. Al-Mar'ashi's page is a catalogue of Sunni masters, Ibn Manzur, al-Nawawi, Ibn Abi l-Hadid, Ibn al-Athir, each on record that the lines are Ali's ﵇. The blow the whole camp heard closed the campaign's decisive gate. No companion is named as fleeing, and the Prophet's ﷺ migraine, kept in the reports, explains why the banner passed forward while he lay in the camp.

What the Campaign Left

Tactical: the fortress line taken, the citadel by the duel, the forts surrendered one by one

Strategic: the financial and military base of the exiled instigators destroyed, and the northern road to Medina closed

Restraint on record: the palm cutting ordered and then forbidden, four hundred clusters and no more

The palm order is the campaign's own measure of conduct. The trees were the enemy's wealth and the land's future at once. The order to stop, kept by the reports of the campaign, chose the land's future. The strategic outcome completed what the Trench had begun, the coalition's paymaster gone. The road lay open for the pilgrimage of the next year and, in the second year after, the entry into Mecca itself.

Was the Campaign of Khaybar Justified

The cause is the shortest ledger in this series. The men who had bought the ten thousand man confederacy against Medina were living armed in the forts of Khaybar. While the trenches were being dug they were bribing the desert tribes again, half the produce of a year to whoever would come. A state that leaves that base standing invites the next coalition before the truce with Mecca runs out.

The conduct matched the cause. The forts were fought and the families left outside them. The palms were spared once the point was made. The settlement that ended the campaign left the cultivators on their land, working it, and the sources of both communities record the arrangement for the years after. The duel that decided the citadel was a single combat between champions, the most contained form of decision that age knew.

Prevention belonged to the exiled instigators, in the fortress they could have left, the bribe to Ghatafan they could have withheld, and the coalition they could have stopped financing. The truce with Mecca made the northern war possible, and the men who made the northern war necessary had spent two years earning it.
