Exegetical Analysis

Why the Prophet Forbade What God Made Lawful

Quran 66:1 as oath, self-denial, and release: the evidence from the lexicons, the poets, and the hadith of the Ahl al-Bayt ﵈

The Thesis

The claim: at Quran 66:1 the Prophet Muhammad ﷺ committed no sin and legislated nothing against God's permission. He forbade Marya the Copt to himself under oath. The classical lexicons show the verse's two verbs as tying and untying, and the pre-Islamic poets use the same grammar for a man barring a lawful thing from his own reach. The Shia hadith classify the Prophet's ﷺ act as an oath: the expiation fell on the oath, and nothing at all fell on the forbidding. God had already ordained the release of oaths, and the Prophet ﷺ took it.

Background

Surat al-Tahrim opens with a question addressed to the Prophet ﷺ: why do you forbid what God has made lawful to you, seeking to please your wives? Readers have heard two accusations inside the question: that the Prophet ﷺ legislated a prohibition God never issued, or that he acted against God's permission and sinned. The Shia hadith sources give the episode a different spine. On their account the Prophet ﷺ barred one lawful thing from himself under oath, God treated the act as an oath, and the release God had already legislated for oaths settled the matter within two verses.

Premise 1: The Verbs Track the Person, and the Thing Stays Lawful

Halal and haram in Arabic mark a person's access to a thing. The woman of Antara's couplet is lawful to one man and forbidden to another in the same breath, and the couplet reads as plain sense. Quran 66:1 uses the same relational grammar: what God loosed for you, and what you barred.

لِمَ تُحَرِّمُ مَا أَحَلَّ اللَّهُ لَكَ تَبْتَغِي مَرْضَاةَ أَزْوَاجِكَ وَاللَّهُ غَفُورٌ رَحِيمٌ (1)

Lima tuḥarrimu mā aḥalla llāhu laka, tabtaghī marḍāta azwājik? Wa-llāhu ghafūrun raḥīm.

"O Prophet: why do you forbid what God has made lawful to you, seeking to please your wives? And God is forgiving, merciful."

- The Qur'an, al-Tahrim 66:1

Premise 2: The Verse Pair Is Tying and Untying

The root h-r-m carries the image of a cord drawn tight; the root h-l-l carries the image of a knot released. The taḥillat al-aymān of 66:2, the release of oaths, is the untying root's own settled term for undoing a sworn knot. The sura takes its name from the first verb and answers with the second.

Premise 3: The Forbidding of a Man Binds Only Himself

In the hadith of the school, a husband's declaration that his wife is forbidden to him accomplishes nothing in law. Imam al-Baqir ﵇ calls it, where it presumes to recast God's ruling, a lie. The Prophet's ﷺ act carried no such presumption: he barred Marya from himself and swore an oath, and the expiation fell on the oath alone.

Premise 4: The Blame of the Sura Falls Elsewhere

The sura demands repentance from two wives who divulged a confidence, and it closes its opening address over the Prophet ﷺ with the words and God is forgiving, merciful. The question of 66:1 asks about motive, and what the sura supplies in answer is a release, a charge against the schemers, and mercy.

Conclusion: An Oath Taken and Released, With No Sin and No Legislation

The Prophet ﷺ abstained from a lawful thing under oath, revelation declined to let the abstention stand as precedent, and the ordinance God had already revealed for oaths closed the episode. The sin the objection alleges would require either a false claim about God's law or an act of disobedience. The sources show neither: the lexicon and the poets fix the grammar of the verbs, the hadith fix the classification of the act, and the scholars of the school state the verdict directly.

The dispute turns on two verbs: tuḥarrimu, you forbid, from the root h-r-m, and aḥalla, God made lawful, from h-l-l. The classical lexicons give the anatomy of both roots, and the pre-Islamic poets show them alive in ordinary speech, long before any creed had a stake in this verse. The poetry matters because the poets defended no doctrine of prophethood. They show what the words meant to an Arab ear on their own, and one couplet of Antara carries the exact grammar of the verse.

The Root That Bars: Prevention and Tight Binding

Introduction

Ibn Faris organizes his lexicon by roots, and for each root he posits one underlying sense from which every derived word descends. For h-r-m that sense is prevention. His entry in the Maqayis al-Lugha:

الحاء والراء والميم أصل واحد وهو المنع والتشديد

Al-ḥāʾ wa-al-rāʾ wa-al-mīm: aṣlun wāḥidun, wa-huwa al-manʿu wa-al-tashdīd.

"Ha, ra, mim is a single root, and it is prevention and tight binding."

- Ibn Faris (d.395 AH), Maqayis al-Lugha, Vol. 2, p. 45
Example

Ibn Sayyidih fixes the legal use of the root as the plain opposite of the lawful:

الحِرْمُ والحَرامُ : نَقيضُ الحَلالِ

Al-ḥirmu wa-al-ḥarāmu: naqīḍu al-ḥalāl.

"Al-hirm and al-haram are the opposite of al-halal."

- Ibn Sayyidih (d.458 AH), al-Muhkam wa-l-Muhit al-A'zam, Vol. 3, p. 326
Implication

Ibn Faris strips away every religious accretion the root had acquired by the fourth century and records the bare image underneath. The image is physical: a cord drawn tight, a gate shut, a hand closed. Whatever else the root comes to name, a month, a city, a person, a morsel of food, the constant in every use is blocked access. One feature of the definition carries the whole verse: the verb names the act of barring, and it is silent on the right to bar. Authority is foreign to the word itself. Arabic asks the authority question with other words entirely, and the Quran asks it only when it means to accuse, as the next tab shows. Notice also what Ibn Sayyidih chose as the opposite of the barred: a word about loosening. The forbidden and the lawful are, in this vocabulary, the shut and the open.

Conclusion

h-r-m names blocked access and the act of blocking; the right to block lies entirely outside the word.

The Sacred Precinct and the Profane Ring

Introduction

The oldest organized lexicon in Arabic, the Kitab al-Ayn of al-Khalil ibn Ahmad, defines the haram as geography. His entry:

Example

الحرم : حرم مكة وما أحاط بها إلى قريب من المواقيت التي يحرمون منها ، مفصول بين الحل والحرم بمنى

Al-ḥaramu: ḥaramu Makkata wa-mā aḥāṭa bihā ilā qarībin mina al-mawāqīti allatī yuḥrimūna minhā, maqṣūlun bayna al-ḥilli wa-al-ḥarami bi-Minā.

"The haram is the sanctuary of Mecca and what surrounds it, up to near the stations at which they enter ihram. A boundary separates the hill and the haram at Mina."

- Al-Khalil ibn Ahmad (d.175 AH), Kitab al-Ayn, Vol. 3, p. 221
Implication

Al-Khalil's map makes the root pair into two sides of one boundary. The land inside is the haram, the barred and guarded zone. The ring of ordinary land around it is the hill, from the loosed root. An Arab of the Hijaz learned halal and haram as terrain before he met them as legal abstractions: he crossed a marked line, entered a state called ihram, from the same barring root, and moved through zones named by the two roots of this verse. The same family names the relatives a marriage can never reach, the maharim, and the protected folk under a tribe's covenant. Across the whole family the direction of value is consistent: to be haram is to be guarded, inviolable, held safe. The root carries no intrinsic sense of wrong or filth. Any reproach attached to a use of it must arrive from the context, and the context of 66:1 supplies a question about motive and a close of mercy.

Conclusion

The barring root names sanctuaries, protected persons, and sacred time as naturally as it names the forbidden morsel; sanctity is its native register.

The Root That Unties: The Slackened Cord

Introduction

The second root of the verse runs the opposite physics. Al-Maydani preserves its image inside a proverb about a traveler and his baggage:

Example

والحل بمعنى الحلول يقال حل بالمكان يحل حلا وحلولا ومحلا وأصله في الرجل يشد حمله فيسرف في الاستيثاق حتى يضر ذلك به وبراحلته عند الحلول

Wa-al-ḥullu bi-maʿnā al-ḥulūl: yuqālu ḥalla bi-al-makāni yaḥillu ḥallan wa-ḥulūlan wa-maḥillan, wa-aṣluhu fī al-rajuli yashuddu ḥamilahu fa-yusrifu fī al-istīthāqi ḥattā yuḍirra dhālika bihi wa-bi-rāḥilatihi ʿinda al-ḥulūl.

"And al-hull in the sense of alighting: one says halla bi-l-makan, yahullu, halan and hululan and mahillan. Its origin is the man who straps his baggage so excessively tight that it harms him and his mount when he dismounts."

- Al-Maydani (d.518 AH), Majma' al-Amthal, Vol. 2, p. 377

The proverb wears the root's meaning plain: over-binding harms the binder, and hull is the relief of the cord drawn too tight. From the image of slackening comes the verb of arrival. Ibn Manzur records it:

حَلَّ بالمكان يَحُلُّ حُلولًا ومَحَلًّا وحَلًّا وحَلَلًا ، بفك التضعيف نادر : وذلك نزول القوم بمَحَلَّة وهو نقيض الارتحال

Ḥalla bi-al-makāni yaḥullu ḥulūlan wa-maḥallan wa-ḥallan wa-ḥalalan, bi-fakki al-tashdīdi nādirun: wa-dhālika nuzūlu al-qawmi bi-maḥallatin wa-huwa naqīḍu al-irtiḥāl.

"Halla bi-l-makan, yahullu: alighting, a stopping place, and the rare doubled form. It means a people alighting at an encampment, and it is the opposite of decamping."

- Ibn Manzur (d.711 AH), Lisan al-Arab, Vol. 11, p. 163
Implication

Behind the gloss stands the caravan. The loads come off the animals, the hobbles come off the legs, the tent ropes go slack. The camp, the maḥalla, is named for the act of untying, and to settle, in Arabic, is to untie. The language cannot describe arrival except as release. When the Quran wants a word for a thing open to use, it takes it from this root, and the same derivation produces the verb of 66:2 that undoes an oath. The sura's opening pair of verbs is therefore one physics seen twice: a cord God left slack, and the same cord drawn shut by a hand.

Conclusion

h-l-l is the physics of the open knot, from baggage straps to campsites to permissions, and it supplies the release verb of the sura itself.

The Lawful, the Spouse, and the Oath

Introduction

Ibn Sayyidih's entry in al-Mukhassas runs the derivation chain from the lawful thing to the released oath in a single breath:

Example

الحَلَالُ ضِدُّ الحَرام وهو الحِلُّ والحَلِيلُ حَلَّ الشىءُ يَحِلُّ حِلًّا وأَحَلَّهُ اللَّهُ سبحانه واسْتَحْلَلْتُهُ - اتَّخَذْتُهُ حَلالا ومنه حَلَّلْتُ اليمينَ تَحْلِيلًا وتَحِلَّة وتَحِلًّا

Al-ḥalālu ḍiddu al-ḥarāmi wa-huwa al-ḥillu wa-al-ḥalīl. Ḥalla al-shayʾu yaḥillu ḥillan, wa-aḥallahu llāhu subḥānahu, wa-istaḥlaltuhu: ittakhadhtuhu ḥalālan. Wa-minhu ḥallaltu al-yamīna taḥlīlan wa-taḥillatan wa-taḥillan.

"Al-halal is the opposite of al-haram; it is al-hill and al-halil. A thing becomes lawful, halla; God made it lawful, ahallahu; I took it as lawful, istahlaltuhu. From this root also: I released the oath, hallaltu al-yamin, tahilan and tahillatan."

- Ibn Sayyidih (d.458 AH), al-Mukhassas, Vol. 13, p. 105

Every link matters. Ḥalla, the thing became lawful. Aḥallahu, God loosed it. Istaḥlaltuhu, I took it as lawful. And the last link: ḥallaltu al-yamīn, I released the oath, with its noun taḥilla. Al-Raghib al-Isfahani, the lexicographer of the Quran, glosses the verb of 66:1 directly:

أي : لم تحكم بتحريم ذلك ؟

Ayy: lima taḥkumu bi-taḥrīmi dhālika?

"That is: why do you pronounce the ruling of prohibition over it?"

- Al-Raghib al-Isfahani (d.425 AH), al-Mufradat fi Gharib al-Qur'an, p. 230
Implication

The taḥillat al-aymān of Quran 66:2 is this root's own settled term for undoing a sworn knot, in continuous use since before Islam. The verse that answers the rebuke of 66:1 is built from the untying word of the untying root. The everyday words for spouse belong to the same family: the ḥalīla is the lawful wife, the ḥalīl her lawful husband, the people whose knot stands open to each other. Al-Raghib's gloss fixes the register of the verb: he hears the verse as a question about the Prophet's ﷺ own declaration over a thing, a pronouncing of prohibition, which is precisely the shape the hadith give the act: a declaration, and an oath upon it. A declaration binds the declarer; it reaches no one else, and it touches the thing's standing before God not at all, because the standing belongs to the God who loosed it.

Conclusion

Read with both roots, the verse pair is one image: a knot God left open, a hand that drew it closed upon itself, and the same cord running loose again through the release of oaths.

Antara's Gazelle: One Woman, Two Verdicts

Introduction

The pre-Islamic poet Antara ibn Shaddad addressed a couplet to a woman that carries the exact grammar of Quran 66:1, centuries before the verse. It stands in his Mu'allaqa among the hunting scenes, and the classical anthologists preserved it as canonical Arabic.

Example

يا شاةَ ما قَنَصٍ لِمَنْ حَلَّتْ لهُ * حَرُمَتْ عَليّ وَلَيْتَها لم تَحْرُمِ

Yā shāta mā qaṣaṣin li-man ḥallat lahu / ḥurimat ʿalayya wa-laytahā lam taḥrum.

"You gazelle: what chase remains for the man to whom she is already lawful? She is forbidden to me, and would that she had never been."

- Antara ibn Shaddad, Mu'allaqa, in Jamharat Ash'ar al-Arab, p. 167
Implication

The woman is figured as a wild cow of the chase. The first hemistich addresses her from the standpoint of the man who lawfully has her: for him the hunt is over, since the lawful thing is the attained thing, with nothing left to pursue. The second hemistich, in the same breath, gives her the opposite standing from the poet's own: she is haram to him. Ḥallat lahu, lawful to him; ḥurimat ʿalayya, forbidden to me. Nothing in the woman changed between the two halves. The verbs record two men's access to one object, and no listener of the age took the poet for a legislator: he barred her from himself, and the line became proverbial. Now lay Quran 66:1 over the couplet. The verse says the Prophet ﷺ barred what God loosed for you, the relational form, God's loosing addressed to one man, and the same man's barring of himself. Two standings over one object: God's open knot, and the Prophet's ﷺ closed one. The question of the verse is put to the second standing only. The first standing, what God loosed, the verse treats as fixed and untroubled from beginning to end.

Conclusion

The relational grammar of the verse was ordinary Arabic before Islam: lawful to him, forbidden to me, in one couplet, with no legislator in sight.

The Protected Man and the Closed Hand

Introduction

Two further verse witnesses fix the barring root's full range, from the guarded life to the denied gift.

Example

The same Mu'allaqa, a few lines earlier, over a champion Antara has just slain:

فشَكَكْتُ بالرّمْحِ الأَصَمّ ثِيابَهُ * لَيْسَ الكَريمُ على القَنَا بِمُحَرَّمِ

Fashakkaktu bi-al-ramḥi al-aṣammi thiyābah / laysa al-karīmu ʿalā al-qanā bi-muḥarram.

"I threaded his garments with the flawless spear. Nobility earns no sanctuary against the spearpoints."

- Antara ibn Shaddad, Mu'allaqa, in Jamharat Ash'ar al-Arab, p. 167

And al-A'sha's proverb of the persistent asker:

ومَنْ يُكثِرِ التَّسآلَ لا بُدَّ يَحْرِمُ

Wa-man yukthiri al-tasʾāla lā budda yaḥrum.

"Whoever asks and asks will end denied."

- Al-A'sha, as indexed in al-Khwarizmi (d.383 AH), al-Amthal al-Muwallada, p. 596
Implication

Antara's line works only because muḥarram normally names a man beyond reach: the protected one, the sacrosanct, the person others are barred from touching. His boast is that the spear ignores that custody; nobility itself buys no immunity. Al-A'sha's line shows the root's ground floor: yaḥrumu, he ends denied, the beggar shut out of the gift. Every sense above is the same closed hand at a higher altitude: the closed gift, the closed month, the closed precinct, the closed spouse, the closed self. The honorific sense matters most for reading 66:1. The verb of the verse belongs to the family that guards sanctuaries and protected persons, and an Arab ear heard a verb of custody and access in it. Any sense of wrongdoing had to arrive from context, and the context supplies a question about motive and a close of mercy.

Conclusion

From the denied beggar to the guarded sanctuary, the barring root describes custody and access; the reproach in 66:1 rides on the question why, and on nothing inside the verb itself.

The Sacred Months and the Pilgrim's Map

Introduction

Two witnesses from the calendar and the pilgrimage place the word pair in the public furniture of pre-Islamic Arabia.

Example

The diwan of Abu Talib, the Prophet's ﷺ uncle and guardian, on the turning of the year:

وتنسى حَلِيلَة * حَلِيلاً ويَغْشى مُحَرَّمٌ بعد مُحَرَّمِ

Wa-tansā ḥalīlatun / ḥalīlan wa-yaghshā muḥarramun baʿda muḥarram.

"A wife forgets a husband, and one sacred month rolls over the next."

- Diwan Abu Talib (d.10 AH), p. 216

And Labid, the Mu'allaqa that opens on the pilgrim ground of Mina:

عَفَتِ الدّيَارُ مَحَلُّهَا فَمَقَامُهَا . . . بِمنًى تأبَّدَ غَوْلُها فَرِجَامُهَا

ʿafati al-diyāru maḥalluhā fa-maqāmuhā / bi-Minā taʾabbada ghawluhā fa-rijāmuhā.

"The campsites have faded, their halting-ground and their resting-ground alike. At Mina, Ghawl and Rijam have gone wild."

- Labid (d.41 AH), Mu'allaqa, Diwan Labid, p. 107

دِمَنٌ تَجَرَّمَ بعدَ عَهْدِ أنِيسِهَا . . . حِجَجٌ خَلَوْنَ حَلالُهَا وحَرَامُهَا

Dimanun tajarrama baʿda ʿahdi anīsihā / ḥijajun khalawna ḥalāluhā wa-ḥarāmuhā.

"Dung-worn traces, crumbled since the days of their people. Years of pilgrimage have passed over them, their lawful and their forbidden."

- Labid (d.41 AH), Mu'allaqa, Diwan Labid, p. 107
Implication

Abu Talib's line moves through a year that arrives pre-partitioned: one sacred month rolls over the next, and while they run, bloodshed is barred. The poet reports the partitions as fixed furniture of the world, the way another line reports weather; no poet issues a sacred month, and none rescinds one. Labid surveys the same order as terrain. The maḥall of his first line is the h-l-l word, the halting-ground where the loads come off, and he sets it at Mina, the pilgrim's station; Abu Ubayd cites the very line as a linguistic witness in Gharib al-Hadith. His third line then carries the pair together: ḥalāluhā wa-ḥarāmuhā, the lawful and the forbidden of the pilgrim world at Mina, its game and its zones and its seasons, which years of pilgrimage have washed over the ruins. The pair is native to the pilgrim's map, the zone where the locks come off and the zone where they hold. Measured against that public scale, the act of 66:1 sits at the opposite end: the sacred months and the sanctuary bind a whole people, while the Prophet's ﷺ act, on the hadith account, barred one bondswoman from one man, himself.

Conclusion

The poets inherited the public partitions and surveyed them; the act of the verse is a private abstention, one man, one thing, himself.

The Lawful Pair

Introduction

Al-Kumayt ibn Zayd, the poet of the Ahl al-Bayt ﵈ in the Umayyad century, supplies the marriage register of the loosed root.

Example

ولا حَلِيلَةُ جاري لستُ زاعِمَها * تَصْبو إلَيّ وساءَ الصِّدقُ والكَذِبُ

Wa-lā ḥalīlatu jārin lastu zāʿimahā / taṣbū ilayya wa-sāʾa al-ṣidqu wa-al-kadhib.

"Never my neighbor's lawful wife do I claim, though she incline toward me; good faith would sink into falsehood."

- Al-Kumayt ibn Zayd (d.126 AH), Diwan al-Kumayt, Vol. 1, p. 80
Implication

The ḥalīla of the line is the lawful wife, and the ḥalīl her lawful husband. The everyday Arabic words for spouse are built on the loosed root: spouses are the people whose knot stands open to each other. The line also shows the ethic of the register: al-Kumayt refuses the neighbor's wife though she incline to him, because claiming her would sink truth into falsehood. That is the background against which the marriage formula of the fiqh narrations operates. When a man says to his wife you are forbidden to me, he attempts by speech alone to flip the one person his language defines as open to him. The Imam's ruling, that the speech accomplishes nothing in law, is the lexical point carried into law, and it prepares the reading of the Prophet's ﷺ own words in the next tab: he did not declare Marya haram in the world's eyes; he declared that he had forbidden her to himself.

Conclusion

The marriage vocabulary itself encodes the verse's physics: the lawful bond is the loosed one, and its looseness is what makes it lawful.

The Quran sorts every act of forbidding into three registers, and it assigns the Prophet ﷺ to none of the ones that carry blame. Once the registers are clear, the hadith of the Ahl al-Bayt ﵈ fix which act the verse addressed: an oath, with its release already legislated.

The Verse and the Release That Follows It

Introduction

The sura opens on the question and answers it within one verse:

Example

لِمَ تُحَرِّمُ مَا أَحَلَّ اللَّهُ لَكَ تَبْتَغِي مَرْضَاةَ أَزْوَاجِكَ وَاللَّهُ غَفُورٌ رَحِيمٌ (1)

Lima tuḥarrimu mā aḥalla llāhu laka, tabtaghī marḍāta azwājik? Wa-llāhu ghafūrun raḥīm.

"O Prophet: why do you forbid what God has made lawful to you, seeking to please your wives? And God is forgiving, merciful."

- The Qur'an, al-Tahrim 66:1

قَدْ فَرَضَ اللَّهُ لَكُمْ تَحِلَّةَ أَيْمانِكُمْ وَاللَّهُ مَوْلاكُمْ وَهُوَ الْعَلِيمُ الْحَكِيمُ

Qad faraḍa llāhu lakum taḥillata aymānikum, wa-llāhu mawlākum, wa-huwa al-ʿalīmu al-ḥakīm.

"God has already ordained for you the release of your oaths. And God is your master; He is the knowing, the wise."

- The Qur'an, al-Tahrim 66:2
Implication

Three features of the pair govern everything that follows. First, the loosing is God's and it stays God's: aḥalla llāhu laka, what God loosed for you, a ruling the verse never withdraws and never says was threatened. Second, the remedy precedes any talk of blame. The second verse legislates nothing new; qad faraḍa points at an ordinance already in force, God has already ordained the release of oaths, and the address invites the addressee into an exit that existed before the question was asked. Third, the first verse closes with wa-llāhu ghafūrun raḥīm, God is forgiving, merciful, appended to the question itself. A charge sheet does not carry a mercy clause; a correction of a son's needless self-denial does.

Conclusion

The verse opens with a question about motive, its successor supplies an exit already legislated, and the thing loosed stays loosed throughout.

The Three Registers of Forbidding in the Quran

Introduction

The Quran uses the forbidding verb in three distinct constructions, and the construction is the verdict. God alone legislates in the first register, marked by God as subject or the passive: aḥalla, uḥilla, ḥurrima, as in 66:1 itself, 5:1, and 4:23. The second register addresses believers about their own abstentions:

Example

لَا تُحَرِّمُوا طَيِّبَاتِ مَا أَحَلَّ اللَّهُ لَكُمْ وَلَا تَعْتَدُوا إِنَّ اللَّهَ لَا يُحِبُّ الْمُعْتَدِينَ (87)

Lā tuḥarrimū ṭayyibāti mā aḥalla llāhu lakum wa-lā taʿtadū, inna llāha lā yuḥibbu al-muʿtadīn.

"O you who believe: do not forbid the good things that God has made lawful to you, and do not exceed the bounds. God does not love those who exceed."

- The Qur'an, al-Ma'ida 5:87

The third register is the accusation, aimed at people who carved God's provision into forbidden and lawful on their own authority:

فَجَعَلْتُمْ مِنْهُ حَرَامًا وَحَلَالًا قُلْ آَللَّهُ أَذِنَ لَكُمْ أَمْ عَلَى اللَّهِ تَفْتَرُونَ (59)

Fa-jaʿaltum minhu ḥarāman wa-ḥalālan. Qul: āllāhu adhina lakum am ʿalā llāhi taftirūn?

"...and you have made of it forbidden and lawful. Say: did God authorize you, or do you fabricate against God?"

- The Qur'an, Yunus 10:59
Implication

The same verb, three grammars. In 5:87 ordinary believers are told lā tuḥarrimū, do not forbid yourselves the good things God made lawful, and the harm named there is the excess: lā taʿtadū, do not transgress, and God's love withdrawn from the transgressors. The abstention weighs on the soul; the sin named is going too far. In 10:59 the question āllāhu adhina lakum, did God authorize you, defines the crime as forgery, and the charge is framed for people who legislated where God had not. Now place 66:1. Its construction matches 5:87 and stands apart from 10:59: the Prophet ﷺ is addressed as a man who barred something from himself, and the authorization question of the forgery charge is never put to him anywhere in the Book. The reading that takes 66:1 as an accusation of legislating against God must force the verse into a register its grammar refuses.

Conclusion

By construction, 66:1 sits in the register of self-denial addressed to a believer; the register of forgery belongs to a different verse and different people.

Imam al-Sadiq's ﵇ Account

Introduction

The narration-based tafsir of the school transmits a report from Imam al-Sadiq ﵇ that names the act, the oath, and the remedy in three sentences.

Example

في تفسير علي بن إبراهيم أخبرنا أحمد بن إدريس قال : حدثنا أحمد بن محمد عن الحسين بن سعيد عن ابن سنان عن أبي عبد الله ﵇ في قوله : يا أيها النبي لم تحرم ما أحل الله لك الآية قال : اطلعت عائشة وحفصة على النبي ﵌ وهو مع مارية فقال النبي ﵌ : والله ما أقر بها فأمره الله ان يكفر عن يمينه

Fī tafsīri ʿAliyyi ibni Ibrāhīma: akhbaranā Aḥmadu ibnu Idrīsa qāla: ḥaddathanā Aḥmadu ibnu Muḥammadin ʿan al-Ḥusayni ibni Saʿīdin ʿan ibni Sinānin ʿan Abī ʿAbdillāhi ʿalayhi al-salāmu fī qawlihi: yā ayyuhā al-nabiyyu lima tuḥarrimu mā aḥalla llāhu lak, al-āyatu, qāla: iṭṭalaʿat ʿāʾishatu wa-Ḥafṣatu ʿalā al-nabiyyi ṣallā llāhu ʿalayhi wa-ālihi wa-huwa maʿa Māriyata, fa-qāla al-nabiyyu: wa-llāhi mā aqrubuhā, fa-amara-hu llāhu an yukaffira ʿan yamīnih.

"In the Tafsir of Ali ibn Ibrahim: Ahmad ibn Idris informed us, saying Ahmad ibn Muhammad related to us, from al-Husayn ibn Sa'id, from Ibn Sinan, from Abu Abdillah ﵇, concerning His word 'O Prophet, why do you forbid what God has made lawful to you', the verse. He said: Aisha and Hafsa came upon the Prophet ﷺ while he was with Marya. So the Prophet ﷺ said: by God, I shall not touch her. And God commanded him to expiate his oath."

- Al-Huwayzi (d.1112 AH), Tafsir Nur al-Thaqalayn, Vol. 5, p. 367, from Tafsir Ali ibn Ibrahim
Implication

The report fixes every element the lexical analysis predicted. The thing barred is Marya the Copt, whom the Quran itself had already loosed for the Prophet ﷺ. The form of the barring is an oath: wa-llāhi mā aqrubuhā, by God, I shall not touch her, the sworn formula that opens every case of tahrim in the fiqh. And the divine response runs through the oath channel: fa-amara-hu llāhu an yukaffira ʿan yamīnih, God commanded him to expiate his oath. The account stands in Tafsir al-Qummi, and al-Majlisi carries the parallel in Bihar al-Anwar.

Conclusion

The Imam's ﵇ account reads the verse exactly as the roots demand: a self-denial under oath, met by the release of oaths.

The Occasion in Tafsir al-Qummi

Introduction

Ali ibn Ibrahim al-Qummi's tafsir preserves the fullest occasion report, and its wording settles the grammar of the Prophet's ﷺ declaration.

Example

فاستحيا رسول الله منها ، فقال كفى فقد حرمت مارية على نفسي ولا أطأها بعد هذا ابدا وأنا أفضي إليك سرا فان أنت أخبرت به فعليك لعنة الله والملائكة والناس أجمعين

Fa-istaḥyā rasūlu llāhi minhā fa-qāla: kafā, qad ḥarramtu Māriyata ʿalā nafsī wa-lā aṭʾuhā baʿda hādhā abadan, wa-anā ufḍī ilayki sirran, fa-in anti akhbarti bihi fa-ʿalayki laʿnatu llāhi wa-al-malāʾikati wa-al-nāsi ajmaʿīn.

"The Prophet of God felt shy before her, and he said: enough. I have forbidden Marya to myself, and I shall never touch her again. And I shall confide to you a secret. If you disclose it, the curse of God and the angels and all people shall be upon you."

- Ali ibn Ibrahim al-Qummi (d.329 AH), Tafsir al-Qummi, Vol. 2, p. 376

The report continues that the secret concerned the succession, that it traveled from Hafsa to Aisha and on to Abu Bakr and Umar, and that revelation followed, with Gabriel bringing the sura and its reading of the release:

فنزل جبرئيل على رسول الله ﵌ بهذه السورة (يا أيها النبي لم تحرم ما أحل الله لك - إلى قوله - تحلة ايمانكم) يعني قد أباح الله لك ان تكفر عن يمينك

Fa-nazala Jibrīʾīlu ʿalā rasūli llāhi bi-hādhihi al-sūrati: yā ayyuhā al-nabiyyu lima tuḥarrimu mā aḥalla llāhu lak, ilā qawlihi taḥillata aymānikum, yaʿnī qad abāḥa llāhu laka an tukaffira ʿan yamīnik.

"Then Gabriel descended upon the Prophet of God with this sura: 'O Prophet, why do you forbid what God has made lawful to you', to His word 'the release of your oaths', meaning: God has permitted you to expiate your oath."

- Ali ibn Ibrahim al-Qummi (d.329 AH), Tafsir al-Qummi, Vol. 2, p. 376
Implication

The sentence to weigh is qad ḥarramtu Māriyata ʿalā nafsī, I have forbidden Marya to myself. The preposition ʿalā nafsī, upon myself, is the self-directed form, the same grammar as Antara's ḥurimat ʿalayya, forbidden to me. Arabic has a form for legislating, and this is not it: no ruling is issued, no community is addressed, no claim is made about God's law. The Prophet ﷺ closes his own access and binds the closure with an oath, and the revealed reading confirms the channel: qad abāḥa llāhu laka an tukaffira ʿan yamīnik, God has permitted you to expiate your oath. The remedy named is the remedy for oaths, in the loosed root's own vocabulary.

Conclusion

The occasion report and the revelation report agree with the lexicon: the barred thing is Marya, the form is upon myself, and the divine ruling is the expiation of the oath.

The Act Classified as an Oath

Introduction

A companion report transmits the classification in the Imam's ﵇ own words, with the expiation itemized.

Example

عن أبي جعفر عليه السّلام في قوله : « تَحِلَّةَ أَيْمانِكُمْ » قال : جعلها يمينا وكفّرها رسول اللّه بأن أطعم عشرة مساكين لكلّ مسكين مدّ . قلنا : فمن وجد الكسوة ؟ قال : ثوب يواري عورته

ʿan Abī Jaʿfarin ʿalayhi al-salāmu fī qawlihi taḥillata aymānikum qāla: jaʿalahā yamīnan wa-kaffarahā rasūlu llāhi bi-an aṭʿama ʿasharata masākīna li-kulli miskīnin muddan. Qulnā: fa-man wajada al-kiswata? Qāla: thawbun yuwārī bihi ʿawratahu.

"From Abu Ja'far ﵇, concerning His word 'the release of your oaths': He made it an oath, and the Messenger of God released it by feeding ten poor persons, a mudd for each poor person. We said: and whoever finds clothing, what is its measure? He said: a garment that covers his nakedness."

- Al-Jaza'iri (d.1112 AH), Uqud al-Marjan fi Tafsir al-Qur'an, Vol. 5, p. 157, from Imam al-Baqir
Implication

Jaʿalahā yamīnan, He made it an oath. The verb jaʿala is a ruling verb: God, in the Imam's ﵇ reading, classified the act. The classification matters because oaths and legislation travel through different channels in the law. An oath binds the swearer alone, is released by an expiation, and changes nothing in the world's permissions. A legislation binds everyone and stands until repealed. The expiation is then itemized with the poor-feeding scale: ten poor persons, a mudd each. The chain, from Muhammad ibn Qays from Imam al-Baqir ﵇, runs through al-Kafi, and al-Tusi carries it in al-Istibsar, with al-Hurr al-Amili gathering the versions in Wasa'il al-Shi'a and al-Majlisi in Bihar al-Anwar.

Conclusion

God classified the act as an oath, and the expiation followed the oath channel exactly.

The Wording in al-Tusi's Amali

Introduction

The self-directed formula survives independently in a report al-Tusi delivered in his Amali, which al-Bahrani transmits in al-Burhan.

Example

وجدت حفصة رسول الله (﵌) مع أم إبراهيم في يوم عائشة ، فقالت : لأخبرنها . فقال رسول الله (﵌) : «اكتمي ذلك ، وهي علي حرام»

Wajadtu Ḥafṣatu rasūla llāhi maʿa ummi Ibrāhīma fī yawmi ʿāʾishata fa-qālat: la-ukhbirannahā. Fa-qāla rasūlu llāhi: uktumī dhālika, wa-hiya ʿalayya ḥarām.

"Hafsa found the Messenger of God with Umm Ibrahim on Aisha's day. She said: I shall surely tell her. The Messenger of God said: conceal this; she is forbidden to me."

- Al-Bahrani (d.1107 AH), al-Burhan fi Tafsir al-Qur'an, Vol. 5, p. 419, hadith 10858, from al-Tusi (d.460 AH), al-Amali
Implication

Wa-hiya ʿalayya ḥarām, and she is forbidden to me. The formula stands alone, with no ruling verb over anyone else, no legislation, no community bound. Hafsa has found the Prophet ﷺ with Umm Ibrahim, Marya's byname, on Aisha's day, and threatens to tell. The Prophet's ﷺ answer has two parts: conceal this, and she is forbidden to me. The first part is a confidence; the second is the self-barring. Two parts, exactly what the occasion reports of al-Qummi describe, arriving here through a separate chain of transmission.

Conclusion

A second independent chain lands on the same formula: forbidden to me, said of one person, by one man, about himself.

Zurara's Question and the Imam's Answer

Introduction

The central narration of the whole question is a dialogue preserved in al-Kafi. Zurara, a senior companion of Imam al-Baqir ﵇, asks about a man who declares his wife forbidden to him, and receives a ruling whose severity is itself the argument.

Example

عدة من أصحابنا ، عن سهل بن زياد ، عن ابن أبي نصر ، عن محمد بن سماعة ، عن زرارة ، عن أبي جعفر عَلَيْهِ السَّلَامُ ، قَالَ : سَأَلْتُهُ عَنْ رَجُلٍ قَالَ لِامْرَأَتِهِ : أَنْتِ عَلَيَّ حَرَامٌ ؟ فَقَالَ لِي : « لَوْ كَانَ لِي عَلَيْهِ سُلْطَانٌ لَأَوْجَعْتُ رَأْسَهُ ، وَقُلْتُ لَهُ : اللَّهُ أَحَلَّهَا لَكَ ، فَمَا حَرَّمَهَا عَلَيْكَ ؟ إِنَّهُ لَمْ يَزِدْ عَلى أَنْ كَذَبَ ، فَزَعَمَ أَنَّ مَا أَحَلَّ اللَّهُ لَهُ حَرَامٌ ، وَلَا يَدْخُلُ عَلَيْهِ طَلَاقٌ وَلَا كَفَّارَةٌ »

ʿIddatun min aṣḥābinā ʿan Sahlī ibni Ziyādin ʿan ibni Abī Naṣrin ʿan Muḥammadi ibni Simāʿata ʿan Zurārata ʿan Abī Jaʿfarin ʿalayhi al-salāmu qāla: saʾaltuhu ʿan rajulin qāla li-imraʾatihi: anti ʿalayya ḥarāmun? Fa-qāla lī: law kāna lī ʿalayhi sulṭānun la-awjaʿtu raʾsahu wa-qultu lahu: allāhu aḥallalahā laka fa-mā ḥarramahā ʿalayka? Innahu lam yazid ʿalā an kadhaba fa-zaʿama anna mā aḥalla llāhu lahu ḥarāmun, wa-lā yadkhulu ʿalayhi ṭalāqun wa-lā kaffārah.

"A number of our companions, from Sahl ibn Ziyad, from Ibn Abi Nasr, from Muhammad ibn Sima'a, from Zurara, from Abu Ja'far ﵇. He said: I asked him about a man who says to his wife: you are forbidden to me. He said to me: 'Were authority over him mine, I would bruise his head, and I would say to him: God made her lawful to you. So who forbade her to you? He has done nothing but lie, for he claims that what God made lawful to him is forbidden. No divorce enters upon it, and no expiation.'"

- Al-Kulayni (d.329 AH), al-Kafi, Vol. 6, p. 135, hadith 10973

Zurara then puts the objection at issue, that God Himself placed an expiation in the verse:

فَقُلْتُ : قَوْلُ اللَّهِ عَزَّ وَجَلَّ : « يا أَيُّهَا النَّبِيُّ لِمَ تُحَرِّمُ ما أَحَلَّ اللَّهُ لَكَ » فَجَعَلَ فِيهِ الْكَفَّارَةَ ؟ فَقَالَ : « إِنَّمَا حَرَّمَ عَلَيْهِ جَارِيَتَهُ مَارِيَةَ ، وَحَلَفَ أَنْ لَايَقْرَبَهَا ، فَإِنَّمَا جَعَلَ عَلَيْهِ الْكَفَّارَةَ فِي الْحَلْفِ ، وَلَمْ يَجْعَلْ عَلَيْهِ فِي التَّحْرِيمِ »

Fa-qultu: qawlu llāhi ʿazza wa-jalla: yā ayyuhā al-nabiyyu lima tuḥarrimu mā aḥalla llāhu lak, fa-jaʿala fīhi al-kaffārata? Fa-qāla: innamā ḥarrama ʿalayhi jāriyatahu Māriyata wa-ḥalafa an lā yaqrabahā, fa-innamā jaʿala ʿalayhi al-kaffārata fī al-ḥalaf, wa-lam yajʿal ʿalayhi fī al-taḥrīm.

"I said: then the word of God, mighty and majestic: 'O Prophet, why do you forbid what God has made lawful to you', He placed expiation in it. He said: 'He only forbade his bondswoman Marya to himself, and he swore that he would not approach her. The expiation was laid upon him only for the oath, and nothing was laid upon him for the forbidding.'"

- Al-Kulayni (d.329 AH), al-Kafi, Vol. 6, p. 135, hadith 10973
Implication

The Imam's ﵇ ruling is that the ordinary man's declaration accomplishes nothing: no divorce enters upon it, and no expiation. Its only content is a lie, fa-zaʿama anna mā aḥalla llāhu lahu ḥarāmun, he claims that what God made lawful for him is forbidden. Read the severity carefully, because it locates the sin with precision. The sin of a false tahrim is the attribution: the man asserts that God's loosed thing is barred, and that assertion is the offense. The abstention itself harms no one in law. Zurara's objection then supplies the test case, and the Imam's ﵇ answer separates the two acts the Prophet ﷺ performed. He forbade his bondswoman Marya to himself, and he swore that he would not approach her. Fa-innamā jaʿala ʿalayhi al-kaffārata fī al-ḥalaf, the expiation was laid upon him only for the oath, wa-lam yajʿal ʿalayhi fī al-taḥrīm, and nothing was laid upon him for the forbidding. The ruling carries its own implication: since the Imam ﵇ has just defined claiming as the sin, and since nothing was laid on the Prophet ﷺ for the forbidding, the Prophet's ﷺ forbidding claimed nothing about God's law. He barred a thing from his own reach, and he swore. Parallels run through the four books: al-Saduq's Man La Yahduruhu al-Faqih, al-Tusi's Tahdhib al-Ahkam, al-Hurr al-Amili's Wasa'il al-Shi'a, and al-Majlisi's Mir'at al-Uqul.

Conclusion

The sin question is answered inside the narration itself: the expiation fell on the oath alone, and the forbidding carried nothing, because the forbidding presumed nothing.

The exegetes of the school put the sin question to the verse directly and answer it in set terms. Their verdicts hold across both variant occasions of the sura, and the one flag of caution in the corpus concerns the political details of the occasion report, never its legal core.

Al-Jaza'iri: No Indication of Sin

Introduction

Nimat Allah al-Jaza'iri's Qur'an commentary states the verdict on the sin question in terms the scholars of the school repeat.

Example

ليس فيه دلالة على وقوع ذنب منه . لأنَّ تحريم الرجل بعض نسائه أو بعض الملاذّ لسبب أو لغير سبب ليس بقبيح . ولا يمتنع أن يكون خرج هذا القول مخرج التوجّع له عليه السّلام إذ بالغ على إرضاء أزواجه وتحمّل ذلك المشقّة . ولو أنَّ إنسانا أرضى بعض نسائه بتطليق بعضهنّ ، لجاز أن يقال له : لم فعلت ذلك ؟ وإن لم يكن قبيحا

Laysa fīhi dalālatun ʿalā wuqūʿi dhanbin minhu, li-anna taḥrīma al-rajuli baʿḍa nisāʾihi aw baʿḍa al-malādhdhi li-sababin aw li-ghayri sababin laysa bi-qabīḥin, wa-lā yumtaniʿu an yakūna kharaja hādhā al-qawlu makhraja al-tawajjuʿi lahu ʿalayhi al-salāmu idh bālagha ʿalā irḍāʾi azwājihi wa-taḥammala dhālika al-mashaqqata, wa-law anna insānan arḍā baʿḍa nisāʾihi bi-taṭlīqi baʿḍihinna la-jāza an yuqāla lahu: lima faʿalta dhālika wa-in lam yakun qabīḥan.

"There is in it no indication that a sin occurred from him. For a man's forbidding to himself one of his wives, or some pleasure, for a cause or without a cause, is not ugly. And it is not impossible that this saying came out in the mode of sympathy for him, peace be upon him, since he went to great lengths to please his wives and bore that hardship. If a man pleased one of his wives by divorcing another, it would be permissible to say to him: why did you do that, even though it is not ugly."

- Al-Jaza'iri (d.1112 AH), Uqud al-Marjan fi Tafsir al-Qur'an, Vol. 5, p. 157

The same passage records the variant occasions of the sura under a single oath analysis:

وإنّما أوجب اللّه فيه الكفّارة لأنّ النبيّ كان حلف أن لا يقرب جاريته أو لا يشرب الشراب المذكور

Wa-innamā awjaba llāhu fīhi al-kaffārata li-anna al-nabiyya kāna ḥalafa an lā yaqraba jāriyatahu aw lā yashraba al-sharāba al-madhkūr.

"God made the expiation obligatory in it only because the Prophet had sworn not to approach his bondswoman, or not to drink the mentioned drink."

- Al-Jaza'iri (d.1112 AH), Uqud al-Marjan fi Tafsir al-Qur'an, Vol. 5, p. 157
Implication

Three moves in the passage. First, laysa fīhi dalālatun ʿalā wuqūʿi dhanbin minhu, there is in it no indication that a sin occurred from him: a direct ruling on the question, in the negative. Second, the reason: a man's forbidding to himself one of his wives or some pleasure, for a cause or without one, is laysa bi-qabīḥ, free of ugliness. The ethical category the scholar reaches for is ugliness, the category of the deformed act, and the act fails to meet it. Third, the genre of the verse's question: it may have come forth makhraja al-tawajjuʿ, in the mode of sympathy, for a man who went to great lengths to please his wives and bore the hardship of it. His parallel makes the grammar plain: a man who pleases one wife by divorcing another may be asked why did you do that, though the act is free of ugliness. The question why interrogates motive and cost; it does not convict. And the variant occasions change nothing: whether the thing barred was Marya or a honey drink, the vow-form carries the expiation and the tahrim itself carries nothing.

Conclusion

The scholarly verdict is categorical and invariant across the variant occasions: no sin is indicated, and the expiation attaches to the oath.

Where the Blame of the Sura Falls

Introduction

The sura itself names the parties it holds at fault, and the Imam's ﵇ gloss supplies the guard it raised around the Prophet ﷺ. Verse 66:4 turns from the Prophet ﷺ to two of his wives: in tatūbā ilā llāhi fa-qad ṣaghat qulubukumā, if you two repent to God, your hearts have inclined. The threat follows: if the two of you conspire against him, then God is his master, and Gabriel, and the salih of the believers, and the angels after that, supporters.

Example

Imam al-Baqir ﵇ names the salih of the believers:

عن أبي جعفر عليه السّلام في قوله : « صالِحُ الْمُؤْمِنِينَ » قال : صالح المؤمنين هو عليّ بن أبي طالب عليه السّلام

ʿan Abī Jaʿfarin ʿalayhi al-salāmi fī qawlihi ṣāliḥu al-muʾminīna qāla: ṣāliḥu al-muʾminīna huwa ʿAliyyu ibnu Abī Ṭālibin ʿalayhi al-salām.

"From Abu Ja'far ﵇, concerning His word 'the salih of the believers': the salih of the believers is Ali ibn Abi Talib ﵇."

- Al-Jaza'iri (d.1112 AH), Uqud al-Marjan fi Tafsir al-Qur'an, Vol. 5, p. 157, from Imam al-Baqir
Implication

The repentance demanded by the sura belongs to the two wives, and the backing listed belongs to the Prophet ﷺ. On the occasion reports, the two are the ones who divulged the confidence and carried it onward, and the sura answers with an arrayed guard: God, Gabriel, Ali ﵇ named as the salih of the believers, and the angels. The structure of the passage could hardly be clearer about where fault is laid. The opening verse carries a question over a self-denial; the verses that follow carry a demand for repentance and a threat of replacement against two wives, and a backing for the Prophet ﷺ that names his support by name.

Conclusion

The repentance verse addresses the two wives; the backing verse arrays God, Gabriel, Ali ﵇, and the angels behind the Prophet ﷺ.

Al-Majlisi's Caution

Introduction

Al-Majlisi, the encyclopedist of Bihar al-Anwar, appends a caution to the occasion report he transmits, and the caution's precise target matters.

Example

وفي هذا الخبر غرائب لا نعلم حقيقتها فطوينا على غرها والله يعلم وحججه صلوات الله عليهم جهة صدورها

Wa-fī hādhā al-khabari gharāʾibu lā naʿlamu ḥaqīqatahā fa-ṭuwaynā ʿalā gharrihā, wa-llāhu yaʿlamu wa-ḥujajuhu ṣalawātu llāhi ʿalayhim jihata ṣudūrihā.

"This report contains strange things whose truth we do not know, so we have passed over them in silence. God knows, and His proofs, God's blessings upon them, know the direction of its issuance."

- Al-Majlisi (d.1111 AH), Bihar al-Anwar, Vol. 22, p. 241
Implication

Wa-fī hādhā al-khabari gharāʾib, this report contains strange things, and al-Majlisi declines to affirm their truth. The flagged elements are the political ones: the confidence about the succession, the names it traveled through, and the poisoning plot at the report's end. His caution is a scholar's hygiene about dramatic details of the occasion. The legal core of the episode is not flagged and does not depend on the flagged material: Marya, the oath, and the expiation for the oath recur across al-Kafi, al-Faqih, al-Tahdhib, and al-Istibsar, in independent chains, and the fiqh of the four books builds directly on that core. A reader may set the whole political drama aside and lose nothing of the argument, which rests on the roots, the registers, and the classification narration.

Conclusion

The caution covers the occasion's political details; the legal core recurs independently across the four books and carries the verdict by itself.

Protection at Work: The Verse and Ismah

Introduction

The doctrine of ismah, protection from sin, meets this verse head on, and on the sources' reading the verse shows the protection itself at work.

Implication

Consider the sequence the sources give. A personal abstention occurs, under oath. Revelation intervenes at once, before the abstention can settle into precedent, and the intervention takes the form of a question about motive and an exit through the release of oaths, with the expiation that is the oath's own remedy. The community thereby learns, inside the sura itself, that no vow of any man, the Prophet's ﷺ own included, can narrow what God has left open; the record publishes the episode together with its correction, and nothing is carved away. The sura closes its address over the Prophet ﷺ with forgiveness and mercy, and turns its severity on the two wives and the scheming. That is what protection looks like in action on the school's reading: an abstention corrected within two verses, a community taught the limits of vows, and mercy sealing the address.

Conclusion

The verse performs the protection it presupposes: an abstention corrected, a precedent prevented, and mercy closing the address.

Glossary of Key Terms

Tahrim: An act of forbidding, from the root h-r-m; in Quran 66:1 the barring of a lawful thing from oneself.

Halal / Haram: The loosed and the barred; lawful and forbidden, named by the roots h-l-l and h-r-m.

Hill: The open land around the sanctuary; the profane ring outside the haram of Mecca.

Tahillat al-ayman: The release of oaths, Quran 66:2; from the untying root, the settled term for undoing a sworn knot.

Ihram: The pilgrim's consecrated state, from the barring root; entered at marked stations outside Mecca.

Mu'allaqa: One of the seven golden odes of pre-Islamic Arabia, prized above ordinary verse; Antara's and Labid's are cited above.

Ismah: Protection from sin and error; the attribute the school affirms of the Prophet ﷺ and the Imams ﵈.

Mudd: A classical measure of volume, roughly three-quarters of a liter; the per-person scale of the poor-feeding expiation.

Final Verdict

At Quran 66:1 the Prophet ﷺ issued no ruling and broke none. He barred a lawful thing from himself under oath, and the God who had already ordained the release of oaths invited him into it, and he entered it, feeding ten poor persons, a mudd each. The verbs of the verse were the poets' verbs before they were the jurists': Antara's gazelle is lawful to one man and forbidden to another in the same breath, and the poets barred, guarded, and surveyed partitions without ever issuing one. The Imam's ﵇ ruling fixes that grammar in law: the tahrim of a man binds only the man, and where it presumes to recast what God loosed, the presumption is a lie. The Prophet's ﷺ forbidding presumed nothing; he abstained, he swore, and he was released. The blame of the sura falls on two wives who carried a confidence abroad, and the address that opened with a question closes over the Prophet ﷺ with forgiveness and mercy. On the school's reading the verse is the protection at work: revelation intervened the moment a private abstention threatened to read as precedent, and the lesson it published stands for everyone after him, that no vow of any man, the Prophet's ﷺ own included, can narrow what God has left open.
