Did the Prophet Forbid What God Made Lawful?

Quran 66:1 as oath, self-denial, and release: the evidence from the lexicons, the poets, and the hadith of the Ahl al-Bayt ﵈

The Thesis

The claim: at Quran 66:1 the Prophet Muhammad ﷺ committed no sin and legislated nothing against God's permission. He forbade Marya the Copt to himself under oath. The classical lexicons show the verse's two verbs as tying and untying, and the pre-Islamic poets use the same grammar for a man barring a lawful thing from his own reach. The Shia hadith classify the Prophet's ﷺ act as an oath: amends were owed for the oath alone, and nothing at all was owed for the forbidding. God had already ordained the release of oaths, and the Prophet ﷺ took it.

Overview

The Question the Sura Opens With

Surat al-Tahrim opens with a question addressed to the Prophet ﷺ: why do you forbid what God has made lawful to you, seeking to please your wives? Readers have heard two accusations inside the question: that the Prophet ﷺ legislated a prohibition God never issued, or that he acted against God's permission and sinned. The Shia hadith sources give the episode a different spine. On their account the Prophet ﷺ barred one lawful thing from himself under oath, God treated the act as an oath, and the release God had already legislated for oaths settled the matter within two verses.

Premise 1: The Verbs Track the Person, and the Thing Stays Lawful

Halal and haram in Arabic mark a person's access to a thing. The woman of Antara's couplet is lawful to one man and forbidden to another in the same breath, and the couplet reads as plain sense. Quran 66:1 uses the same relational grammar: what God made lawful for you, and what you barred from yourself.

بِسْمِ ٱللَّهِ ٱلرَّحْمَٰنِ ٱلرَّحِيمِ يَٰٓأَيُّهَا ٱلنَّبِىُّ لِمَ تُحَرِّمُ مَآ أَحَلَّ ٱللَّهُ لَكَ ۖ تَبْتَغِى مَرْضَاتَ أَزْوَٰجِكَ ۚ وَٱللَّهُ غَفُورٌۭ رَّحِيمٌۭ

Yā ayyuha al-nabiyyu: lima tuḥarrimu mā aḥalla llāhu laka, tabtaghī marḍāta azwājik? Wa-llāhu ghafūrun raḥīm.

"O Prophet: why do you forbid what God has made lawful to you, seeking to please your wives? And God is forgiving, merciful."

- The Qur'an, al-Tahrim 66:1

Premise 2: The Verse Pair Is Tying and Untying

The root h-r-m carries the image of a cord drawn tight; the root h-l-l carries the image of a knot released. The sura takes its name from the first verb and answers with the second:

يَا أَيُّهَا النَّبِيُّ لِمَ تُحَرِّمُ مَا أَحَلَّ اللَّهُ لَكَ تَبْتَغِي مَرْضَاةَ أَزْوَاجِكَ وَاللَّهُ غَفُورٌ رَحِيمٌ

Yā ayyuhā al-nabiyyu lima tuḥarrimu mā aḥalla llāhu laka, tabtaghī marḍāta azwājik? Wa-llāhu ghafūrun raḥīm.

"O Prophet: why do you forbid what God has made lawful to you, seeking to please your wives? And God is forgiving, merciful."

- The Qur'an, al-Tahrim 66:1

قَدْ فَرَضَ اللَّهُ لَكُمْ تَحِلَّةَ أَيْمَانِكُمْ وَاللَّهُ مَوْلَاكُمْ وَهُوَ الْعَلِيمُ الْحَكِيمُ

Qad faraḍa llāhu lakum taḥillata aymānikum, wa-llāhu mawlākum, wa-huwa al-ʿalīmu al-ḥakīm.

"God has already ordained for you the release of your oaths. And God is your master; He is the knowing, the wise."

- The Qur'an, al-Tahrim 66:2

Tuharrimu, you forbid, ties the knot; taḥillata aymānikum, the release of your oaths, unties it, and it is the untying root's own settled term for undoing a sworn knot.

Premise 3: The Forbidding of a Man Binds Only Himself

In the hadith of the school, a husband's declaration that his wife is forbidden to him accomplishes nothing in law. Imam al-Baqir ﵇ calls it, where it presumes to recast God's ruling, a lie. The Prophet's ﷺ act carried no such presumption: he barred Marya from himself and swore an oath, and amends were owed for the oath alone.

Premise 4: The Blame of the Sura Falls Elsewhere

The sura demands repentance from two wives who divulged a confidence, and it closes its opening address over the Prophet ﷺ with the words and God is forgiving, merciful. The question of 66:1 asks about motive, and what the sura supplies in answer is a release, a charge against the schemers, and mercy.

Conclusion: An Oath Taken and Released, With No Sin and No Legislation

The Prophet ﷺ abstained from a lawful thing under oath, revelation declined to let the abstention stand as precedent, and the ordinance God had already revealed for oaths closed the episode. The sin the objection alleges would require either a false claim about God's law or an act of disobedience. The sources show neither: the lexicon and the poets fix the grammar of the verbs, the hadith fix the classification of the act, and the scholars of the school state the verdict directly.

1. The Language

The dispute turns on two verbs: tuḥarrimu, you forbid, from the root h-r-m, and aḥalla, God made lawful, from h-l-l. The lexicons give the anatomy of both roots, and the pre-Islamic poets show them alive in ordinary speech, long before any creed had a stake in this verse. The witnesses stand in the open below; the running analysis that binds them sits in the Commentary at the end of the tab.

The Root That Bars

Ibn Faris posits one underlying sense for every word of the root, and for h-r-m that sense is prevention. Ibn Sayyidih fixes the legal use as the plain opposite of the lawful:

الحاء والراء والميم أصل واحد وهو المنع والتشديد

Al-ḥāʾ wa-al-rāʾ wa-al-mīm: aṣlun wāḥidun, wa-huwa al-manʿu wa-al-tashdīd.

"Ha, ra, mim is a single root, and it is prevention and tight binding."

- Ibn Faris (d.395 AH), Maqayis al-Lugha, Vol. 2, p. 45

الحِرْمُ والحَرامُ : نَقيضُ الحَلالِ

Al-ḥirmu wa-al-ḥarāmu: naqīḍu al-ḥalāl.

"Al-hirm and al-haram are the opposite of al-halal."

- Ibn Sayyidih (d.458 AH), al-Muhkam wa-l-Muhit al-A'zam, Vol. 3, p. 326

The Sacred Precinct

Al-Khalil's Kitab al-Ayn, the oldest organized lexicon in Arabic, defines the haram as geography, with the profane ring around it named from the untying root:

الحرم : حرم مكة وما أحاط بها إلى قريب من المواقيت التي يحرمون منها ، مفصول بين الحل والحرم بمنى

Al-ḥaramu: ḥaramu Makkata wa-mā aḥāṭa bihā ilā qarībin mina al-mawāqīti allatī yuḥrimūna minhā, maqṣūlun bayna al-ḥilli wa-al-ḥarami bi-Minā.

"The haram is the sanctuary of Mecca and what surrounds it, up to near the stations at which they enter ihram. A boundary separates the hill and the haram at Mina."

- Al-Khalil ibn Ahmad (d.175 AH), Kitab al-Ayn, Vol. 3, p. 221

The Root That Unties

The second root of the verse runs the opposite physics. Al-Maydani preserves its image inside a proverb about a traveler and his baggage, and Ibn Manzur records the verb of arrival that grows out of it:

فالحل بمعنى الحلول يقال حل بالمكان يحل حلا وحلولا ومحلا وأصله في الرجل يشد حمله فيسرف في الاستيثاق حتى يضر ذلك به وبراحلته عند الحلول

Wa-al-ḥullu bi-maʿnā al-ḥulūl: yuqālu ḥalla bi-al-makāni yaḥillu ḥallan wa-ḥulūlan wa-maḥillan, wa-aṣluhu fī al-rajuli yashuddu ḥamilahu fa-yusrifu fī al-istīthāqi ḥattā yuḍirra dhālika bihi wa-bi-rāḥilatihi ʿinda al-ḥulūl.

"And al-hull in the sense of alighting: one says halla bi-l-makan, yahullu, halan and hululan and mahillan. Its origin is the man who straps his baggage so excessively tight that it harms him and his mount when he dismounts."

- Al-Maydani (d.518 AH), Majma' al-Amthal, Vol. 2, p. 377

حَلَّ بالمكان يَحُلُّ حُلولًا ومَحَلًّا وحَلًّا وحَلَلًا ، بفك التضعيف نادر : وذلك نزول القوم بمَحَلَّة وهو نقيض الارتحال

Ḥalla bi-al-makāni yaḥullu ḥulūlan wa-maḥallan wa-ḥallan wa-ḥalalan, bi-fakki al-tashdīdi nādirun: wa-dhālika nuzūlu al-qawmi bi-maḥallatin wa-huwa naqīḍu al-irtiḥāl.

"Halla bi-l-makan, yahullu: alighting, a stopping place, and a halting; and the form with the doubled consonant undone is rare. It means a people alighting at an encampment, and it is the opposite of decamping."

- Ibn Manzur (d.711 AH), Lisan al-Arab, Vol. 11, p. 163

The Lawful, the Spouse, and the Oath

Ibn Sayyidih's entry in al-Mukhassas runs the derivation chain from the lawful thing to the released oath in a single breath, and al-Raghib, the lexicographer of the Quran, glosses the verb of 66:1 directly:

الحَلَالُ ضِدُّ الحَرام وهو الحِلُّ والحَلِيلُ حَلَّ الشىءُ يَحِلُّ حِلًّا وأَحَلَّهُ اللَّهُ سبحانه واسْتَحْلَلْتُهُ - اتَّخَذْتُهُ حَلالا ومنه حَلَّلْتُ اليمينَ تَحْلِيلًا وتَحِلَّة وتَحِلًّا

Al-ḥalālu ḍiddu al-ḥarāmi wa-huwa al-ḥillu wa-al-ḥalīl. Ḥalla al-shayʾu yaḥillu ḥillan, wa-aḥallahu llāhu subḥānahu, wa-istaḥlaltuhu: ittakhadhtuhu ḥalālan. Wa-minhu ḥallaltu al-yamīna taḥlīlan wa-taḥillatan wa-taḥillan.

"Al-halal is the opposite of al-haram; it is al-hill and al-halil. A thing becomes lawful, halla; God made it lawful, ahallahu; I took it as lawful, istahlaltuhu. From this root also: I released the oath, hallaltu al-yamin, tahilan and tahillatan."

- Ibn Sayyidih (d.458 AH), al-Mukhassas, Vol. 13, p. 105

أي : لم تحكم بتحريم ذلك ؟

Ayy: lima taḥkumu bi-taḥrīmi dhālika?

"That is: why do you pronounce the ruling of prohibition over it?"

- Al-Raghib al-Isfahani (d.425 AH), al-Mufradat fi Gharib al-Qur'an, p. 230

Antara's Gazelle

A pre-Islamic couplet carries the exact grammar of Quran 66:1, centuries before the verse. One woman receives two verdicts in two lines: lawful to him, forbidden to me.

يا شاةَ ما قَنَصٍ لِمَنْ حَلَّتْ لهُ * حَرُمَتْ عَليّ وَلَيْتَها لم تَحْرُمِ

Yā shāta mā qanaṣin li-man ḥallat lahu / ḥurimat ʿalayya wa-laytahā lam taḥrum.

"You gazelle: what chase remains for the man to whom she is already lawful? She is forbidden to me, and I wish she had never been."

- Antara ibn Shaddad, Mu'allaqa, in Jamharat Ash'ar al-Arab, p. 167

The Protected Man and the Closed Hand

Two further witnesses fix the barring root's range, from the guarded life to the denied gift:

فشَكَكْتُ بالرّمْحِ الأَصَمّ ثِيابَهُ * لَيْسَ الكَريمُ على القَنَا بِمُحَرَّمِ

Fashakkaktu bi-al-ramḥi al-aṣammi thiyābah / laysa al-karīmu ʿalā al-qanā bi-muḥarram.

"I threaded his garments with the flawless spear. Nobility earns no sanctuary against the spearpoints."

- Antara ibn Shaddad, Mu'allaqa, in Jamharat Ash'ar al-Arab, p. 167

ومَنْ يُكثِرِ التَّسآلَ لا بُدَّ يَحْرِمُ

Wa-man yukthiri al-tasʾāla lā budda yaḥrum.

"Whoever asks and asks will end denied."

- Al-A'sha, as indexed in al-Khwarizmi (d.383 AH), al-Amthal al-Muwallada, p. 596

The Sacred Months and the Pilgrim's Map

The diwan of Abu Talib, the Prophet's ﷺ uncle and guardian, moves through a pre-partitioned year, and Labid's Mu'allaqa surveys the pilgrim ground of Mina where the word pair lives as terrain:

وتنسى حَلِيلَة * حَلِيلاً ويَغْشى مُحَرَّمٌ بعد مُحَرَّمِ

Wa-tansā ḥalīlatun / ḥalīlan wa-yaghshā muḥarramun baʿda muḥarram.

"A wife forgets a husband, and one sacred month rolls over the next."

- Diwan Abu Talib (d.10 AH), p. 216

عَفَتِ الدّيَارُ مَحَلُّهَا فَمَقَامُهَا . . . بِمنًى تأبَّدَ غَوْلُها فَرِجَامُهَا

ʿafati al-diyāru maḥalluhā fa-maqāmuhā / bi-Minā taʾabbada ghawluhā fa-rijāmuhā.

"The campsites have faded, their halting-ground and their resting-ground alike. At Mina, Ghawl and Rijam have gone wild."

- Labid (d.41 AH), Mu'allaqa, Diwan Labid, p. 107

دِمَنٌ تَجَرَّمَ بعدَ عَهْدِ أنِيسِهَا . . . حِجَجٌ خَلَوْنَ حَلالُهَا وحَرَامُهَا

Dimanun tajarrama baʿda ʿahdi anīsihā / ḥijajun khalawna ḥalāluhā wa-ḥarāmuhā.

"Dung-worn traces, crumbled since the days of their people. Years of pilgrimage have passed over them, their lawful and their forbidden."

- Labid (d.41 AH), Mu'allaqa, Diwan Labid, p. 107

The Lawful Pair

Al-Kumayt ibn Zayd, the poet of the Ahl al-Bayt ﵈ in the Umayyad century, supplies the marriage register of the untying root:

ولا حَلِيلَةُ جاري لستُ زاعِمَها * تَصْبو إلَيّ وساءَ الصِّدقُ والكَذِبُ

Wa-lā ḥalīlatu jārin lastu zāʿimahā / taṣbū ilayya wa-sāʾa al-ṣidqu wa-al-kadhib.

"Never my neighbor's lawful wife do I claim, though she incline toward me; good faith would sink into falsehood."

- Al-Kumayt ibn Zayd (d.126 AH), Diwan al-Kumayt, Vol. 1, p. 80

Commentary

>
The Verbs Track the Person

Halal and haram in Arabic mark a person's access to a thing, and Antara's couplet proves it by contradiction. The woman is figured as a wild cow of the chase. The first hemistich addresses her from the standpoint of the man who lawfully has her: for him the hunt is over, since the lawful thing is the attained thing, with nothing left to pursue. The second hemistich, in the same breath, gives her the opposite standing from the poet's own. Nothing in the woman changed between the two halves; the verbs record two men's access to one object, and no listener of the age took the poet for a legislator. Now lay Quran 66:1 over the couplet. The verse says the Prophet ﷺ barred what God made lawful for you, the relational form, God's permission addressed to one man, and the same man's barring of himself. Two standings over one object: God's open knot, and the Prophet's ﷺ closed one. The question of the verse is put to the second standing only; the first, what God made lawful, the verse treats as fixed and untroubled from beginning to end.

The Barred Root Carries No Reproach

Ibn Faris strips away every religious accretion the root had acquired by the fourth century and records the bare image underneath: a cord drawn tight, a gate shut, a hand closed. Whatever else the root comes to name, a month, a city, a person, a morsel of food, the constant is blocked access. One feature of the definition carries the whole verse: the verb names the act of barring, and it is silent on the right to bar. Authority is foreign to the word itself. Arabic asks the authority question with other words entirely, and the Quran asks it only when it means to accuse. Notice also what Ibn Sayyidih chose as the opposite of the barred: a word about untying. The forbidden and the lawful are, in this vocabulary, the shut and the open. And the honorific sense heard in Antara's spear line matters most for reading 66:1: the muharram man is the protected one, the sacrosanct. To be haram is to be guarded, inviolable, held safe. The root carries no intrinsic sense of wrong, so any reproach must arrive from context, and the context supplies a question about motive and a close of mercy.

Sanctuaries, Months, and the Scale of the Act

Al-Khalil's map makes the root pair into two sides of one boundary, and an Arab of the Hijaz learned halal and haram as terrain before he met them as legal abstractions. Abu Talib's line then moves through a year that arrives pre-partitioned: one sacred month rolls over the next, and while they run, bloodshed is barred. The poet reports the partitions as fixed furniture of the world, the way another line reports weather; no poet issues a sacred month, and none rescinds one. Labid surveys the same order as terrain, at Mina, the pilgrim's station, where the halting-ground carries the untying root's name and the pair itself stands as a state of the pilgrim world. The lesson of the scale: the sacred months and the sanctuary bind a whole people, while the act of 66:1, on the hadith account, barred one bondswoman from one man, himself.

The Untying Root and the Architecture of the Sura

Behind the lexicon's gloss stands the caravan: the loads come off the animals, the hobbles come off the legs, the tent ropes go slack. The camp is named for the act of untying, and to settle, in Arabic, is to untie; the language cannot describe arrival except as release. When the Quran wants a word for a thing open to use it takes it from this root, and the same derivation produces the verb of 66:2 that undoes an oath: taḥillat al-aymān is the root's own settled term for the untying of a sworn knot, in continuous use since before Islam. The everyday words for spouse belong to the same family: the lawful wife and the lawful husband are the people whose knot stands open to each other. Al-Raghib hears the verb of 66:1 as a question about the Prophet's ﷺ own declaration over a thing, a pronouncing of prohibition, which is precisely the shape the hadith give the act: a declaration, and an oath upon it. A declaration binds the declarer; it reaches no one else, and it touches the thing's standing before God not at all, because the standing belongs to the God who opened it. Read with both roots, the sura's opening pair is one physics seen twice: a knot God left open, and the same cord pulled shut by a hand, and the same cord coming open again through the release of oaths.

2. The Quran and the Hadith

The Quran sorts every act of forbidding into three registers, and it assigns the Prophet ﷺ to none of the ones that carry blame. Once the registers are clear, the hadith of the Ahl al-Bayt ﵈ fix which act the verse addressed: an oath, with its release already legislated.

The Verse and the Release That Follows It

The sura opens on the question and answers it within one verse:

بِسْمِ ٱللَّهِ ٱلرَّحْمَٰنِ ٱلرَّحِيمِ يَٰٓأَيُّهَا ٱلنَّبِىُّ لِمَ تُحَرِّمُ مَآ أَحَلَّ ٱللَّهُ لَكَ ۖ تَبْتَغِى مَرْضَاتَ أَزْوَٰجِكَ ۚ وَٱللَّهُ غَفُورٌۭ رَّحِيمٌۭ

Yā ayyuha al-nabiyyu: lima tuḥarrimu mā aḥalla llāhu laka, tabtaghī marḍāta azwājik? Wa-llāhu ghafūrun raḥīm.

"O Prophet: why do you forbid what God has made lawful to you, seeking to please your wives? And God is forgiving, merciful."

- The Qur'an, al-Tahrim 66:1

قَدْ فَرَضَ ٱللَّهُ لَكُمْ تَحِلَّةَ أَيْمَٰنِكُمْ ۚ وَٱللَّهُ مَوْلَىٰكُمْ ۖ وَهُوَ ٱلْعَلِيمُ ٱلْحَكِيمُ

Qad faraḍa llāhu lakum taḥillata aymānikum, wa-llāhu mawlākum, wa-huwa al-ʿalīmu al-ḥakīm.

"God has already ordained for you the release of your oaths. And God is your master; He is the knowing, the wise."

- The Qur'an, al-Tahrim 66:2

The Three Registers of Forbidding

God alone legislates in the first register, marked by God as subject or the passive: aḥalla, uḥilla, ḥurrima, as in 66:1 itself, 5:1, and 4:23. The second register addresses believers about their own abstentions, and the third is the accusation, aimed at people who carved God's provision into forbidden and lawful on their own authority:

يَٰٓأَيُّهَا ٱلَّذِينَ ءَامَنُوا۟ لَا تُحَرِّمُوا۟ طَيِّبَٰتِ مَآ أَحَلَّ ٱللَّهُ لَكُمْ وَلَا تَعْتَدُوٓا۟ ۚ إِنَّ ٱللَّهَ لَا يُحِبُّ ٱلْمُعْتَدِينَ

Yā ayyuhā alladhīna āmanū: lā tuḥarrimū ṭayyibāti mā aḥalla llāhu lakum wa-lā taʿtadū, inna llāha lā yuḥibbu al-muʿtadīn.

"O you who believe: do not forbid the good things that God has made lawful to you, and do not exceed the bounds. God does not love those who exceed."

- The Qur'an, al-Ma'ida 5:87

فَجَعَلْتُم مِّنْهُ حَرَامًۭا وَحَلَٰلًۭا قُلْ ءَآللَّهُ أَذِنَ لَكُمْ ۖ أَمْ عَلَى ٱللَّهِ تَفْتَرُونَ

Fa-jaʿaltum minhu ḥarāman wa-ḥalālan. Qul: āllāhu adhina lakum am ʿalā llāhi taftirūn?

"...and you have made of it forbidden and lawful. Say: did God authorize you, or do you fabricate against God?"

- The Qur'an, Yunus 10:59

Imam al-Sadiq's ﵇ Account

The narration-based tafsir of the school transmits a report from Imam al-Sadiq ﵇ that names the act, the oath, and the remedy in three sentences:

في تفسير علي بن إبراهيم أخبرنا أحمد بن إدريس قال : حدثنا أحمد بن محمد عن الحسين بن سعيد عن ابن سنان عن أبي عبد الله ﵇ في قوله : يا أيها النبي لم تحرم ما أحل الله لك الآية قال : اطلعت عائشة وحفصة على النبي ﵌ وهو مع مارية فقال النبي ﵌ : والله ما أقر بها فأمره الله ان يكفر عن يمينه

Fī tafsīri ʿAliyyi ibni Ibrāhīma: akhbaranā Aḥmadu ibnu Idrīsa qāla: ḥaddathanā Aḥmadu ibnu Muḥammadin ʿan al-Ḥusayni ibni Saʿīdin ʿan ibni Sinānin ʿan Abī ʿAbdillāhi ʿalayhi al-salāmu fī qawlihi: yā ayyuhā al-nabiyyu lima tuḥarrimu mā aḥalla llāhu lak, al-āyatu, qāla: iṭṭalaʿat ʿāʾishatu wa-Ḥafṣatu ʿalā al-nabiyyi ṣallā llāhu ʿalayhi wa-ālihi wa-huwa maʿa Māriyata, fa-qāla al-nabiyyu: wa-llāhi mā uqirru bihā, fa-amara-hu llāhu an yukaffira ʿan yamīnih.

"In the Tafsir of Ali ibn Ibrahim: Ahmad ibn Idris informed us, saying Ahmad ibn Muhammad related to us, from al-Husayn ibn Sa'id, from Ibn Sinan, from Abu Abdillah ﵇, concerning His word 'O Prophet, why do you forbid what God has made lawful to you', the verse.

He said: Aisha and Hafsa came upon the Prophet ﷺ while he was with Marya. So the Prophet ﷺ said: by God, I will have nothing to do with her.

And God commanded him to make amends for his oath."

- Al-Huwayzi (d.1112 AH), Tafsir Nur al-Thaqalayn, Vol. 5, p. 367, from Tafsir Ali ibn Ibrahim

The Occasion in Tafsir al-Qummi

Ali ibn Ibrahim al-Qummi's tafsir preserves the fullest occasion report, and its wording settles the grammar of the Prophet's ﷺ declaration. The report continues that the secret concerned the succession and traveled from Hafsa to Aisha and on to Abu Bakr and Umar, and that revelation followed:

فاستحيا رسول الله منها ، فقال كفى فقد حرمت مارية على نفسي ولا أطأها بعد هذا ابدا وأنا أفضي إليك سرا فان أنت أخبرت به فعليك لعنة الله والملائكة والناس أجمعين

Fa-istaḥyā rasūlu llāhi minhā fa-qāla: kafā, qad ḥarramtu Māriyata ʿalā nafsī wa-lā aṭʾuhā baʿda hādhā abadan, wa-anā ufḍī ilayki sirran, fa-in anti akhbarti bihi fa-ʿalayki laʿnatu llāhi wa-al-malāʾikati wa-al-nāsi ajmaʿīn.

"The Prophet of God felt shy before her, and he said: enough. I have forbidden Marya to myself, and I shall never touch her again. And I shall confide to you a secret. If you disclose it, the curse of God and the angels and all people shall be upon you."

- Ali ibn Ibrahim al-Qummi (d.329 AH), Tafsir al-Qummi, Vol. 2, p. 376

فنزل جبرئيل على رسول الله ﵌ بهذه السورة (يا أيها النبي لم تحرم ما أحل الله لك - إلى قوله - تحلة ايمانكم) يعني قد أباح الله لك ان تكفر عن يمينك

Fa-nazala Jibrīʾīlu ʿalā rasūli llāhi bi-hādhihi al-sūrati: yā ayyuhā al-nabiyyu lima tuḥarrimu mā aḥalla llāhu lak, ilā qawlihi taḥillata aymānikum, yaʿnī qad abāḥa llāhu laka an tukaffira ʿan yamīnik.

"Then Gabriel descended upon the Prophet of God with this sura: 'O Prophet, why do you forbid what God has made lawful to you', to His word 'the release of your oaths', meaning: God has permitted you to make amends for your oath."

- Ali ibn Ibrahim al-Qummi (d.329 AH), Tafsir al-Qummi, Vol. 2, p. 376

The Act Classified as an Oath

A companion report transmits the classification in the Imam's ﵇ own words, with the amends set out item by item. The chain, from Muhammad ibn Qays from Imam al-Baqir ﵇, runs through al-Kafi, and al-Tusi carries it in al-Istibsar:

عن أبي جعفر عليه السّلام في قوله : « تَحِلَّةَ أَيْمانِكُمْ » قال : جعلها يمينا وكفّرها رسول اللّه بأن أطعم عشرة مساكين لكلّ مسكين مدّ . قلنا : فمن وجد الكسوة ؟ قال : ثوب يواري عورته

ʿan Abī Jaʿfarin ʿalayhi al-salāmu fī qawlihi taḥillata aymānikum qāla: jaʿalahā yamīnan wa-kaffarahā rasūlu llāhi bi-an aṭʿama ʿasharata masākīna li-kulli miskīnin muddan. Qulnā: fa-man wajada al-kiswata? Qāla: thawbun yuwārī bihi ʿawratahu.

"From Abu Ja'far ﵇, concerning His word 'the release of your oaths': He made it an oath, and the Messenger of God made amends by feeding ten poor persons, a mudd for each poor person. We said: and whoever finds clothing, what is its measure? He said: a garment that covers his nakedness."

- Al-Jaza'iri (d.1112 AH), Uqud al-Marjan fi Tafsir al-Qur'an, Vol. 5, p. 157, from Imam al-Baqir ﵇

The Wording in al-Tusi's Amali

The self-directed formula survives independently in a report al-Tusi delivered in his Amali, which al-Bahrani transmits in al-Burhan. Hafsa has found the Prophet ﷺ with Umm Ibrahim, Marya's byname, on Aisha's day, and threatens to tell:

وجدت حفصة رسول الله (﵌) مع أم إبراهيم في يوم عائشة ، فقالت : لأخبرنها . فقال رسول الله (﵌) : «اكتمي ذلك ، وهي علي حرام»

Wajadat Ḥafṣatu rasūla llāhi maʿa ummi Ibrāhīma fī yawmi ʿāʾishata fa-qālat: la-ukhbirannahā. Fa-qāla rasūlu llāhi: uktumī dhālika, wa-hiya ʿalayya ḥarām.

"Hafsa found the Messenger of God with Umm Ibrahim on Aisha's day. She said: I shall surely tell her. The Messenger of God said: conceal this; she is forbidden to me."

- Al-Bahrani (d.1107 AH), al-Burhan fi Tafsir al-Qur'an, Vol. 5, p. 419, hadith 10858, from al-Tusi (d.460 AH), al-Amali, by way of al-Zuhri, from Ibn Abbas

Zurara's Question and the Imam's ﵇ Answer

The central narration of the whole question is a dialogue preserved in al-Kafi. Zurara, a senior companion of Imam al-Baqir ﵇, asks about a man who declares his wife forbidden to him, and receives a ruling whose severity is itself the argument. He then puts the objection at issue, that God Himself set amends in the verse:

عدة من أصحابنا ، عن سهل بن زياد عن ابن أبي نصر ، عن محمد بن سماعة ، عن زرارة ، عن أبي جعفر ﵇ قال : سألته عن رجل قال لامرأته : أنت علي حرام ، فقال لي : لو كان لي عليه سلطان لأوجعت رأسه ، وقلت له : الله أحلها لك فما حرمها عليك ، إنه لم يزد على أن كذب فزعم أن ما أحل الله له حرام ، ولا يدخل عليه طلاق ولا كفارة

ʿIddatun min aṣḥābinā ʿan Sahlī ibni Ziyādin ʿan ibni Abī Naṣrin ʿan Muḥammadi ibni Simāʿata ʿan Zurārata ʿan Abī Jaʿfarin ʿalayhi al-salāmu qāla: saʾaltuhu ʿan rajulin qāla li-imraʾatihi: anti ʿalayya ḥarāmun? Fa-qāla lī: law kāna lī ʿalayhi sulṭānun la-awjaʿtu raʾsahu wa-qultu lahu: allāhu aḥallalahā laka fa-mā ḥarramahā ʿalayka? Innahu lam yazid ʿalā an kadhaba fa-zaʿama anna mā aḥalla llāhu lahu ḥarāmun, wa-lā yadkhulu ʿalayhi ṭalāqun wa-lā kaffārah.

"A number of our companions, from Sahl ibn Ziyad, from Ibn Abi Nasr, from Muhammad ibn Sima'a, from Zurara, from Abu Ja'far ﵇. He said: I asked him about a man who says to his wife: you are forbidden to me.

He said to me: 'Were authority over him mine, I would bruise his head, and I would say to him: God made her lawful to you, so what has forbidden her to you? He has done nothing but lie, for he claims that what God made lawful to him is forbidden. No divorce counts, and no amends are owed.'"

- Al-Kulayni (d.329 AH), al-Kafi, Vol. 6, p. 134, Book of Marriage, chapter of the man who declares his wife forbidden to him, hadith 1

فَقُلْتُ : قَوْلُ اللَّهِ عَزَّ وَجَلَّ : « يا أَيُّهَا النَّبِيُّ لِمَ تُحَرِّمُ ما أَحَلَّ اللَّهُ لَكَ » فَجَعَلَ فِيهِ الْكَفَّارَةَ ؟ فَقَالَ : « إِنَّمَا حَرَّمَ عَلَيْهِ جَارِيَتَهُ مَارِيَةَ ، وَحَلَفَ أَنْ لَايَقْرَبَهَا ، فَإِنَّمَا جَعَلَ عَلَيْهِ الْكَفَّارَةَ فِي الْحَلْفِ ، وَلَمْ يَجْعَلْ عَلَيْهِ فِي التَّحْرِيمِ »

Fa-qultu: qawlu llāhi ʿazza wa-jalla: yā ayyuhā al-nabiyyu lima tuḥarrimu mā aḥalla llāhu lak, fa-jaʿala fīhi al-kaffārata? Fa-qāla: innamā ḥarrama ʿalayhi jāriyatahu Māriyata wa-ḥalafa an lā yaqrabahā, fa-innamā jaʿala ʿalayhi al-kaffārata fī al-ḥalaf, wa-lam yajʿal ʿalayhi fī al-taḥrīm.

"I said: then the word of God, mighty and majestic: 'O Prophet, why do you forbid what God has made lawful to you', He set amends in it. He said: 'He only forbade his bondswoman Marya to himself, and he swore that he would not approach her. The amends were laid upon him only for the oath, and nothing was laid upon him for the forbidding.'"

- Al-Kulayni (d.329 AH), al-Kafi, Vol. 6, p. 134, Book of Marriage, chapter of the man who declares his wife forbidden to him, hadith 1

Commentary

>
The Three Registers and the Grammar of the Verse

The same forbidding verb runs through three registers in the Quran, and the middle one must be read with care, because it carries a prohibition. In 5:87 ordinary believers are told not to forbid themselves the good things God made lawful. The address there is a command against the act, and the blame it names falls on the excess that follows the abstention: do not transgress. What the verse prohibits is treating God's permissions as a thing a man may lock away from himself; it does not name the self-barring itself a sin against God's authority, and it does not charge its addressees with forgery. In 10:59 the question did God authorize you defines a different crime outright: forgery, legislating where God had not, framed for a people who declared foods forbidden as a ruling. Place 66:1 between them. The verse's grammar is the self-directed barring of 5:87's world, a man closing something on himself; and the authorization question of 10:59, the forgery charge, is never put to the Prophet ﷺ anywhere in the Book. Two consequences follow. A reading that takes 66:1 as an accusation of legislating against God must import the forgery register, which the verse does not carry. And the 5:87 parallel cuts against the Prophet in one respect that candor requires naming: addressed to anyone else, the act would draw that verse's prohibition. What dissolves the asymmetry is not privilege but the two institutions the Qur'an itself keeps apart. The vow to abstain is the permitted oath institution that 66:2 itself legislates for and names, tahillat aymānikum; what 5:87 prohibits is the locking away of God's permissions as though what God opened were barred. The Prophet's recorded act belongs to the first institution by its form, a sworn personal abstention cleared on the oath's scale, and never to the second by any record. The interruption came from heaven in the oath's own channel, and the amends that followed are the oath's own amends.

The Nearest Rival: Zihar, al-Mujadila 58:1-4

One classification deserves to be met before any other, because it is the nearest rival: the zihar of Surat al-Mujadila. A man bars his wife from himself with a pre-Islamic formula, and the Qur'an answers with amends of its own. If 66:1 were a zihar, the amends would answer the barring itself, and the oath-reading would be wrong.

The test is textual, and the Qur'an runs it for us. A zihar is the comparison of a wife to the back of a mother; its grammar is a simile spoken over the wife, and the Qur'an names what it is in the same breath:

ٱلَّذِينَ يُظَٰهِرُونَ مِنكُم مِّن نِّسَآئِهِم مَّا هُنَّ أُمَّهَٰتِهِمْ ۖ إِنْ أُمَّهَٰتُهُمْ إِلَّا ٱلَّٰٓـِٔى وَلَدْنَهُمْ ۚ وَإِنَّهُمْ لَيَقُولُونَ مُنكَرًۭا مِّنَ ٱلْقَوْلِ وَزُورًۭا ۚ وَإِنَّ ٱللَّهَ لَعَفُوٌّ غَفُورٌۭ

Alladhīna yuẓāhirūna minkum min nisāʾihim mā hunna ummahātuhum; in ummahātuhum illā allātī waladnahum, wa-innahum la-yaqūlūna munkaran mina al-qawli wa-zūrā, wa-inna llāha la-ʿafuwwun ghafūr.

"Those of you who declare zihar from their wives: they are not their mothers; their mothers are only those who gave them birth. And they surely utter a monstrous word and a forgery; and God is pardoning, forgiving."

- The Qur'an, al-Mujadila, 58:2

Every element of the test fails for 66:1. The formula the records quote is not a zihar formula: it is the sworn self-directed barring, by God I will have nothing to do with her, and the plain she is forbidden to me, with no comparison, no address to the wife, no mother-back language anywhere. And the Qur'an's own verdict on zihar is the verdict this commentary has shown 66:1 does not carry: munkar min al-qawl, a monstrous word, wa-zur, a forgery, the blame vocabulary of 10:59's world, not the oath vocabulary of 66:2. The Qur'an does more than set amends for a zihar; it condemns the words even while it sets the amends. No such word is spoken over 66:1, whose immediate sequel is God's forgiveness and mercy. The two institutions also differ at law, as the fiqh of both camps codifies them: the amends for zihar escalate through the freeing of a neck, two consecutive months of fasting, and only then sixty poor, and they bar the marriage bed until performed; the amends of 66:2 are the oath's amends, and 66:2 calls it by the oath's own name, tahillat aymānikum, the release of your oaths. A classification is proved by its consequences, and the consequences here are the oath's.

The Occasion and the Classification

The report of Imam al-Sadiq ﵇ fixes every element the lexical analysis predicted: the thing barred is Marya, the form is the sworn formula by God I will have nothing to do with her, and the divine response runs through the oath channel, God commanded him to make amends for his oath. Al-Qummi's occasion report settles the grammar of the declaration itself: qad ḥarramtu Māriyata ʿalā nafsī, I have forbidden Marya to myself, the self-directed form, the same grammar as Antara's forbidden to me. Arabic has a form for legislating, and this is not it: no ruling is issued, no community is addressed, no claim is made about God's law. The classification narration then rules on the act: jaʿalahā yamīnan, He made it an oath. The verb is a ruling verb, and the classification matters because oaths and legislation travel through different channels in the law: an oath binds the swearer alone, is cleared by a set amends, feeding ten poor people, and changes nothing in the world's permissions, while a legislation binds everyone and stands until repealed. The amends are itemized on the poor-feeding scale, ten poor persons, a mudd each. And the self-directed formula survives a second, independent chain in al-Tusi's Amali: she is forbidden to me, said of one person, by one man, about himself.

The Sin Question Answered Inside the Narration

The Imam's ﵇ ruling on the ordinary man is that his declaration accomplishes nothing: no divorce enters upon it, and no amends. Its only content is a lie, he claims that what God made lawful for him is forbidden. Read the severity carefully, because it locates the sin with precision: the sin of a false tahrim is the attribution, the assertion that what God made lawful is barred. The abstention itself harms no one in law. Zurara's objection then supplies the test case, and the answer separates the two acts the Prophet ﷺ performed. He forbade his bondswoman Marya to himself, and he swore that he would not approach her. The amends were laid upon him only for the oath, and nothing was laid upon him for the forbidding. The silence must be read with precision, because the reading cuts both ways, and candor reads it both ways. In Zurara's case the same silence, no amends for the tahrim, accompanies an explicit verdict of lie; so the absence of amends by itself proves nothing either way, and the Imam's severity there is the measure of what a claiming tahrim is. What separates the Prophet's ﷺ act from the Imam's hypothetical liar is therefore not the silence but the record around it: the Qur'an's own sequel. Zurara's liar is condemned out of his own mouth, he claims that what God made lawful is forbidden; and nothing in any record of 66:1 puts such a claim in the Prophet's ﷺ mouth, while the Qur'an itself classifies what he did perform under the oath's name and settles it with the oath's amends, closing with forgiveness and mercy rather than the monstrous word it speaks over the zihar. The classification, not the silence, carries the conclusion: the Qur'an itself sorted the act into the oath channel, and an oath claims nothing about God's law.

3. The Scholarly Discussion

The exegetes of the school put the sin question to the verse directly and answer it in set terms. Their verdicts hold across both variant occasions of the sura, and the one flag of caution in the corpus concerns the political details of the occasion report, never its legal core.

Al-Jaza'iri: No Indication of Sin

Nimat Allah al-Jaza'iri's Qur'an commentary states the verdict on the sin question in terms the scholars of the school repeat, and the same passage records the variant occasions of the sura under a single oath analysis:

ليس فيه دلالة على وقوع ذنب منه . لأنَّ تحريم الرجل بعض نسائه أو بعض الملاذّ لسبب أو لغير سبب ليس بقبيح . ولا يمتنع أن يكون خرج هذا القول مخرج التوجّع له عليه السّلام إذ بالغ على إرضاء أزواجه وتحمّل ذلك المشقّة . ولو أنَّ إنسانا أرضى بعض نسائه بتطليق بعضهنّ ، لجاز أن يقال له : لم فعلت ذلك ؟ وإن لم يكن قبيحا

Laysa fīhi dalālatun ʿalā wuqūʿi dhanbin minhu, li-anna taḥrīma al-rajuli baʿḍa nisāʾihi aw baʿḍa al-malādhdhi li-sababin aw li-ghayri sababin laysa bi-qabīḥin, wa-lā yumtaniʿu an yakūna kharaja hādhā al-qawlu makhraja al-tawajjuʿi lahu ʿalayhi al-salāmu idh bālagha ʿalā irḍāʾi azwājihi wa-taḥammala dhālika al-mashaqqata, wa-law anna insānan arḍā baʿḍa nisāʾihi bi-taṭlīqi baʿḍihinna la-jāza an yuqāla lahu: lima faʿalta dhālika wa-in lam yakun qabīḥan.

"There is in it no indication that a sin occurred from him. For a man's forbidding to himself one of his wives, or some pleasure, for a cause or without a cause, is not ugly.

And it is not impossible that this saying came out in the mode of sympathy for him, peace be upon him, since he went to great lengths to please his wives and bore that hardship. If a man pleased one of his wives by divorcing another, it would be permissible to say to him: why did you do that, even though it is not ugly."

- Al-Jaza'iri (d.1112 AH), Uqud al-Marjan fi Tafsir al-Qur'an, Vol. 5, p. 157

وإنّما أوجب اللّه فيه الكفّارة لأنّ النبيّ كان حلف أن لا يقرب جاريته أو لا يشرب الشراب المذكور

Wa-innamā awjaba llāhu fīhi al-kaffārata li-anna al-nabiyya kāna ḥalafa an lā yaqraba jāriyatahu aw lā yashraba al-sharāba al-madhkūr.

"God made the amends obligatory in it only because the Prophet had sworn not to approach his bondswoman, or not to drink the mentioned drink."

- Al-Jaza'iri (d.1112 AH), Uqud al-Marjan fi Tafsir al-Qur'an, Vol. 5, p. 157

Where the Blame of the Sura Falls

Verse 66:4 turns from the Prophet ﷺ to two of his wives and demands repentance, and the backing verse arrays his guard by name. Imam al-Baqir ﵇ identifies the salih of the believers:

عن أبي جعفر عليه السّلام في قوله : « صالِحُ الْمُؤْمِنِينَ » قال : صالح المؤمنين هو عليّ بن أبي طالب عليه السّلام

ʿan Abī Jaʿfarin ʿalayhi al-salāmi fī qawlihi ṣāliḥu al-muʾminīna qāla: ṣāliḥu al-muʾminīna huwa ʿAliyyu ibnu Abī Ṭālibin ʿalayhi al-salām.

"From Abu Ja'far ﵇, concerning His word 'the salih of the believers': the salih of the believers is Ali ibn Abi Talib ﵇."

- Al-Jaza'iri (d.1112 AH), Uqud al-Marjan fi Tafsir al-Qur'an, Vol. 5, p. 157, from Imam al-Baqir ﵇

Al-Majlisi's Caution

Al-Majlisi, the encyclopedist of Bihar al-Anwar, appends a caution to the occasion report he transmits, and the caution's precise target matters:

وفي هذا الخبر غرايب لا نعلم حقيقتها ، فطوينا علي غرها والله يعلم وحججه صلوات الله عليهم جهه صدورها

Wa-fī hādhā al-khabari gharāʾibu lā naʿlamu ḥaqīqatahā fa-ṭuwaynā ʿalā gharrihā, wa-llāhu yaʿlamu wa-ḥujajuhu ṣalawātu llāhi ʿalayhim jihata ṣudūrihā.

"This report contains strange things whose truth we do not know, so we have passed over them in silence. God knows, and His proofs, God's blessings upon them, know the direction of its issuance."

- Al-Majlisi (d.1111 AH), Bihar al-Anwar, Vol. 22, p. 243

Commentary

>
The Scholarly Verdict and Where Blame Falls

Al-Jaza'iri makes three moves. First, a direct ruling in the negative: there is in it no indication that a sin occurred from him. Second, the reason: a man's forbidding to himself one of his wives or some pleasure, for a cause or without one, is free of ugliness. Third, the genre of the verse's question: it may have come forth in the mode of sympathy, for a man who went to great lengths to please his wives and bore the hardship of it. His parallel makes the grammar plain: a man who pleases one wife by divorcing another may be asked why did you do that, though the act is free of ugliness. The question why interrogates motive and cost; it does not convict. And the variant occasions change nothing: whether the thing barred was Marya or a honey drink, the vow-form carries the amends and the tahrim itself carries nothing. The sura's own structure agrees on where fault is laid: the repentance verse addresses the two wives who carried a confidence abroad, and the backing verse arrays God, Gabriel, Ali ﵇ named as the salih of the believers, and the angels behind the Prophet ﷺ.

Al-Majlisi's Caution and the Legal Core

Al-Majlisi's caution names what it names: strange things in the report whose truth we do not know, set down with the report's own chain, and the reader is not told which elements he meant. What can be said is that the report's own close names its oddities as belonging to the occasion's drama, and the legal elements the argument uses travel in their own reports: Marya and the oath in the al-Kafi and al-Qummi material above, the classification and the amends scale in the Ibn Qays report, the bare self-directed formula in the Amali report. A word on the classification report's chain, in candor: it runs through Sahl ibn Ziyad, whom the school's rijal literature grades harshly, and the hadith editors of al-Kafi print it among the book's chapters all the same, as they print his material throughout, trusting the book's structure over the man. The argument of this study does not rest on his report alone: the oath-reading of 66:2, the amends scale, and the self-directed formula are each carried by other chains quoted above, and the Sahl report is quoted for its analytic precision, not as a lone pillar.

The Other Camp's Own Reading

The strongest objection is not that the Sunni tradition convicts the Prophet ﷺ of sin at 66:1; it is that the Sunni tradition never needed the school's apparatus, because it already holds that no sin occurred, on a simpler frame: the verse corrects a lesser choice, tark al-awla, the honey occasion of Bukhari and Muslim standing in its books. That reading deserves to be stated at its strongest and answered on shared ground.

إِن تَتُوبَآ إِلَى ٱللَّهِ فَقَدْ صَغَتْ قُلُوبُكُمَا ۖ وَإِن تَظَٰهَرَا عَلَيْهِ فَإِنَّ ٱللَّهَ هُوَ مَوْلَىٰهُ وَجِبْرِيلُ وَصَٰلِحُ ٱلْمُؤْمِنِينَ ۖ وَٱلْمَلَٰٓئِكَةُ بَعْدَ ذَٰلِكَ ظَهِيرٌ

In tatūbā ilā llāhi fa-qad ṣaghat qulūbukumā, wa-in taẓāharā ʿalayhi fa-inna llāha huwa mawlāhu wa-Jibrīlu wa-ṣāliḥu al-muʾminīna, wa-al-malāʾikatu baʿda dhālika ẓahīr.

"If you two repent to God, then your hearts have inclined; and if you support each other against him, then God is his master, and Gabriel, and the righteous of the believers, and the angels after that are backers."

- The Qur'an, al-Tahrim, 66:4

The sura's own sequel shows where its weight falls: the repentance demanded in 66:4 is demanded of the two wives, and the warning of replacement in 66:5, better wives in their place, is addressed to them. The sura turns on the schemers, and its protection arrays itself around the Prophet ﷺ. The one corrective word addressed to him is 66:1's question, and the corrective travel of the passage ends there.

Three observations meet it. First, on its own terms the frame is an inference, and the school's charge against it is the one this commentary has already made structural: a correction addressed to a prophet, followed by legislated amends, reads as more than counsel about a preferable choice, and the Sunni exegesis itself divides here, with al-Razi and others allowing that the verb is a reproach and then excusing it as haste in a good cause, an allowance the school declines to make about a ma'sum. Second, the frame concedes everything the article needs: no sin, no legislation against God, and amends that answer a vow, which is why the Sunni fiqh, like the school's, clears 66:1 as an oath. Third, the disagreement that remains is not about 66:1 at all; it is about isma, the doctrine of protection, and candor names that: the commentary reads the verse through the school's doctrine of the Prophet's ﷺ protection, as its opponent reads it through a doctrine that permits lesser lapses. What the shared record shows, whichever doctrine a reader brings, is the verse's own grammar: a self-directed barring, an oath's amends, and God's forgiveness and mercy in the same breath. The reader who accepts the Sunni frame still has no sin at 66:1; the reader who accepts the school's frame has the same verse doing the same work, with the protection doctrine explaining why the interruption came as a correction of channel and not a charge.

Ismah: The Protection at Work

Consider the sequence the sources give. A personal abstention occurs, under oath. Revelation intervenes at once, before the abstention can settle into precedent, and the intervention takes the form of a question about motive and an exit through the release of oaths, with the amends that are the oath's own remedy: feeding ten poor people. The community thereby learns, inside the sura itself, that no vow of any man, the Prophet's ﷺ own included, can narrow what God has left open; the record publishes the episode together with its correction, and nothing is carved away. The sura closes its address over the Prophet ﷺ with forgiveness and mercy, and turns its severity on the two wives and the scheming. That is what protection looks like in action on the school's reading: an abstention corrected within two verses, a community taught the limits of vows, and mercy sealing the address.

Glossary of Key Terms

Tahrim: An act of forbidding, from the root h-r-m; in Quran 66:1 the barring of a lawful thing from oneself.

Halal / Haram: The open and the barred; lawful and forbidden, named by the roots h-l-l and h-r-m.

Hill: The open land around the sanctuary; the profane ring outside the haram of Mecca.

Tahillat al-ayman: The release of oaths, Quran 66:2; from the untying root, the settled term for undoing a sworn knot.

Ihram: The pilgrim's consecrated state, from the barring root; entered at marked stations outside Mecca.

Mu'allaqa: One of the seven golden odes of pre-Islamic Arabia, prized above ordinary verse; Antara's and Labid's are cited above.

Ismah: Protection from sin and error; the attribute the school affirms of the Prophet ﷺ and the Imams ﵈.

Mudd: A classical measure of volume, roughly three-quarters of a liter; the per-person scale of the poor-feeding that clears an oath.

Amends (kaffara): The act the Qur’an sets for clearing an oath, most often feeding ten poor people; clothing them, freeing a slave, or fasting serve when means are lacking.

Final Verdict

At Quran 66:1 the Prophet ﷺ issued no ruling and broke none. He barred a lawful thing from himself under oath, and the God who had already ordained the release of oaths invited him into it, and he entered it, feeding ten poor persons, a mudd each. The verbs of the verse were the poets' verbs before they were the jurists': Antara's gazelle is lawful to one man and forbidden to another in the same breath, and the poets barred, guarded, and surveyed partitions without ever issuing one. The Imam's ﵇ ruling fixes that grammar in law: the tahrim of a man binds only the man, and where it presumes to recast what God made lawful, the presumption is a lie. The Prophet's ﷺ forbidding presumed nothing; he abstained, he swore, and he was released. The blame of the sura falls on two wives who carried a confidence abroad, and the address that opened with a question closes over the Prophet ﷺ with forgiveness and mercy. On the school's reading the verse is the protection at work: revelation intervened the moment a private abstention threatened to read as precedent, and the lesson it published stands for everyone after him, that no vow of any man, the Prophet's ﷺ own included, can narrow what God has left open.
