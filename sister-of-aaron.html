Why Maryam Is Called the Sister of Aaron

Maryam's ﵍ father was Imran and her mother Hanna. Vowed to the sanctuary before her birth, she was raised by Zakariyya ﵇, the husband of her sister, who won her charge by lot. Her Imran is not the Amram of the Torah, and sister of Aaron names kinship, not womb sisterhood. From the Ahl al-Bayt tafsir, al-Kafi, Lisan al-Arab, and the Gospel of Luke.

The Argument Under Examination

When her people confront Maryam ﵍ with her infant, the Qur'an gives their address, the sister of Aaron, joined to a defense of her parents' honor (Qur'an 19:27-28). Aaron the prophet, brother of Moses ﵇, lived about a thousand years before her. Christian critics say the Qur'an has confused two women, Maryam ﵍ the mother of Isa ﵇ and Miriam the sister of Moses ﵇, and rolled them into one. If she was not Aaron's ﵇ sister, the charge concludes, the verse should never have said sister.

The surah's own story answers the family questions in order. Her father was Imran, her mother Hanna. Her mother vowed the child in her womb to the sanctuary, the custodians cast lots for her care, and the lot fell to Zakariyya ﵇, the husband of her sister. Her father is not the Amram who fathered Moses ﵇ and Aaron ﵇ a thousand years earlier; the two men share one ancient name, carried by the custom of naming children after the righteous. Sister of Aaron rides the same custom and the language's kinship grammar. The school's recorded readings, the Prophet's ﷺ own answer at Najran, the lexicons, the poetry they preserve, and the Gospel's kinship words all carry it.

The Imams ﵈, the twelve of the Ahl al-Bayt whom the school follows, read the verse this way from the beginning. Ali ibn Ibrahim al-Qummi (d.329) narrates the scene and explains the address inside it. Al-Sharif al-Murtada (d.436) gave a session of his Amali to the readings of the verse. The objection reached the Prophet ﷺ in his own lifetime from the Christians of Najran, and his answer has chains in the Sunni and the Shia books alike.

Premise 1: Maryam's ﵍ father was Imran and her mother Hanna.

In al-Kafi, Imam al-Sadiq ﵇ reports Allah's promise to Imran of a messenger son, and that Imran told his wife Hanna, she being the mother of Maryam. Al-Hilli (d.726) names the couple the same way (Idah al-Mukhalafa, p.204). The Qur'an opens her birth story with this woman's vow (Qur'an 3:35) and calls Maryam the daughter of Imran (Qur'an 66:12), naming the man of her own century.

Premise 2: She was vowed to the sanctuary, and Zakariyya ﵇, the husband of her sister, won her charge by lot.

Her mother vowed the child as a muharrar, dedicated to the mosque for life. The custodians cast lots over her care, and the lot fell to Zakariyya, whom the school's report identifies as the husband of her sister (Nur al-Thaqalayn, vol.1, p.336). The Qur'an itself records the casting of the pens (Qur'an 3:44) and Zakariyya's charge (Qur'an 3:37). Guardianship by vow and lot leaves paternity where the Qur'an puts it, with Imran.

Premise 3: Her Imran is not the Amram of the Torah.

The Torah's Amram fathered Aaron ﵇ and Moses ﵇ in the Exodus generation, a millennium and more before Maryam. Her father is the Imran of Zakariyya's century. The names are one name recurring, by the custom the Prophet ﷺ gave at Najran, that the Children of Israel named their children after their prophets and the righteous. The Bible never names Mary's father.

Premise 4: Sister of Aaron is a kinship address, and no recorded reading takes it for a womb sisterhood.

The school's exegetes recorded the readings. A righteous namesake of her own people, with the Najran answer behind it. A woman of Aaron's ﵇ line, stated by al-Murtada among the readings and carried by Muqatil from the Prophet ﷺ. A righteous brother who shared neither of her parents. On none of them does the verse name a womb-sister of Moses' ﵇ brother, and that is the only reading the confusion charge needs.

Conclusion: No recorded reading makes the verse name a womb-sister of Aaron the prophet.

From Premise 1 and Premise 2, the family is fixed. Her father Imran, her mother Hanna, her guardian the husband of her sister, chosen by the sanctuary's lot. From Premise 3, the father is a man of her own century whose ancient name the custom explains. From Premise 4, the address carries kinship in the language's own grammar, the Imams' ﵈ usage, the lexicons, the poetry they preserve, and the Gospel's kinship words. The charge needs sister to mean one womb, and no part of the evidence gives it that rule.

- Al-Huwayzi, Nur al-Thaqalayn, vol.3, p.333

The report attests that the verse was heard and tested within the Prophet's ﷺ lifetime by Christians who knew the scriptures the charge draws on. It also preserves the answer in the Prophet's own words. The Children of Israel used to attach their children to their prophets and righteous men, and an address by a saint's name follows that custom. The same report stands in Sahih Muslim's Kitab al-Adab, among the most strictly graded Sunni collections. The records of the exchange in both communities' books carry it from al-Mughira.

Al-Murtada's Amali keeps the exchange in the school's own sitting, where the Prophet's ﷺ rebuke is softened into a lesson. When the Messenger of Allah sent al-Mughira to the people of Najran, they said his Prophet claimed Aaron was the brother of Moses ﵇, when Allah knows the years between Moses and Jesus. Al-Mughira did not know what to answer until he returned. The Prophet ﷺ asked him why he had not said that they used to name themselves after their prophets and the righteous before them (al-Murtada, Amali, vol.2, p.197). The envoy's silence on the spot is the report's own detail. The answer he carried back named a custom checkable in the objectors' own scripture.

Open question: what sister of Aaron means

Claim: the address names a woman of Aaron's ﵇ line the way an Arab is named by his tribe

ومنها أن يكون معنى قوله : يا أُخْتَ هارُونَ يا من هي من نسل هارون أخي موسى ؛ كما يقال للرجل : يا أخا بنى تميم ، ويا أخا بنى فلان

"It may also be that 'O sister of Aaron' means, O you who are of the line of Aaron, the brother of Moses. This is as one says to a man, 'O brother of the Banu Tamim,' or 'O brother of the Banu so-and-so.'"

- Al-Murtada, Amali, vol.2, p.197

The reading stands on al-Murtada's page with the tribe parallel in his own hand, sister of Aaron read as a woman of the line, addressed the way an Arab is addressed by his tribe. He states it among the readings without closing the session on it alone. The idiom was ordinary Arabic to a first-rank scholar of the school.

Source: Muqatil ibn Sulayman (d.150 AH), in al-Murtada, Amali, vol.2, p.197

Source: from the Prophet ﷺ

"Muqatil ibn Sulayman, on Allah's word 'O sister of Aaron,' said, it is reported from the Prophet that he said, 'The Aaron they mentioned is Aaron, the brother of Moses.'"

"Muqatil explained, 'O sister of Aaron' means O you who are of the line of Aaron, as Allah says, 'And to Ad, their brother Hud,' and 'And to Thamud, their brother Salih.' By 'their brother' He means one of their descendants and their kind."

- In al-Murtada, Amali, vol.2, p.197

The report grants the charge its identification. The Aaron of the address is Aaron the prophet ﵇, brother of Moses ﵇. And the Prophet's ﷺ own explanation, as Muqatil transmits it, is that brother-of in the Qur'an's mouth means one of their descendants and their kind, exactly the Hud and Salih construction he cites.

Muqatil's standing is the obvious objection. He is an early transmitter whom the later critics graded harshly, and a hostile reader will say so; the school's case does not lean on him. Al-Murtada, the hardest critic of reports his generation produced, cites him as one reading among several. Every other reading reaches the same ground by another road, through the Qur'an's own usage, the naming custom from Najran, the lexicons, and the living speech. If Muqatil falls, the idiom stands on the rest; if he holds, he carries the Prophet's ﷺ own sentence for it.

Claim: a righteous Harun of her people

- Al-Murtada, Amali, vol.4, p.106

Forty thousand men named Harun at one funeral is a report's way of saying the name filled the city. Behind the number stands a people who named their sons after the saints of the line so consistently that a righteous Harun could die with a crowd of his namesakes behind his bier. A woman of that people, addressed by the same name, is being measured against the house's standard of piety.

"The believing women of the Children of Israel came forward and spat in Maryam's face. She did not speak to them until she entered her prayer-niche. Then the Children of Israel and Zakariyya came to her. They said, 'Maryam, you have come with a thing unheard of, a grave forbidden thing. O sister of Aaron, your father was not an evil man, and your mother was not wanton.'"

"By 'sister of Aaron' they meant that Aaron was an immoral man and an adulterer, and they likened her to him. Where did this affliction you have brought come from, and the shame you have fastened on the Children of Israel?"

- Tafsir al-Qummi, vol.2, p.50

On this reading the crowd named no genealogy of hers. They cut her with a comparison to a notorious man of their own generation, a Harun known for sin. The address is an insult, and an insult needs a reference the audience shares. A Harun of their own day gives them one, and the verse reports their words without endorsing their sneer. On this reading too, the thousand years the confusion charge needs are gone, since a contemporary Harun stands inside Maryam's ﵍ own world. Al-Qummi's Harun is a wicked man and the exegetes' Harun is a saint, and the disagreement between the reports is real. The two readings still share one presupposition, that the name Harun sat on many men of her people, and that breadth of the name is what the naming custom rests on. A name forty thousand men carried sat on sinners and saints alike, and a crowd reaching for the house's name could reach for a notorious namesake or a holy one. The two readings answer different questions, al-Qummi's namesake telling which man the crowd reached for, and Muqatil's report telling whose house the name carries.

Later compilations gather the collected readings, and the Sunni exegetical tradition carries the same range. Al-Farra (d.207), the founder of the Kufan school of Qur'anic grammar, keeps a brother in the picture, one who shares neither of her parents.

"She had a brother named Aaron, one of the best of the Children of Israel, though he was not from her two parents. So it was said, 'sister of Aaron,' for his righteousness. Your brother is righteous, and your parents are your parents."

- Al-Farra, Ma'ani al-Qur'an, vol.2, p.167

The kinship word was already doing lineage work before anyone thought to doubt it.

No recorded reading takes the sister of the address for a womb-sister of the brother of Moses ﵇, and that is the only reading the confusion charge needs. The conclusion is narrower than the polemic and sufficient for the verse. On the Gospel's own kinship words Maryam's ﵍ kin are of the house of Aaron ﵇. Direct descent from Aaron is a recorded reading, plausible without being textually demonstrated. The address itself needs only the kinship grammar of the language and the canon's own kinship words, which the next two sections carry.

A descendant people is a brother people, generation after generation, in the very scriptures the charge appeals to.

The woman's name is Maryam, Miriam, the name of Aaron's ﵇ own sister, and her parents reached into the house's memory and took the sister's name for their daughter. That is the naming custom the Prophet ﷺ gave as his answer at Najran, here inside the house of Aaron itself. The Torah, when it honors Miriam the prophetess, titles her the sister of Aaron ﵇.

6. The Address in the Language

Contested words. The charge reads ukht, sister, as one born of the same parents only. The Imams' ﵈ own usage, the lexicons, and the poetry they preserve fix the word as kinship within a house, covering descendants and tribesmen.

"And to Thamud, their brother Salih."

Hud shares no womb with the whole nation Ad, and Salih shares none with every man of Thamud. The Qur'an sends Shu'ayb to Madyan in the same register (Qur'an 7:85). It calls each prophet the brother of the nation he was sent to because he is of their stock and their kind.

Daughter of Imran shows the kinship word joined to a named individual, for descent. Brother of Ad and brother of Thamud show the kinship word joined to a collective, for belonging. What 19:28 writes, a feminine kinship word joined to the house's named saint, composes those two registers. No independent Arabic example of this exact form, a kinship word joined to one named man, survives in the surveyed record. The two registers are each attested, and the verse's own early exegetes read the composition without strain, Muqatil glossing it at the verse itself and al-Murtada stating it with the Tamim parallel. The Qur'an spends the same words more loosely still. It calls the believers brothers wherever they live (Qur'an 49:10), and the reckless spendthrifts brothers of the devils (Qur'an 17:27), where no blood is in question.

7. The Canon Does Not Pass Its Own Test

Source: the Gospels of Matthew, Luke, and Mark; Helvidius (4th c.); Jerome, Against Helvidius (383); John of Damascus, Homily on the Nativity (8th c.)

Claim: Mary's Davidic line; the Aaronic line of her kinswoman; the perpetual virginity and the brothers of the Lord

Open question: does the Christian canon survive the test it presses on Qur'an 19:28

The charge's rule, that a kinship word means the womb, is the polemicists' own. The Christian canon meets it on the four measures any confusion charge must answer, fidelity to the text, coherence, the ethical cost, and economy.

Christian tradition traces the Lord's family to David, and Matthew opens his Gospel with the claim. Luke builds the Aaronic link into the infancy narrative through Elizabeth, as shown above. Helvidius, writing at Rome before 383, taught that the brothers named in Mark 6:3 were the Virgin's own sons by Joseph, and Jerome reports him so. Jerome answered that they were kin, the sons of Mary the wife of Alphaeus, called Mary of Clopas. He takes her for the sister or kinswoman of the Lord's mother, so that sons of the mother's sister can be called the Lord's brethren (Jerome, Against Helvidius, 383, chs. 14-16). The older tradition holds the Virgin ever virgin, a title the Second Council of Constantinople confessed in 553. John of Damascus (d.749) gives Mary a double lineage in his Homily on the Nativity, Davidic on her father's side, Aaronic on her mother's. The canon's own family register thus runs through the same wide kinship words, and the strongest Christian readings of them are readings of kinship. The kinship words carry the range the charge denies to Arabic.

Verse: Matthew 1:1

Source: Elzevir Textus Receptus, Van Dyck, King James Version

Claim: the Gospel opens the Lord's genealogy with the son of David

βιβλος γενεσεως ιησου χριστου υιου δαβιδ υιου αβρααμ

كِتَابُ مِيلاَدِ يَسُوعَ الْمَسِيحِ ابْنِ دَاوُدَ ابْنِ إِبْراهِيمَ

Biblos geneseōs Iēsou Christou huiou Dauid huiou Abraam

"The book of the generation of Jesus Christ, the son of David, the son of Abraham."

- The Gospel of Matthew 1:1; Greek, Elzevir Textus Receptus; Arabic, Van Dyck; English, King James Version

Son of David is son a thousand years after David, and no Christian tradition has read it as a literal paternity. The canon's own opening verse runs on the genealogical kinship grammar that the charge refuses to Arabic.

On textual fidelity, the Islamic reading keeps every word of 19:28 in its attested range. Ukht stays house-kinship, Aaron stays the house's saint, and the parentage defense stays the address's own frame. The charge must read one kinship word of one book literally. The same book calls a nation the sons of a patriarch a millennium dead and two prophets the brothers of whole nations, and the charge offers no rule that separates them. Its own Numbers joins a kinship word to one named individual, Israel, for a descendant people (Numbers 20:14).

On rational coherence, the charge's rule fails inside the Christian canon itself. Brethren names a nephew in Genesis, a brother nation in Numbers, and the Lord's kin in Mark. A Christian who accepts his own canon's usage has no coherent ground to deny the same range to Arabic. A Christian may answer that his canon is human report while the Qur'an claims to be God's own word, so a blunder in it would be fatal. The answer does not rescue the rule. The charge's claim is about what the Arabic word sister can mean, and that is a fact of the language, whoever is speaking.

On the ethical criterion, the charge asks a reader to believe that the book of God blundered in a genealogy plain on a first reading. The reading asks only that God spoke the kinship grammar the language's speakers, lexicons, and poets actually used, and that the first objectors were answered from the source in the verse's own lifetime.

On economy, the reading needs one settled idiom to carry every exhibit above, while the charge needs a confusion no Muslim transmitter reports, an idiom no dictionary supports, and a rule the canon itself breaks.

The Christian who presses the charge has been pressed by the same test, and his canon does not pass it. The canon that reads son of David across a millennium and brethren across a family network cannot rule that sister across a house is a blunder. The Christian who declines the test, reading his own kinship words by context, has granted the whole method.

Glossary of Key Terms

ukht sister, the feminine of akh, brother. The Arabic kinship words cover descendants and tribesmen of a house, and the address grammar joins them to a people's name.

aal the house or line of a father, as in Aal Imran, the House of Imran, the third surah's title.

mawla a freed slave or client attached to a household, with a bond of patronage the Arabs counted as a form of kinship.

zindiq a skeptic who denies prophethood, the Egyptian debater of al-Kafi.

vocative the grammar of calling, the case that addresses a person directly, ya before the name in Arabic.

honorific marks the small characters after sacred names, ﷺ for the Prophet Muhammad, ﵇ for male saints and Imams, ﵍ for female saints, ﵈ for the Imams together, abbreviating blessings upon them.

Final Verdict

The charge requires the verse to name a womb-sister of Aaron the prophet, and none of the recorded readings does. Her father was Imran and her mother Hanna. Her mother vowed her to the sanctuary, and Zakariyya ﵇, the husband of her sister, won her charge by lot. Her Imran is a man of her own century, not the Amram of the Torah. The two share one name by the custom the Prophet ﷺ gave at Najran, where this objection was first raised and answered by him in his own lifetime. The school's readings give the address a righteous namesake or a house's lineage. The descendant reading is recorded without being established, and the Gospel itself joins Maryam's ﵍ kin to the daughters of Aaron while never calling her Aaron's ﵇ descendant. The language settles what the genealogy leaves open. Kinship words in Arabic, in Hebrew, and in the Qur'an itself name houses and lines, and sister of Aaron is that address.

The canon that presses the literal reading does not carry it at home. Son of David opens its first Gospel, brethren names a nephew in Genesis and a nation in Numbers, and the Lord's own brothers at Nazareth are read by context even inside Christianity. A scripture whose kinship words are read by context has no ground to deny the same reading to another scripture's sister-word. The verse said sister because sister was true, in the language it was said in, to the woman of Aaron's ﵇ house standing before the assembly with her son in her arms.
