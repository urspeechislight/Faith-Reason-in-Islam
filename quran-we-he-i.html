Why the Quran Says We, He, and I

The Quran's We is the Arabic plural of majesty, its He is the same Speaker presenting Himself, and its I is that Speaker at His nearest, shown from the grammarians, the poets, the book's own usage, the school's tafsir, the hadith of the Ahl al-Bayt, and the opponent's own canon.

The Argument Under Examination

The charge comes in two forms, and both travel widely. The first asks it as arithmetic. Your book says We created you, We sent down the Reminder, We built the heaven. A plural speaker is plural, so who are the others helping Allah? The second asks it as confusion. The same book keeps moving between We, He, and I, sometimes inside one passage, and a speaker who knows who he is does not keep changing his own pronouns.

Both forms assume one thing, that an Arabic pronoun counts its referents the way an English pronoun does. It does not. Arabic has a standing plural of majesty, a we that one exalted speaker says of himself alone. The rule is printed in the grammar books, alive in the poets, and used by the Quran itself for single referents of no theological weight at all. When the Quran means that others act alongside Allah, it names them. When it wants the nearness of answer, it drops the relay and speaks as I. When it describes the Lord for the servant to learn of Him, it says He. One Speaker, and many rooms of speech.

The map of the argument runs in the order the evidence builds itself. The grammarians and the lexicon come first. Then the poets and the kings, then the book's own bookkeeping, then the placement of the We beside the book's strictest oneness. Then the named device of the shifts and the named relay of the Speaker, then the hadith of the Ahl al-Bayt showing Allah answering in His own I. Then the Sunni mufassirun agreeing. Last comes the Christian canon, where the method of the charge meets Genesis before it ever reaches the Quran.

Conclusion

The We carries majesty and never number, and no helper hides inside it, because the plural of majesty is ordinary Arabic and the Quran names its co-agents when it has them. The shifts of person follow design, because iltifat is a named device the Imams themselves use. The charge survives only by reading an Arabic pronoun as an English census, and that method convicts Genesis before it reaches the sura of the Reminder.

1. The Grammar and the Lexicon

The case here is addressed to any reader who accepts Arabic dictionaries, grammar, and poetry as evidence. The charge rests on one grammatical assumption, that a pronoun counts its referents. Arabic breaks that assumption before the Quran is even opened. The language has a plural of majesty, nahnu, a we that one exalted speaker says of himself alone. The rule is printed in the grammar books with the Quran's own We as the textbook example.

Contested words. The charge reads nahnu, the Arabic we, as a count of speakers, and the person-shifts as a change of speaker. The grammarians, the lexicon's witnesses, the poets, and the Quran's own usage fix nahnu as register, and the shifts as a named device with named purposes.

The school's senior classical commentator states the rule first. The speech of one speaker can run in the plural form for greatness of station, and he says this is simply how speakers of Arabic talk. His proof text is the charge's own target, the doubled We of the Reminder verse (Qur'an 15:9), and the We of man's creation (Qur'an 23:12). The report he relays cuts both ways, and the honest reading records both edges. Al-Khalil ibn Ahmad, the founder of Arabic grammar, was asked about the plural of the dying soul's plea in Qur'an 23:99, send them back, addressed to the one God. He declined to force a parse and said he does not know its analysis, because it is a plural, and the people of his circle found the reserve good. A reader who wants an anomaly can read the reserve his way. The rule of al-Tusi is the tradition's answer, the plural of rank where the founder paused. It runs in both directions, a creature addressing the one God in the plural and God speaking of Himself in it. Neither direction carries number.

2. How the Kings and Poets Said We

The grammarians state the rule. The lexicographers preserved it alive in speech, and the record is rich. Its honesty is exact. Every human we on exhibit gathers rank around a speaker who has a house behind him, and the divine We gathers rank without needing one. The case of a speaker alone with no house rests with the grammar of section 1 and the kings' custom that al-Razi explains in section 7. The grammarians wrote after the book, and the book is their largest text. The poets and the kings' custom are the evidence no scripture controls, and the verse of the two directions, Qur'an 23:99, is the book meeting the grammarians halfway.

Source: Nashwan al-Himyari (d.573), Shams al-'Ulum, v3, p287

Speaker: Imru' al-Qays, the Jāhiliyya's most famous poet

Claim: the royal we in the poets' mouth, rank without census

نحن الملوك وأبناء الملوك لنا * مُلكٌ به عاش هذا الناسُ أحقابا

We are kings, sons of kings. Our rule has kept these people alive for ages.

Imru' al-Qays, in Nashwan al-Himyari, Shams al-'Ulum, v3, p287

No reader counts the speakers of that line. The line ranks them. And the same art allows a speaker to present himself in the third person when grandeur calls for it, which is the register the Quran enters when it says He of Allah.

The second view is that this verse means Muhammad, since he is the one favored above all others. It says, “He raised some of them in ranks,” as a hint and an allusion. A person may do something great and be asked, “Who did this?” He may answer, “One of you,” or “Some of you,” while meaning himself. This sounds grander than naming himself directly. Al-Hutay'ah was asked who was the greatest poet. He named Zuhayr and al-Nabighah, then said, “If I wished, I could name the third.” He meant himself. His words, “Imru al-Qays shifted,” point to a fourth type, the change from speaking in the first person to addressing someone directly in “your night.” He mentioned these four because they are the most common and famous types. In the science of meanings, this shift differs from what the situation normally requires. In rhetoric, it expresses one meaning through different ways that make it clearer and more vivid. These two aspects give speech its own beauty and eloquence. In literary embellishment, it joins opposite forms within one meaning, so it is a type of meaningful embellishment. The usage is alive and the device has a name. The poets practiced the shifts long before the Quran, and the rhetoricians came later and named what the poets had been doing. Whether the Quran itself keeps this bookkeeping is the next question, and the book answers on its own terms.

3. The Same Act in Two Pronouns

The strongest test of a reading is the book that provoked it. If the We of the Quran counted speakers, the Quran would use its plurals only where referents are many. It does the opposite, and a Shia scholar of the sixth century drew up the evidence.

Source: Ibn Shahrashub (d.588), Mutashabih al-Qur'an, v2, p272

Claim: a catalogue of the Quran's plural forms with single referents, the mosques that is one mosque, the callers that are one caller, the dual addressed to two

The first person is fixed by the grammarians of section 1 and the kings' custom of section 2, and together the two close both doors, the form and the person. The book also describes the same act with both pronouns, and the school's own literature moves between them without effort.

The comparison runs on four named tests. First, what each view asks a believer to accept about God. Second, whether each reading coheres with its own scripture. Third, which reading fits the words themselves. Fourth, which demands less of the text.

On the first test, each reading must square with its own scripture's monotheism, and both books hold the pair, plural forms and strict oneness, without dissolving either. On the second, the Quranic reading is a rule of the language the book speaks, settled by dictionaries, grammars, and usage. The Trinitarian reading of Genesis reads the fourth-century creed back into Hebrew written a millennium before it.

On the third test, the words themselves. Augustine argues from the plural pronouns to plurality of persons, so the number of the pronoun is the load-bearing point, and the Hebrew grammar gives it no such freight. The noun of God stands under singular agreement, and Deuteronomy above shows a plural noun of God standing under one LORD, with nobody reading the morphology as a census. Genesis 1:26's verb is plural in form, like the Arabic nahnu, and that case is carried by the royal we of English, the queen's we are under one speaker, which is the point already made. On the fourth test, the plural of majesty needs no extra persons and no creed to import them, while the Trinitarian reading needs Nicaea to decide what the us means.

The strongest form of the remaining objection deserves its name. Read the king's we as executors hidden inside the pronoun, and the divine We becomes Allah with his angels as his retinue. The book names the retinue wherever it acts, and the named retinue are creatures under command, worshippers who descend by permission and salute the Prophet, which is not the plurality the charge wants. The charge needs helpers of the divine act who share the act's rank. The book gives creatures who carry out the act of the One. The grammar of majesty and the naming rule together leave the retinue standing only as what has already been named, creatures under command, with nothing between creature and Creator inside the pronoun.

The sophisticated form of the Christian reading does not rest on the pronoun at all. It reads Genesis through John's prologue, all things made through him, and through Paul's by him all things were created. That reading carries its agent into the Hebrew from outside it, and the four tests above measure exactly that move. The pronoun charge is the popular form of the case, and the creedal form rests on the creed. A method that convicts the Quran's We convicts Genesis first, and applied with one standard it convicts neither.

Final Verdict

The charge asked three questions, and the record answers each. Does We mean others help Allah. No. Arabic has a plural of majesty, and its grammarians state it with the Quran's own We as the textbook case. The rule runs in both directions, God saying We of Himself and a creature addressing the one God in a plural. The poets and kings used it, the Prophet used it, the Imams used it, and the Quran itself uses plural forms for single referents where no theology is at stake at all. Where helpers truly exist, the book names them, Allah and His angels, the angels and the Spirit by their Lord's permission, and keeps the We to itself. And the school's own reading of 5:55 stands on this grammar, so within the tradition the rule stands at the foundation.

When the Quran says He, who is speaking. Allah is speaking, presenting Himself in the third person the way the rhetoricians name and the Imams practice, and the words travel a named relay. The Quran is Allah's speech placed on the tongue of a noble messenger, Jibril, carried to the Prophet's heart, recited by the Prophet, taught to the servant. The say-verses make the relay visible, and the qudsi Fatiha makes all three persons audible in one report.

Why does the book move between We, He, and I. Because the registers do different work, and the language has a device for moving between them. The We carries majesty in creation and sending down. The He presents the Lord for the servant to learn of Him. The I answers where nearness and mercy are promised, and at Qur'an 2:186 the relay command is dropped so the Lord answers the questioner Himself. The shifts have a name, iltifat, a taxonomy, and stated purposes, and the sermon and prayer life of the Ahl al-Bayt run on the same grammar.

The method that reads number into these pronouns would convict Genesis before it reaches the sura of the Reminder. Held to one standard, the We is majesty, the He is presence taught, and the I is the Lord answering. The helpers the charge went looking for are exactly where the Quran put them, named, permitted, and creatures.
