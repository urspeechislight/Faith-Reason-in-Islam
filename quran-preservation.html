Quranic Integrity

The Preserved Quran

Do the Shia believe the Quran was corrupted? What do their own sources, their scholars, and their Imams say about the text between the two covers?

A charge circulates against the Shia: that their hadith books contain narrations implying the Quran was altered, and therefore the Shia hold the present text to be incomplete. The charge is stated at its strongest: early Shia collections, especially al-Kulayni's al-Kafi, do carry reports in which an Imam ﵇ recites a verse differently from the standard mushaf, calls a word a scribal error, or speaks of a larger revelation. These reports exist. They are not denied. The question is what they establish.

The argument that follows is addressed to readers who accept the Quran as Allah's ﷻ word and the Imams of the Ahl al-Bayt ﵈ as authoritative interpreters. Within that framework, the school's own sources, methods, and scholars converge on one conclusion: the text between the two covers is complete and unaltered.

Two premises frame the answer:

Premise 1: Allah ﷻ Guarantees the Quran's Preservation

Allah ﷻ attaches preservation to Himself in the first person. The promise is explicit and eternal.

﴿إِنَّا نَحْنُ نَزَّلْنَا الذِّكْرَ وَإِنَّا لَهُ لَحَافِظُونَ﴾

Inna nahnu nazzalna al-dhikra wa-inna lahu la-hafizun.

"Indeed, We sent down the Reminder, and indeed We are surely its preservers."

- Quran 15:9

Premise 2: The Imams ﵈ Bound Their Followers to the Received Text

Imam al-Sadiq ﵇ gave a binding instruction when a man recited a variant in his presence:

كف عن هذه القراءة، اقرأ كما يقرأ الناس حتى يقوم القائم

Kuffa 'an hadhihi al-qira'ati, iqra' kama yaqra'u al-nasu hatta yaquma al-Qa'im.

"Desist from this recitation. Recite as the people recite until the Qa'im rises."

- al-Kulayni (d. 329 AH), al-Kafi, vol. 2, p. 633

Conclusion: The Reports of Alteration Cannot Establish Doctrine

If Allah ﷻ guaranteed the text and the Imams ﵈ directed their followers to that same text, then any report attributing corruption to the Quran must overcome both. The reports suggesting alteration are solitary chains through contested narrators. They cannot override the Quran's certainty or the Imams' binding instruction.

The Arabic word tahrif carries a range of meanings, from distortion of interpretation to textual corruption. The Imami scholarly tradition drew a sharp line between these senses and tracked the debate through five distinct historical phases.

What Tahrif Means

The Lexical Definition

The classical Arabic dictionaries define tahrif as bending something away from its proper place. The root h-r-f means "edge" or "side," and the verbal noun means to incline or deflect something from its course.

Ma'rifat (d. 1427 AH) opens his chapter on the linguistic definition in al-Tamhid fi Ulum al-Quran:

التحريف بالشيء إمالته والعدول به عن موضعه إلى جانب، مأخوذ من حرف الشيء بمعنى طرفه وجانبه

Al-taḥrīfu bi'l-shay'i imālatuhu wa'l-ʿudūlu bihi ʿan mauḍiʿihi ilā jānib, ma'khūdhun min ḥarfi al-shay'i bi-maʿnā ṭarafihi wa-jānibihi.

"Tahrif is the inclining of a thing and the turning of it away from its proper place to a side, derived from the edge or side of a thing."

- Ma'rifat (d. 1427 AH), al-Tamhid fi Ulum al-Quran, vol. 8, p. 17; verified from FTS5 corpus

Al-Jawhari (d. 393 AH) in al-Sihah narrows the definition to the specific context of scripture: "تَحْرِيفُ الكَلِمِ عَنْ مَوَاضِعِهِ تَغْيِيرُه" ("Tahrif of words from their proper places is to change them"). Ibn Manẓur (d. 711 AH) in Lisan al-Arab gives the same gloss: "تحريف الكلم عن مواضعه، تغييره" ("Tahrif of words from their proper places is to change them"). The dictionaries define the word as changing a statement's meaning, bending it away from what the speaker intended.

The Quran itself uses the word three times (4:46, 5:13, 5:41), each time accusing the People of the Book of yuharrijuna al-kalima 'an mawadi'ihi ("they twist the words from their proper places"). In every Quranic usage, the accusation is that the meaning was distorted through misinterpretation. The Quran never uses the word to mean physical deletion or addition of text.

The Seven Types

Ayatollah Ma'rifat (d. 2007) and al-Khoei classified tahrif into seven types:

  1. Tahrif of meaning: misinterpreting verses contrary to their intent. Universally affirmed by all Shia and Sunni scholars.
  2. Tahrif of position: rearranging verse or surah order. Denied; the order was set by Prophetic instruction.
  3. Tahrif of pronunciation: variant vocalizations. Denied; these are the recognized qira'at.
  4. Tahrif of variant phrasing: minor word substitutions. Denied.
  5. Tahrif by substitution: replacing one word with another. Contested, generally denied.
  6. Tahrif by addition: adding to the Quran. Universally rejected by all Shia scholars.
  7. Tahrif by omission: removing revealed verses. The core disputed category, denied by the dominant school since al-Saduq.
The Critical Binary

All seven types collapse into one binary: tahrif al-nass (textual corruption) versus tahrif al-ma'na (interpretive distortion). Contemporary Shia scholarship affirms only tahrif al-ma'na, that the Quran's meaning has been distorted by misinterpretation, while denying tahrif al-nass entirely.

Conclusion

The seven types collapse into a single binary, and the school's verdict on each side is clear. Tahrif of meaning is universally affirmed: every scholar agrees the Quran's interpretation has been distorted. Tahrif of text, the category that would implicate the mushaf itself, has been denied by the dominant school since al-Saduq, leaving only a historical minority in dissent.

The Key Tahrif Narrations from al-Kafi

Introduction

The most important Shia hadith collection, al-Kafi by al-Kulayni (d. 329 AH), contains several narrations in Kitab al-Hujja (Book of the Divine Proof) that have historically been cited in the tahrif debate. These reports exist in the books. The question is what they establish, and whether they survive the school's own conditions for establishing doctrine.

The "17,000 Verses" Narration

Imam al-Sadiq ﵇ describes a larger revelation:

إن القرآن الذي جاء به جبريل إلى محمد سبعة عشر ألف آية

Inna al-Qur'āna alladhī jā'a bihi Jibrīl ilā Muḥammad sab'ata 'ashara alfa āyah.

"The Quran that Jibril brought to Muhammad consisted of seventeen thousand verses."

- al-Kulayni (d. 329 AH), Furu' al-Kafi, vol. 4, p. 456

The standard Quran has approximately 6,236 verses. The school's scholars read this narration as referring to the full revelation including its explanatory material and commentary given to the Ahl al-Bayt ﵈, not as evidence that the recited text is missing verses.

"No One Compiled the Quran Except Ali"

Imam al-Baqir ﵇ describes Ali's ﵇ unique compilation:

لا يقولن أحد إنه جمع القرآن كله وما جمعه ولا حفظه كما أنزل إلا علي والأئمة من بعده

Lā yaqūlanna aḥadun annahu jama'a al-Qur'āna kullahu wa-mā jama'ahu wa-lā ḥafaẓahu kamā unzila illā ʿAlī wa'l-a'immatu min ba'dih.

"No one should claim that he compiled the entire Quran. No one has compiled it and memorized it as it was revealed by Allah except Ali ﵇ and the Imams ﵈ after him."

- al-Kulayni (d. 329 AH), al-Kafi, vol. 1, p. 441, Hadith 607

Ali's ﵇ compilation included the full interpretive framework: which verses abrogate which, the circumstances of revelation, and the complete tafsir. The school reads this as referring to the completeness of Ali's ﵇ commentary, since Ali ﵇ endorsed Uthman's codex as sufficient for salvation (see Tab 4).

"Recite as the People Recite Until al-Qa'im Arrives"

Imam al-Sadiq ﵇ directed a follower who recited a variant:

اقرؤوا كما يقرأ الناس حتى يقوم القائم، فإذا قام القائم قرأ كتاب الله عز وجل على وجهه وأخرج المصحف الذي كتبه علي

Iqra'ū kamā yaqra'u al-nāsu ḥattā yaqūma al-Qā'im, fa-idhā qāma al-Qā'im qara'a kitāba Allāhi 'azza wa-jalla 'alā wajhihi wa-akhraja al-muṣḥafa alladhī katabahu ʿAlī.

"Recite as the people recite, until al-Qa'im arrives. When al-Qa'im arrives, he will recite the Book of Allah in its proper manner and he will bring forth the Mushaf written by Ali ﵇."

- al-Kulayni (d. 329 AH), al-Kafi, vol. 4, p. 452

The Imam ﵇ turned his follower away from a private variant and back to the common text. The binding instruction operated for the entire period of the Imam's ﵇ absence: recite as the community recites.

The "Nukat wa Nutaf" Chapter

Al-Kafi contains a chapter titled "Nukat wa Nutaf" (Points and Drops), spanning pages 412-432 of volume 1, with 92 reports. In these narrations, Gabriel is described as bringing down verses with insertions about Ali ﵇ and the Ahl al-Bayt ﵈. For example, Q 2:23 is recited as: "If you are in doubt about what We have revealed unto our servant (concerning Ali), then produce a sura the like thereof." The school's scholars read these as tafsir (the verse concerns Ali ﵇ by interpretation), not as evidence that the phrase "(concerning Ali)" was physically removed from the mushaf.

Conclusion

These narrations exist in al-Kafi. They are acknowledged by the school's scholars. The dominant reading, from al-Saduq through al-Khoei, treats them as reports of interpretive material, commentary, and variant readings that never entered the standard codex. They are solitary chains that cannot override the mutawatir text, and the Imams ﵈ directed their followers to the common recitation in every verified practical instruction.

Nahj al-Balagha: Imam Ali's Testimony

Introduction

Imam Ali's ﵇ sermons in Nahj al-Balagha never claim the Quran is incomplete or altered. They consistently present the Quran as a complete, sufficient, and trustworthy guide.

Sermon 175: The Trustworthy Counsellor

وهذا القرآن ناصح لا يغش، ودليل لا يضل، ومحدث لا يكذب

Wa-hādhā al-Qur'ānu nāṣiḥun lā yaghish, wa-dalīlun lā yaḍill, wa-muḥaddithun lā yakdhib.

"This Quran is an adviser whose counsel is free from deceit, a guide that never leads astray, and a speaker that never lies."

- Imam Ali ibn Abi Talib ﵇, Nahj al-Balagha, Sermon 175
Sermon 151: The Strong Rope

خذوا بكتاب الله فإنه حبل متين، ونور مبين، وشفاء نافع، وري عاصف، وعصمة للمتمسك، ونجاة للمتعلق، لا يعوج فيقام، ولا يزيغ فيستعتب

Khudhū bi-kitābi Allāhi fa-innahu ḥablun matīn, wa-nūrun mubīn, wa-shifā'un nāfi', wa-riyyun 'āṣif, wa-'iṣmatun li'l-mutamasik, wa-najāhun li'l-muta'alliq, lā ya'ūju fa-yuqām, wa-lā yazīghu fa-yusta'tab.

"Take hold of the Book of Allah, for it is a strong rope, a clear light, a beneficial cure, a quenching rain, a protection for whoever clings to it, and a deliverance for whoever holds fast to it. There is no crookedness in it that needs straightening, and no deviation in it that needs correction."

- Imam Ali ibn Abi Talib ﵇, Nahj al-Balagha, Sermon 151
Conclusion

Ali ﵇ describes the Quran as a flawless guide, a strong rope, a clear light, a cure with no crookedness in it. If he believed the text were corrupt or incomplete, his language would reflect that. Instead, every description assumes the Quran is complete and trustworthy. Nahj al-Balagha gives no hint that the Imam ﵇ considered the standard text deficient.

The Five Core Arguments Against Tahrif

Five Independent Lines

The Imami school mounts five independent arguments against textual tahrif. Each is sufficient on its own; together they form a convergent case.

1. Tawatur (mass transmission)

The Quran is transmitted by unbroken chains of mass transmission in every generation. A coordinated conspiracy to alter it across every Muslim city, in every century, is inconceivable. The text is qat'i (certain) by consensus of all Imami scholars.

2. Quranic self-attestation

Allah ﷻ attaches the Quran's preservation to Himself. Two verses carry the weight of the argument:

﴿إِنَّا نَحْنُ نَزَّلْنَا الذِّكْرَ وَإِنَّا لَهُ لَحَافِظُونَ﴾

Innā naḥnu nazzalnā al-dhikra wa-innā lahu la-ḥāfiẓūn.

"Indeed, We sent down the Reminder, and indeed We are surely its preservers."

- Quran 15:9

﴿لَا يَأْتِيهِ الْبَاطِلُ مِن بَيْنِ يَدَيْهِ وَلَا مِنْ خَلْفِهِ تَنزِيلٌ مِّنْ حَكِيمٍ حَمِيدٍ﴾

Lā ya'tīhi al-bāṭilu min bayni yadayhi wa-lā min khalfihi, tanzīlun min ḥakīmin ḥamīd.

"Falsehood cannot come to it from before it or from behind it, a revelation from One all-wise and praiseworthy."

- Quran 41:42

The preservation is Allah's ﷻ own responsibility. If scribes introduced an error, the promise was broken and falsehood entered the Book.

3. Hadith al-Thaqalayn

The Prophet ﷺ told Muslims to hold fast to the Quran and the Ahl al-Bayt ﵈, saying the two would never separate until they returned to him at the Pool. If the Quran were corrupted, this instruction would be self-defeating: the Prophet ﷺ would have bound his community to a corrupted text alongside his family.

4. The Imams used the Quran as criterion

Imam al-Sadiq ﵇ instructed followers to test every narration against the Quran:

إذا ورد عليكم حديثان مختلفان فاعرضوهما على كتاب الله، فما وافق كتاب الله فخذوه، وما خالف كتاب الله فردوه

Idhā warada ʿalaykum ḥadīthāni mukhtalifāni fa-aʿriḍūhumā ʿalā kitābi Allāh, fa-mā wāfaqa kitāba Allāhi fa-khudhūhu, wa-mā khālafa kitāba Allāhi fa-ruddūhu.

"When two differing reports come to you, present them to the Book of Allah. Accept that which agrees with the Book of Allah, and reject that which contradicts it."

- al-Kulayni (d. 329 AH), al-Kafi, vol. 1, p. 56

This instruction would be meaningless if the Quran itself were corrupt. The Imams ﵈ treated the existing text as the functional criterion of truth.

5. Imam Ali's silence during Uthman's compilation

Ali ﵇ openly contested the caliphs on Ghadir Khumm, Fadak, and political legitimacy. He challenged them on matters far less consequential than the corruption of Allah's ﷻ Book. On the compilation of the Quran, he said:

لو لم يصنعه عثمان لصنعته

Law lam yaṣnaʿhu ʿUthmānu la-ṣnaʿtuh.

"Had Uthman not done it, I would have done it myself."

- al-Abyari (d. 1414 AH), al-Mawsu'a al-Qur'aniyya, vol. 3, p. 69

If the text were altered under Uthman's watch, Ali's ﵇ silence is inexplicable. He endorsed the standardization. He never alleged corruption.

Conclusion

Each argument targets a different dimension of the claim: transmission (1), divine promise (2), Prophetic instruction (3), the Imams' practical authority (4), and Ali's ﵇ political conduct (5). The five converge on a single conclusion: the Quran between the two covers is the complete text revealed to the Prophet ﷺ.

The Historical Debate

Five Phases

The debate evolved through five distinct phases across a thousand years, each shift driven by a change in how scholars understood the relationship between hadith and reason.

Phase 1: Early Period (3rd-4th c. AH). The earliest Imami scholars worked as collectors and preservers of hadith, and they recorded what they heard without imposing a doctrinal filter. Al-Kulayni (d. 329 AH) included tahrif narrations in al-Kafi without refuting them, because his project was preservation, not adjudication. Ali ibn Ibrahim al-Qummi's Tafsir and al-Ayyashi's Tafsir follow the same pattern. Al-Sayyari's Kitab al-Qira'at is the earliest extant Shia work implying textual changes. Belief in some form of textual alteration was common, because the rationalist tools to distinguish sound from unsound hadith had not yet been developed.

Phase 2: The Rationalist Turn (Late 4th-5th c. AH). Once the school developed a mature theological apparatus, scholars began testing the narrations against reason, consensus, and the Quran's own claims. Al-Saduq (d. 381 AH) produced the first systematic creedal denial. Al-Mufid (d. 413 AH) took a middle position: nothing was added, but reason does not consider omission impossible. Al-Murtada (d. 436 AH) argued the strongest classical denial, and al-Tusi (d. 460 AH) closed the phase by ruling that addition is unanimously false and the sound position is no deletion either. The shift was decisive: the same narrations remained in the books, but the school now had the tools to weigh and reject them.

Phase 3: Akhbari Revival (10th-12th c. AH). The Akhbari school rejected the rationalist apparatus of Phase 2 and returned to accepting hadith at face value, which naturally reopened the question. Fayd al-Kashani (d. 1091 AH) compiled nine pages of tahrif narrations in Tafsir al-Safi, then immediately added that the correct view of the sect is against that. Al-Majlisi (d. 1110 AH) called some traditions mutawatir in one work and akhbar ahad in another. The revival produced intense attention to the narrations, yet even its leading figures could not commit to affirming textual corruption as settled doctrine.

Phase 4: Fasl al-Khitab (c. 1298 AH / 1881 CE). Mirza Husayn Nuri (d. 1320 AH) produced the most comprehensive pro-tahrif work ever written: over one thousand traditions across four hundred pages. The book caused immediate controversy because the school's theological consensus had already settled the question centuries earlier. Scholars at the hawza of Samarra heavily criticized it, and Khomeini reportedly called it a collection of jokes with no academic value. Nuri's project backfired: by forcing the school to confront the narrations in bulk, it triggered the most thorough refutations the question had ever received.

Phase 5: 20th-21st Century. Every major modern marja' affirms complete Quran preservation. Tabataba'i, al-Khoei, Khomeini, Sistani, and Ma'rifat all reject textual tahrif decisively, and each has produced detailed arguments tracing the problematic narrations to weak chains or reinterpretable meanings. The thousand-year arc runs from unfiltered collection through rationalist denial, Akhbari revival, and comprehensive debate to a near-universal consensus that the text between the two covers is complete.

Conclusion

The five phases show a clear trajectory: the school began by collecting what it received, then developed the theological tools to test it, and ultimately concluded that the problematic narrations could not survive those tests. The Akhbari revival and Nuri's massive compilation each momentarily reopened the question, and each reopening produced a more thorough refutation. The thousand-year arc arrives at a near-universal consensus that the text is complete.

Al-Saduq's Creed

Introduction

Al-Saduq (Ibn Babawayh, d. 381 AH) produced the first systematic creedal denial of textual tahrif in the Imami tradition. His al-I'tiqadat fixed the school's position for every subsequent generation of scholars.

The Creed

Al-Saduq wrote in his creedal treatise:

اعتقادنا أن القرآن الذي أنزله الله تعالى على نبيه محمد (﵌) هو ما بين الدفتين وهو ما في أيدي الناس ليس بأكثر من ذلك. ومن نسب إلينا أنا نقول أنه أكثر من ذلك فهو كاذب

I'tiqaduna anna al-Qur'ana alladhi anzalahu Allahu ta'ala 'ala nabiyyihi Muhammadin salla Allahu 'alayhi wa-alihi huwa ma bayna al-daffatayn wa-huwa ma fi aydi al-nasi laysa bi-akthara min dhalik. Wa-man nasaba ilayna anna naqulu annahu aktharu min dhalika fa-huwa kadhib.

"Our belief is that the Quran which Allah, exalted be He, sent down upon His Prophet Muhammad ﷺ is what is between the two covers, and it is what is in people's hands, nothing more. Whoever attributes to us that we say it is more than that is a liar."

- al-Saduq (d. 381 AH), al-I'tiqadat, pp. 92-93
Conclusion

Al-Saduq was the first Imami scholar to fix the complete Quran as a matter of creed, elevating it above scholarly disagreement. His formula, "what is between the two covers and what is in people's hands," became the baseline every later theologian either affirmed or refined. By branding the opposing claim a lie, he placed denial of tahrif firmly within the school's identity.

Al-Murtada's Position

Introduction

Al-Sharif al-Murtada (d. 436 AH) mounted the strongest classical denial of textual tahrif. He argued from three grounds: tawatur (mass transmission), rational necessity, and the rejection of solitary reports (akhbar ahad) in matters of creed.

The Cities Argument

Al-Murtada placed certainty in the Quran's transmission on the level of certainty about the existence of famous cities:

إن العلم بصحة نقل القرآن كالعلم بالبلدان والحوادث العظام والكتب المشهورة وأشعار العرب

Inna al-'ilma bi-sihhati naqli al-Qur'ani ka'l-'ilmi bi'l-buldani wa'l-hawadithi al-'izami wa'l-kutubi al-mashhurati wa-ash'ari al-'arab.

"Knowledge of the correctness of the Quran's transmission is like knowledge of cities, great historical events, famous books, and the poetry of the Arabs."

- al-Murtada (d. 436 AH), Jawabat al-Masa'il al-Tarabulusiyyat

Ibn Hazm records that al-Murtada deemed infidels whoever maintained the belief in the distortion of the Quran. For al-Murtada, to deny the Quran's textual integrity was to deny the fulfillment of Allah's ﷻ promise.

Conclusion

Al-Murtada placed the Quran's textual certainty on the same epistemic footing as the reality of known cities and recorded events. No sane person doubts that Mecca exists or that great battles occurred, because the evidence is too widespread and too multiply attested to be fabricated. Al-Murtada argued the Quran's transmission carries this same self-evident force, and therefore no solitary narration can dent it.

Al-Khoei's Refutation

Introduction

Ayatollah al-Khoei (d. 1413 AH) produced the most comprehensive modern treatment of the tahrif question in al-Bayan fi Tafsir al-Quran. He classified the narrations by chain, identified their sources, and rejected the claim of textual corruption on rational and textual grounds.

The Classification

Al-Khoei traced roughly two-thirds of all tahrif narrations to al-Sayyari, whom he identified as corrupt in doctrine. Another quarter trace to Furat al-Kufi, absent from all early Shia rijal works. The remaining narrations, he argued, point to tahrif of meaning, of text:

إن حديث تحريف القرآن حديث خرافة وخيال، لا يقول به إلا من ضعف عقله، أو من لم يتأمل في أطرافه حق التأمل

Inna haditha tahrifi al-Qur'ani hadithu khurafatin wa-khayal, la yaqulu bihi illa man da'ufa 'aqluhu, aw man lam yata'ammal fi atrafihi haqqa al-ta'ammul.

"The claim of the alteration of the Quran is a tale of fable and fantasy. None asserts it except one whose reason is weak, or one who has not examined its aspects with proper reflection."

- al-Khoei (d. 1413 AH), al-Bayan fi Tafsir al-Quran, p. 220
Conclusion

Al-Khoei's classification dismantled the evidentiary basis of the tahrif claim at the chain level. By tracing the vast majority of narrations to two unreliable sources and showing that the remainder point to interpretive distortion, he demonstrated that the claim rests on reports whose chains and meanings have never been properly examined. The verdict reframes the debate as a failure of method, where reports were cited without investigating their foundations.

The word mushaf is a generic Arabic term meaning a bound book or codex of pages between two covers. Its specialization as a synonym for the Quranic codex was a later cultural development driven by the dominance of the Uthmanic codices.

The Root and Pattern

Introduction

The root s-h-f carries the primary meaning of breadth, expanse, and flatness. Ibn Faris fixes this sense in Maqayis al-Lughah (vol. 3, p. 334): the root conveys breadth and openness. From this root comes sahifa (page, leaf, written sheet), suhuf (pages, scriptures), and the derived noun mushaf.

The Morphology

The word mushaf is the passive participle (ism maf'ul) of the Form IV verb ashhafa, meaning to gather pages into a bound volume between covers. The passive ushifa is the direct derivation: "it was made to contain gathered pages." Ibn Manzhur's Lisan al-Arab (d. 711 AH) records the classical morphological analysis:

إنما سمي المصحف مصحفاً لأنه أُصْحِفَ، أي جُعِلَ جامعاً للصحف المكتوبة بين الدفتين. قال الفراء: يقال مُصْحَفٌ ومِصْحَفٌ كما يقال مُطْرَفٌ ومِطْرَفٌ؛ وقوله مُصْحَفٌ من أُصْحِفَ أي جُمِعَتْ فيه الصحف. استثقلت العرب الضمة في حروف فكسرت الميم، وأصلها الضمُّ.

Innama summiya al-mushaf mushafan li-annahu ushifa, ayy ju'ila jami'an li'l-suhuf al-maktuba bayna al-daffatayn. Qala al-Farra': yuqalu mushafun wa-mishafun kama yuqalu mutrafun wa-mitrafun; wa-qawluhu mushafun min ushifa ayy jumi'at fihi al-suhuf. istathqalat al-'arab al-dammata fi hurufin fa-kasarat al-mim, wa-asluha al-damm.

"It is called mushaf because it was ushifa, meaning it was made a gathering for the written pages between the two covers. Al-Farra' said: one says mushaf and mishaf, just as one says mutraf and mitraf. Mushaf is from ushifa, meaning the suhuf were gathered in it. The Arabs found the damma heavy in these letters and so broke the mim with kasra; its original is the damma."

- Ibn Manzhur (d. 711 AH), Lisan al-Arab, s.v. s h f
Three Dialectal Variants

Al-Fayruzabadi's al-Qamus al-Muhit (d. 817 AH) records three vocalizations of the word, all from the same root with the same meaning:

مُصْحَفُ ومِصْحَفُ ومَصْحَفُ من أُصْحِفَ: أي جُعِلَتْ فيه الصُّحُفُ. وصحيفةُ: الكتابُ، ج: صَحَائِفُ وصُحُفٌ. وتَصْحِيفُ: الخَطَأُ في الصَّحيفَةِ.

Mushafu wa-mishafu wa-mashafu min ushifa: ayy ju'ilat fihi al-suhuf. Wa-sahifatu: al-kitabu, jam': saha'ifu wa-suhuf. Wa-tashif: al-khata' fi al-sahifa.

"Mushaf, mishaf, and mashaf: from ushifa, meaning the suhuf were placed and gathered in it. Sahifa means a book; plural saha'if and suhuf. Tashif means an error in reading the sahifa."

- al-Fayruzabadi (d. 817 AH), al-Qamus al-Muhit, s.v. s h f

Al-Azhari (d. 370 AH) confirms in Tahdhib al-Lugha that the word describes a container, a physical format:

إنما سمي مصحفاً لأنه جُعل وعاء للصحف المكتوبة بين الدفتين

Innama summiya mushafan li-annahu ju'ila wi'a'an li'l-suhuf al-maktuba bayna al-daffatayn.

"It is called mushaf because it was made a receptacle (wi'a) for the written pages between the two covers."

- al-Azhari (d. 370 AH), Tahdhib al-Lugha
Conclusion

The morphological evidence is unambiguous: mushaf is a passive participle derived from a root about breadth and flatness, meaning pages gathered and bound between covers. The classical dictionaries define the word purely as a physical object, a receptacle for written pages, with no inherent connection to divine scripture.

Mushaf as a Generic Word for Any Book

The Lexical Meaning: Any Bound Book

Sayyid Murtada al-Askari (d. 1428 AH) wrote a dedicated study of the word mushaf. His conclusion, from the lexical and historical evidence, is direct:

وبناءً على ما ذكرنا: إنّ المصحف اسم لكلّ كتاب مجلد قرآناً كان أم غير قرآن

Wa-binā'an ʿalā mā dhakarnā: inna al-muṣḥafa ismun li-kulli kitābin mujallad, Qur'ānan kāna am ghayra Qur'ān.

"Based on what we have mentioned: the word mushaf is a name for every bound book, whether it is the Quran or something other than the Quran."

- al-Askari (d. 1428 AH), al-Mushaf fi al-Riwayat wa'l-Athar, p. 10; verified from FTS5 corpus

Al-Askari documents that the Companions themselves used mushaf in its lexical sense during the collection of the Quran. He cites al-Bukhari's report from Zayd ibn Thabit, where the early collection was called ṣuḥuf (loose pages), not muṣḥaf, until the pages were bound. The word described the physical format, not the sanctity of the content.

The Quran Uses Suhuf for Earlier Scriptures

The Quran itself (87:18-19) uses the root for revelations given to Abraham and Moses, centuries before the Quranic codex:

﴿وَإِنَّهُ لَفِي صُحُفِ الْأُولَىٰ ۝ صُحُفِ إِبْرَاهِيمَ وَمُوسَىٰ﴾

Wa-innahu la-fī ṣuḥufi al-ūlā, ṣuḥufi Ibrāhīma wa-Mūsā.

"And this is indeed in the scrolls of the ancients, the scrolls of Abraham and Moses."

- Quran 87:18-19

The word ṣuḥuf was understood generically: any written pages of divine guidance, not exclusively the Quran. The Quran also uses the root in 20:133, 53:36-37, 74:52, and 81:10, each time for scriptures or written records in general.

The Baghdad Verse

A famous line of classical poetry, attributed to Abd al-Wahhab b. Ali (d. 422 AH) and recorded by Ibn Khallikan (d. 681 AH), Ibn Butlan (d. 455 AH), and al-Kutubi (d. 764 AH), uses mushaf for a book sitting unwanted in a heretic's house:

بغداد دارٌ لأهل المال طيبة / وللمفاليس دار الضنك والضيق / ظللت حيران أمشي في أزقتها / كأنني مصحف في بيت زنديق

Baghdādu dārun li-ahl al-māli ṭayyibatun, wa-lil-mafālīsi dār al-ḍanki wa'l-ḍayq. Ẓalaltu ḥayran amshī fī aziqqatihā, ka-annanī muṣḥafun fī bayti zindīq.

"Baghdad is a pleasant home for the wealthy; for the destitute, a house of hardship and constriction. I wandered lost, walking through its alleys, as though I were a mushaf in the house of a zindiq (heretic)."

- Ibn Khallikan (d. 681 AH), Wafayat al-A'yan, vol. 3, p. 217; Ibn Butlan (d. 455 AH), Da'wat al-Atibba', p. 24; verified from FTS5 corpus

A zindiq is a heretic or free-thinker. A heretic would not keep a Quran in his house, much less prize it. The metaphor works because mushaf means simply a book: a book that sits unread and unwanted. The poet uses the word in its generic lexical sense.

The Root in Pre-Islamic Poetry

The root ṣ-ḥ-f (flatness, breadth) was established in Jāhilī Arabic for written pages and flat surfaces. Classical scholarship records that ṣaḥīfa (page, leaf, written sheet) appears in the poetry of al-Mutalammis, Labid, and Aws b. Hajar, all pre-Islamic poets. The word ṣuḥuf appears in their verses for written documents, tribal records, and correspondence. The root produced ṣiḥāf (large flat bowls, Quran 43:71) from the same semantic field of breadth and flatness.

Conclusion

The word mushaf means a bound book. The lexicons, the Quran's own usage of the root, and classical poetry all confirm this. The word's later specialization as a synonym for the Quranic codex was a cultural development driven by usage, not a change in the word's lexical meaning. When the Imams ﵈ spoke of the "Mushaf of Fatimah" ﵍, they used the word in its lexical sense: a bound book belonging to Fatimah ﵍, containing no part of the recited Quran.

The Specialization of the Term

Introduction

The term mushaf became associated primarily with the Quran only after Uthman's compilation (25-30 AH), when the Uthmanic codices became the standard reference text. As Von Denffer notes in Ulum al-Quran (p. 36): "Suhuf stands for the sheets on which the Quran was collected in the time of Abu Bakr. Mushaf in the present context means the sheets on which the Quran was collected in the time of Uthman."

The Modern Dictionary

The modern dictionary al-Mu'jam al-Wasit (Cairo Academy of Arabic Language) confirms this explicitly:

المُصْحَف: مجموعٌ من الصُّحُف في مجلَّد؛ وغلب استعماله في القرآن الكريم

Al-mushaf: majmu'un min al-suhuf fi mujallad; wa-ghalaba istimaluhu fi al-Qur'an al-Karim.

"A collection of suhuf in a bound volume; and its usage became predominant (ghalaba) for the Holy Quran."

- al-Mu'jam al-Wasit (Cairo Academy of Arabic Language)

The word ghalaba signals a conventional specialization on top of the generic lexical meaning. The dictionary does not define mushaf as "the Quran." It defines it as "a bound volume of pages" and notes that this generic term became predominantly associated with the Quran through cultural usage.

Conclusion

Mushaf means a bound book, any bound book. Its specialization as a synonym for the Quranic codex was a post-Uthmanic cultural development, acknowledged by the modern dictionaries with the word ghalaba (became predominant). The presence of the word mushaf in Shia hadith for the Mushaf of Fatimah ﵍ or the Mushaf of Ali ﵇ does not establish a parallel Quran. It establishes a bound book.

The Poetry Evidence

Introduction

A famous line of classical poetry uses mushaf in its generic lexical sense and settles the question of whether the word can mean exclusively the Quran.

The Verse

A poet wandering lost through Baghdad compared himself to a book sitting unread in a heretic's home:

بغداد دارٌ لأهل المال طيبة / وللمفاليس دار الضنك والضيق / ظللت حيران أمشي في أزقتها / كأنني مصحف في بيت زنديق

Baghdadu dārun li-ahl al-māli tayyibatun, wa-lil-mafālīsi dār al-ḍanki wa'l-ḍayq. Zalaltu ḥayran amshī fī aziqqatihā, ka-annanī muṣḥafun fī bayti zindīq.

"Baghdad is a pleasant home for the wealthy; for the destitute, a house of hardship and constriction. I wandered lost, walking through its alleys, as though I were a mushaf in the house of a zindiq (heretic)."

- Ibn Butlan (d. 455 AH), Da'wat al-Atibba'; also Ibn Bassam (d. 542 AH), al-Dhakhirah; al-Kutubi (d. 764 AH), Fawat al-Wafiyat

A zindiq is a heretic or free-thinker. A heretic would keep no Quran in his house. The metaphor works because mushaf means simply a book, a book that sits unread and unwanted in a heretic's home. The poet uses the word in its generic lexical sense.

Conclusion

The poet's metaphor works only because mushaf means a generic book. A zindiq, by definition, rejects organized religion and would keep no Quran in his home. The verse settles the lexical question with a single image: a book sitting unread on a heretic's shelf, and the word for that book is mushaf.

Mushaf of Fatimah: Both Hadiths

Introduction

The Imams ﵈ kept writings the community did not have. The most famous is the Mushaf of Fatimah ﵍. Two narrations from al-Kafi define its contents and distinguish it from the Quran in the clearest terms.

Hadith 1: Three Times the Size, No Quran in It

Imam al-Sadiq ﵇ explicitly distinguished the Mushaf of Fatimah ﵍ from the Quran:

مصحف فيه مثل قرآنكم هذا ثلاث مرات، والله ما فيه من قرآنكم حرف واحد... إنه لعلم وما هو بذاك

Mushafun fihi mithlu qur'anikum hadha thalatha marrat, wa-Allahi ma fihi min qur'anikum harfun wahid... innahu la-'ilmun wa-ma huwa bi-dhalik.

"A codex, in it the like of this Quran of yours three times over. And by God, in it there is not one single letter of your Quran. It is knowledge indeed, but it is not that."

- al-Kulayni (d. 329 AH), al-Kafi, vol. 1, p. 287

The Imam ﵇ defines the Mushaf of Fatimah ﵍ and empties it of Quran by explicit oath. It contains angelic communications, predictions of future events, and the names of future rulers. The word mushaf means exactly what it says lexically: a bound book.

Hadith 2: No Lawful or Unlawful, Only Future Knowledge

The second narration from the same chapter clarifies the Mushaf's ﵍ contents:

أما إنه ليس فيه شيء من الحلال والحرام ولكنه فيه علم ما يكون

Amma innahu laysa fihi shay'un min al-halali wa'l-harami wa-lakinnahu fihi 'ilmu ma yakun.

"There is in it nothing of the lawful and unlawful (halal and haram), but it contains knowledge of what will happen."

- al-Kulayni (d. 329 AH), al-Kafi, Kitab al-Hujja, Bab 40, Hadith 2

The Mushaf of Fatimah ﵍ contains no legal rulings. It is a book of private knowledge given to Fatimah ﵍ after her father's ﷺ death: predictions of future events and the names of future rulers. It is a parallel source of knowledge, not a parallel scripture for recitation.

Conclusion

The Imam's ﵇ own definition empties the Mushaf of Fatimah ﵍ of any Quranic content. It contains no legal rulings, and by the Imam's ﵇ explicit oath, not a single letter of the Quran. The word mushaf applies to it because mushaf means a bound book, and this particular book carries private revelation given to Fatimah ﵍ after her father's ﷺ death.

The strongest narration cited by proponents of tahrif is the report where Imam al-Sadiq ﵇ calls the dual form in 5:95 a scribal error. This narration has the strongest chain of any tahrif report in the Shia corpus, running through universally trusted narrators. Tested against the Imami school's own conditions for establishing doctrine, the narration fails on multiple independent grounds.

The Hadith

Introduction

The report from al-Kafi vol. 8 records Hammad b. 'Uthman reciting Quran 5:95 in the presence of Imam al-Sadiq ﵇. The verse uses the dual form "dhawa adlin minkum" (two persons of justice from among you). The Imam corrects the dual to the singular "dhu adlin minkum" and attributes the difference to a scribal error.

The Report

The full chain and text, verified from the FTS5 corpus:

علي بن إبراهيم، عن أبيه، عن ابن أبي عمير، عن حماد بن عثمان قال: تلوت عند أبي عبد الله (﵇) «ذوا عدل منكم» فقال: «ذو عدل منكم، هذا مما أخطأت فيه الكتاب»

'Ali b. Ibrahim, 'an abihi, 'an Ibn Abi 'Umayr, 'an Hammad b. 'Uthman qala: talawtu 'inda Abi 'Abd Allah (alayhi al-salam) "dhawa 'adlin minkum," fa-qala: "dhu 'adlin minkum, hadha mimma akhata't fihi al-kuttab."

"'Ali b. Ibrahim reported from his father, from Ibn Abi 'Umayr, from Hammad b. 'Uthman, who said: I recited in the presence of Abu Abd Allah (al-Sadiq ﵇): 'Two just persons from among you.' He said: 'A person of justice from among you. This is from what the scribes erred in.'"

- al-Kulayni (d. 329 AH), al-Kafi, vol. 8, p. 229, Hadith 247

The chain runs through Ibn Abi 'Umayr, one of the most universally trusted narrators in the entire Shia tradition, and Hammad b. 'Uthman, who is generally reliable. This is a stronger chain than most tahrif narrations. The rejection must therefore rest on substantive grounds: the school's conditions for establishing creed, the principle of mu'arada, the later Imam rule, and the reinterpretable nature of the phrase "the scribes erred."

Conclusion

The chain through Ibn Abi 'Umayr is stronger than most tahrif narrations, which means the rejection cannot rest on narrator weakness. The school must instead apply its substantive doctrinal conditions: the principle of mu'arada, the rule on solitary reports, and the distinction between tahrif of text and tahrif of meaning.

Khabar Wahid Cannot Establish Creed

Introduction

A khabar al-wahid (solitary report) is any narration that does not reach the threshold of tawatur (mass transmission). Even if every narrator in its chain is perfectly trustworthy, a solitary report conveys only zann (probability). Each narrator, however honest, can mishear, forget, conflate, or paraphrase. Only a mutawatir chain, where the sheer number of independent transmitters makes coordinated error impossible, produces yaqin (certainty).

The Principle in Imami Usul

Shaykh al-Tusi (d. 460 AH), known as Shaykh al-Ta'ifa (Leader of the School), stated the principle directly in his introduction to al-Tibyan, his Quran commentary. He was addressing precisely these tahrif reports:

رويت روايات كثيرة من جهة الخاصّة والعامّة بنقصان كثير من آي القرآن ونقل شيء منه من موضع إلى موضع، طريقها الآحاد لا توجب علماً، فالأولى الإعراض عنها وترك التشاغل بها لأنّه يمكن تأويلها

Ruwyat riwayat kathira min jihat al-khassati wa'l-'ammati bi-nuqsani kathirin min ay al-Qur'ani wa-naqli shay'in minhu min mawdi'in ila mawdi', tariquha al-ahadu la tujibu 'ilman, fa-al-awla al-i'ridu 'anha wa-tarku al-tashaghuli biha li-annahu yumkinu ta'wiluha.

"Many reports have been narrated, from our side and from others, claiming loss of much of the Quran's verses and the moving of parts from one place to another. Their route is single chains that yield no certainty. So the better course is to turn away from them and not busy oneself with them, since they can be interpreted."

- al-Tusi (d. 460 AH), al-Tibyan, vol. 1, p. 3; verified from FTS5 corpus

Al-Tusi's ruling operates at two levels. First, he identifies the reports as akhbar ahad (single-chain), meaning each rests on a limited number of narrators. Second, he states the consequence: reports that yield no certainty cannot establish a matter of belief. The integrity of the Quran falls under Nabuwwah, the fulfillment of Allah's ﷻ promise to His Messenger ﷺ that He would guard the revelation. A claim that this promise was violated requires certainty. Probability cannot supply what certainty demands.

Conclusion

The epistemic gap between a solitary report and the mutawatir Quran is absolute. The Quran's text is established by mass transmission so wide that coordinated error is impossible, while a solitary report carries only probability. Since the integrity of the Book falls under the promise of Nabuwwah, the school requires certainty, and probability cannot supply what certainty demands.

Mu'arada: Contradiction with the Quran

Introduction

The Imami school possesses a principle that stands above all others in the science of hadith authentication: every report, no matter how strong its chain, must be measured against the Quran. The Imam who reportedly said "the scribes erred" is the same Imam who told his followers to reject anything that contradicts the Book of Allah.

The Rule

The foundational Shia principle of mu'arada, preserved in al-Kafi and verified from the FTS5 corpus:

إذا ورد عليكم حديثان مختلفان فاعرضوهما على كتاب الله، فما وافق كتاب الله فخذوه، وما خالف كتاب الله فردوه

Idha warada 'alaykum hadithani mukhtalifani fa-a'riduhuma 'ala kitabi Allah, fa-ma wafaqa kitaba Allah fa-khudhuhu, wa-ma khalafa kitaba Allah fa-rudduhu.

"When two differing reports come to you, present them to the Book of Allah. Accept that which agrees with the Book of Allah, and reject that which contradicts it."

- al-Kulayni (d. 329 AH), al-Kafi; verified from FTS5 corpus
The Three Verses

The claim that the written Quran contains an uncorrected scribal error directly contradicts three explicit, muhkam, mutawatir verses:

Quran 15:9: Allah ﷻ attaches preservation to Himself. If scribes introduced an error that persisted for 1,400 years, the promise was broken.

﴿إِنَّا نَحْنُ نَزَّلْنَا الذِّكْرَ وَإِنَّا لَهُ لَحَافِظُونَ﴾

Inna nahnu nazzalna al-dhikra wa-inna lahu la-hafizun.

"Indeed, We sent down the Reminder, and indeed We are surely its preservers."

- Quran 15:9

Quran 41:42: A scribal error is falsehood that entered the text from behind it. The verse declares this impossible.

﴿لَا يَأْتِيهِ الْبَاطِلُ مِن بَيْنِ يَدَيْهِ وَلَا مِنْ خَلْفِهِ تَنزِيلٌ مِّنْ حَكِيمٍ حَمِيدٍ﴾

La ya'tihi al-batilu min bayni yadayhi wa-la min khalfihi, tanzilun min hakimin hamid.

"Falsehood does not come to it from before it or from behind it, a revelation from One all-wise and praiseworthy."

- Quran 41:42

Quran 75:16-18: Allah ﷻ took personal responsibility for the collection. The scribal-error claim implies the collection was imperfect.

﴿إِنَّ عَلَيْنَا جَمْعَهُ وَقُرْآنَهُ ۖ فَإِذَا قَرَأْنَاهُ فَاتَّبِعْ قُرْآنَهُ﴾

Inna 'alayna jam'ahu wa-qur'anah, fa-idha qara'nahu fa-ttabi' qur'anah.

"It is for Us to collect it and to promulgate it. But when We have promulgated it, follow its recital."

- Quran 75:16-18

The Imam can contradict the Quran, because the Imam's own instruction was to reject anything that contradicts the Quran. If a report attributed to the Imam appears to contradict the Quran, the report must be reinterpreted or rejected.

Conclusion

The scribal-error narration fails contact with the Quran's own self-testimony. Three explicit verses promise preservation, declare falsehood incapable of reaching the text, and assign collection itself to Allah ﷻ. Since the Imam who reportedly identified the scribal error is the same Imam who instructed his followers to reject whatever contradicts the Book, the report must yield to the principle it itself depends upon.

Tahrif of Meaning, and Ibn Ashtah's Framework

Introduction

The phrase "the scribes erred" was a recognized idiom in early Islamic discourse. Ibn Abbas ﵁ used the identical phrase about multiple verses, and the Sunni scholarly consensus is that he meant the scribes chose one valid reading over another. The same framework applies to the Imam's ﵇ statement about 5:95.

Ibn Ashtah's Framework

Ibn Ashtah explained what "the scribes erred" meant in the Sunni framework, in Kitab al-Masahif:

أخطئوا أي في اختيار الأولى من الأحرف السبعة لجمع الناس عليه، لا أن الذي كتبوا من ذلك خطأ لا يجوز

Akhata'u ayyan fi ikhtiyari al-ula min al-ahruf al-sab'ati li-jam'i al-nasi 'alayhi, la anna alladhi katabu min dhalika khata'un la yajuz.

"They erred, meaning in choosing the first of the seven ahruf to gather people upon, not that what they wrote is an error that is impermissible."

- Ibn Ashtah, Kitab al-Masahif; preserved in Abu Shahba (d. 1402 AH), al-Mudkhal, p. 475

The framework is clear: "the scribes erred" meant the community settled on a reading that obscures the deeper meaning. The same idiom applies to the Imam's ﵇ statement about 5:95: the community's reading of "two just persons" obscures the verse's true referent, the divinely appointed arbiters of justice.

Conclusion

The phrase "the scribes erred" belonged to a recognized idiom in early Islamic discourse, meaning the community settled on a reading that obscures deeper meaning. Ibn Ashtah's framework shows that this language referred to choosing among valid readings. Applied to the Imam's ﵇ statement about 5:95, the idiom points to tahrif of meaning: the community's reading of "two just persons" conceals the verse's true referent.

The Later Imam Rule

Introduction

In Imami usul, when two narrations conflict and reconciliation is impossible, the position of the later Imam carries greater practical weight, because it represents the most recent binding instruction. This principle, rooted in the doctrine of naskh extending to the Imams, is stated in al-Tusi's 'Uddat al-Usul and applied throughout al-Istibsar.

The Application

The "scribal error" narration is attributed to Imam al-Sadiq ﵇ (d. 148 AH). No later Imam, from al-Kazim (d. 183 AH) through al-Askari (d. 260 AH), ever recited 5:95 differently from the standard text or claimed it contained a scribal error. The later Imams consistently endorsed the standard codex.

Furthermore, Imam al-Sadiq ﵇ himself, in a separate well-known report, gave the opposite instruction. When a man recited a Quranic word differently from common practice, the Imam ﵇ said:

اقرؤوا كما يقرأ الناس

Iqra'u kama yaqra'u al-nas.

"Recite as the people recite."

- al-Kulayni (d. 329 AH), al-Kafi; al-Hurr al-Amili, Wasa'il al-Shia, vol. 4, p. 821

If the Imam ﵇ endorsed variant recitations, why did he order the man to recite as the public does? The "recite as the people recite" instruction directly contradicts the "scribal error" narration. Under the later Imam rule, the binding guidance is the one that sends the community back to the common codex.

Conclusion

Even if some early narrations contain genuine statements about variant readings, the consistent, cumulative practice of all twelve Imams ﵈ was to recite, teach, and derive rulings from the standard mushaf. The explicit instruction "recite as the people recite" represents the final, binding guidance. Under the later Imam rule, this governs.

Internal Coherence and the Strategic Test

The Internal Coherence Test

The Imams ﵈ lived for over 250 years after the Prophet's ﷺ death. They had thousands of devoted followers across the Muslim world. If they truly believed the Quran contained scribal errors, why did they produce a corrected mushaf? Why did they identify even a single specific passage where the error occurred consistently across multiple independent reports? Why did they instruct their followers to avoid the corrupted text?

The claim is internally incoherent. It identifies no scribes, no occasion, and no nature of the errors. Every Imam ﵇ derived legal rulings from the standard text on prayer, fasting, marriage, inheritance, commerce, and warfare. This practice is logically incompatible with the belief that the text contains errors serious enough to distort divine revelation.

The Tabataba'i Strategic Test

Allamah Tabataba'i (al-Mizan, vol. 12, pp. 104-111) posed a devastating question. If the Quran had genuinely been altered by the caliphs or their scribes, with verses about the wilaya of Ali ﵇ systematically removed, this would have been the single most powerful weapon in the Imams' ﵈ da'wah. It would have proven beyond doubt that the caliphal establishment was so corrupt it tampered even with the Word of Allah ﷻ.

The Imams ﵈ argued from within the existing text. They extracted the merits of Ahl al-Bayt ﵈ from the verses as they stand, using tafsir and ta'wil to draw out meanings already present. They said: "This verse, as written, refers to Ali ﵇." They never said: "The caliphs deleted the verse about Ali's ﵇ authority." Shia scholars who criticized the burning of Quran manuscripts by the third caliph never alleged textual corruption as part of that criticism. If tahrif had occurred, the burning of the codices would have been the moment to expose it. No scholar made that charge.

Conclusion

The scribal-error claim is internally incoherent: it explains no Imam ﵇ produced a corrected mushaf, and it explains why every Imam ﵇ derived rulings from the standard text. The Tabataba'i test adds the strategic dimension: if the text were genuinely altered, the Imams ﵈ would have made this their central argument against the caliphs. They argued from the existing text instead.

The Ibn Abbas Sunni Parallels

Introduction

The "scribal error" claim is unique to Shia sources. Ibn Abbas ﵁, the most prominent exegete among the Companions, used the identical phrase "error of the scribe" about four other verses in reports authenticated by al-Hakim on the conditions of Bukhari and Muslim. This proves the motif was a shared early Islamic idiom, a distinctly Shia doctrine of tahrif.

First: Quran 24:27, "tasta'nisu"

عن ابن عباس، قال في قوله تعالى «حَتَّى تَسْتَأْنِسُوا وَتُسَلِّمُوا عَلَى أَهْلِهَا»: هي من خطأ الكاتب. وإنما هي: حتى تستأذنوا وتسلموا

'An Ibn 'Abbas, qala fi qawlihi ta'ala "hatta tasta'nisu wa-tusallimu 'ala ahliha": hiya min khata' al-katib. Wa-innama hiya: hatta tasta'dhinu wa-tusallim.

"Ibn Abbas said regarding the verse 'until you seek permission and greet': It is from the error of the scribe. It should be: until you seek authorization (tasta'dhinu) and greet."

- Recorded by al-Tabari in his Tafsir; authenticated by al-Hakim; Ma'rifat (d. 2007), al-Tamhid, vol. 1, p. 370
Second: Quran 17:23, "wa-qada"

أنزل الله هذا الحرف على لسان نبيكم «ووصى ربك أن لا تعبدوا إلا إياه» فالتصقت إحدى الواوين بالصاد، فقرأ الناس «وَقَضَى رَبُّكَ»

Anzala Allahu hadha al-harf 'ala lisani nabiyyikum "wa-wassa rabbuka an la ta'budu illa iyyahu" fa-l-tasiqat ihda al-wawayni bi'l-sad, fa-qara'a al-nasu "wa-qada rabbuka."

"Allah sent down this letter on the tongue of your Prophet: 'And your Lord enjoined (wa-wassa).' One of the waws stuck to the sad, and the people read it as 'And your Lord decreed (wa-qada).' Ibn Abbas added: If it had been revealed as qada, no one would have associated partners with Allah."

- Recorded by Abu 'Ubayd; Ma'rifat (d. 2007), al-Tamhid, vol. 1, p. 368
Third: Quran 24:35, "nurihi"

عن ابن عباس في قوله «مثل نوره كمشكاة» قال: هو خطأ من الكاتب، هو أعظم من ذلك، هو مثل نور المؤمن

'An Ibn 'Abbas fi qawlihi "mithlu nurihi ka-mishkat" qala: huwa khata'un min al-katib, huwa a'zam min dhalik, huwa mithlu nuri al-mu'min.

"Ibn Abbas said about 'the likeness of His light is like a niche': It is an error from the scribe. It is greater than that: it is 'the likeness of the light of the believer (nur al-mu'min).'"

- Abu Shahba (d. 1402 AH), al-Mudkhal li-Dirasat al-Quran, p. 475
Fourth: A Second Report on 24:27

قرأ ابن عباس: حتى تستأذنوا، فقال رجل: ألا أراه إلا حتى تستأنسوا، فقال ابن عباس: ذاك من خطأ الكاتب

Qara'a Ibn 'Abbas: hatta tasta'dhinu, fa-qala rajul: ala arahu illa hatta tasta'nisu, fa-qala Ibn 'Abbas: dhalika min khata' al-katib.

"Ibn Abbas read: 'until you seek authorization (tasta'dhinu).' A man said: I see it as 'until you feel at ease (tasta'nisu).' Ibn Abbas said: That is from the error of the scribe."

- Abu Shahba (d. 1402 AH), al-Mudkhal li-Dirasat al-Quran, p. 475
Conclusion

The "scribal error" motif originated in the Sunni corpus, attributed to Ibn Abbas ﵁ about four different verses, authenticated by al-Hakim on the conditions of Bukhari and Muslim. The phrase "error of the scribe" was a Sunni-originated concept that entered Shia discourse through cross-pollination. The al-Kafi narration about 5:95 uses the exact same framework as these Ibn Abbas reports.

The Sunni Cross-Pollination

Introduction

No Sunni source has any variant reading, error claim, or alternative interpretation for "dhawa adlin minkum" (5:95). All seven canonical readers, all ten canonical readers, and even the irregular readings read the verse identically. The claim of scribal error for this verse exists only in Shia sources.

The Motif in the Sunni Corpus

The phrase "the scribes erred" does appear in the Sunni corpus, attributed to Ibn Abbas ﵁ about four other verses (24:27, 17:23, 24:35), authenticated by al-Hakim on the conditions of Bukhari and Muslim. The Sunni corpus also contains substantial narrations suggesting textual incompleteness: the Stoning Verse (al-Bukhari, vol. 8, p. 209), the Suckling Verse (Sahih Muslim), Surah al-Ahzab reportedly equal in length to Surah al-Baqarah, and Ibn Mas'ud's Mushaf without al-Fatiha, al-Falaq, or al-Nas.

Sunni scholars handle these through naskh al-tilawah (abrogation of recitation by Prophetic authority): the verses were genuinely revealed, recited, and then their recitation was withdrawn during the Prophet's ﷺ lifetime. This is categorically different from post-Prophetic scribal corruption.

Khilaf al-Ammah

The Imami school possesses a principle for resolving conflicting narrations, taught by Imam al-Sadiq ﵇ himself:

إذا ورد عليكم حديثان مختلفان فاعرضوهما على كتاب الله، فما وافق كتاب الله فخذوه وما خالف كتاب الله فردوه، فإن لم تجدوهما في كتاب الله فاعرضوهما على أخبار العامة، فما وافق أخبارهم فذروه وما خالف أخبارهم فخذوه

Idha warada 'alaykum hadithani mukhtalifani fa-a'riduhuma 'ala kitabi Allah, fa-ma wafaqa kitaba Allah fa-khudhuhu wa-ma khalafa kitaba Allah fa-rudduhu, fa-in lam tajiduhuma fi kitabi Allah fa-a'riduhuma 'ala akhbari al-'ammati, fa-ma wafaqa akhbarahum fa-dharuhu wa-ma khalafa akhbarahum fa-khudhuhu.

"When two differing reports come to you, present them to the Book of Allah. Accept that which agrees with the Book of Allah, and reject that which contradicts it. If you find them in the Book of Allah, then present them to the reports of the general public (the Sunnis): abandon that which agrees with their reports, and take that which contradicts their reports."

- al-Kulayni (d. 329 AH), al-Kafi, vol. 1, p. 56; al-Hurr al-Amili, Wasa'il al-Shia, vol. 27, p. 118

The dominant Sunni position on 5:95 is unanimous: the verse reads dhawa adlin minkum with no variant. The khilaf al-ammah principle operates within fiqh (jurisprudential disputes where two Imami narrations conflict), and the integrity of the Book of Allah ﷻ is a matter of usul al-din (foundational creed). The principle override the first tier (the Quran's promise of preservation), and it manufacture a textual variant against the universal Muslim reading.

The "scribes erred" motif itself originated in the Sunni corpus with Ibn Abbas ﵁. Accepting it means importing a Sunni-originated framing. The khilaf al-ammah principle exists to flag and filter precisely this kind of cross-pollination from the dominant school.

Conclusion

The scribal-error motif originated in the Sunni corpus, attributed to Ibn Abbas ﵁, and entered Shia collections through cross-pollination from the broader Islamic discourse. The principle of khilaf al-ammah exists to flag and filter this kind of infiltration, and the integrity of the Book is a creedal matter, not a fiqhi dispute where the principle operates.

The Quran the Shia recite today traces by a named, documented chain from the Prophet ﷺ through Ali ﵇ and the Imams ﵈ to the printed mushaf. The reading-manuals count the links by name; the manuscripts confirm the skeleton; the Shia sources carry the same collection history as the Sunni sources, without any charge of corruption.

The Upstream Chain

Introduction

The reading most of the Muslim world recites, Hafs from Asim, carries its own documented chain back to the Prophet ﷺ. The chain is recorded by name in the reading-manuals. Every link is a person who recited the whole book to the person before him.

The Chain

Al-Dhahabi (d. 748 AH), a master reciter, records his own chain in Ma'rifat al-Qurra' al-Kibar:

فإني قرأت القرآن كله على أبي القاسم سحنون المالكي... عن السامري، عن الأشناني، عن عبيد بن الصباح، عن حفص، عن عاصم، عن أبي عبد الرحمن السلمي، عن علي

Fa-inni qara'tu al-Qur'ana kullahu 'ala Abi al-Qasim Sahnun al-Maliki... 'an al-Samiri, 'an al-Ashnani, 'an 'Ubayd b. al-Sabbah, 'an Hafs, 'an 'Asim, 'an Abi 'Abd al-Rahman al-Sulami, 'an 'Ali.

"I recited the whole Quran to Abu al-Qasim Sahnun al-Maliki. The chain runs back through al-Samiri, from al-Ashnani, from Ubayd ibn al-Sabbah, from Hafs, from Asim, from Abu Abd al-Rahman al-Sulami, from Ali ﵇."

- al-Dhahabi (d. 748 AH), Ma'rifat al-Qurra' al-Kibar, vol. 1, p. 210

The chain runs up from Hafs through Asim and al-Sulami to Ali ibn Abi Talib ﵇. Al-Sulami read the whole Quran to four companions: Uthman, Ali ﵇, Zayd ibn Thabit, and Ubayy ibn Ka'b. The load-bearing link on the Shia reading is al-Sulami's recital to Ali ﵇.

Conclusion

The recited text reaches the Prophet ﷺ through a named, documented chain of oral recital, and the load-bearing link for the Shia reading is al-Sulami's transmission from Ali ﵇. Each link in the chain represents a full recitation of the entire Quran verified by the teacher before the student. This is an unbroken line of performance, preserved by the same discipline that produced the qira'at.

Ali Wrote Every Verse

Introduction

Ali ibn Abi Talib ﵇ confirmed that he wrote every verse of the Quran by direct dictation from the Prophet ﷺ, with full instruction on placement, interpretation, abrogation, and clarity. His codex was the most complete record of the revelation in its interpretive context.

The Testimony

From al-Kafi:

ما نزلت على رسول الله آية من القرآن إلا أقرأنيها وأملاها علي فكتبتها بخطي، وعلمني تأويلها وتفسيرها، وناسخها ومنسوخها، ومحكمها ومتشابهها

Ma nuzila 'ala Rasul Allah ayatun min al-Qur'an illa aqra'aniha wa-amleha 'alayya fa-katabtuha bi-khatti, wa-'allamani ta'wilaha wa-tafsiraha, wa-nasikhaha wa-mansukhaha, wa-muhkamaha wa-mutashabihaha.

"No verse of the Quran came down upon the Messenger of God ﷺ without his reciting it to me and dictating it to me. I wrote it in my own hand. He taught me its interpretation and its explanation, its abrogating and its abrogated, its clear and its ambiguous."

- al-Kulayni (d. 329 AH), al-Kafi, vol. 1, p. 112

Ali's ﵇ additions to his personal codex were commentary: marking which verses abrogate which. The content of the reports agrees that the Imam gathered the Quran in a gathering that we today call commentary (al-Askari, Riwayat al-Madrasatayn, vol. 3, p. 200).

The Prophet's Placement Method

The order of the Quranic text was set during the Prophet's ﷺ lifetime, by his own instruction:

إن رسول الله صلى الله عليه وآله كان مما يأتي عليه الزمان ينزل عليه من السور ذوات العدد، وكان إذا أنزل عليه الشيء يدعو بعض من يكتب عنده، يقول: ضعوا هذا في السورة التي يذكر فيها كذا وكذا

Inna Rasula Allahi sallá Allahu 'alayhi wa-alih kana mimma ya'ti 'alayhi al-zaman yunazzalu 'alayhi min al-suwar dhawat al-'adad, wa-kana idha unzila 'alayhi al-shay' yad'u ba'da man yaktubu 'indahu, yaqul: da'u hadha fi al-surati allati yudhkaru fiha kadha wa-kadha.

"There would come upon the Messenger of God ﷺ a time when suras of several verses were being sent down to him. When something came down, he would call one of those who wrote for him. He would say: 'Place this in the sura in which such and such is mentioned.'"

- al-Askari, al-Qur'an al-Karim wa Riwayat al-Madrasatayn, vol. 1, p. 211
Conclusion

Ali ﵇ wrote every verse by direct dictation from the Prophet ﷺ, with full instruction on placement, abrogation, and meaning. The Prophet ﷺ himself directed the placement of each verse within its sura during his lifetime. Ali's ﵇ personal codex included the interpretive framework around the text, which is why the school reads his codex as a tafsir, not a parallel Quran.

The Collection History

Introduction

The Shia sources themselves carry the collection history without any charge of corruption. The same collection narrative that Sunnis transmit sits inside the Shia sources.

Ali's Oath and Collection

After the Prophet's ﷺ death, Ali ﵇ swore an oath to gather the Quran before resuming normal life. The report is preserved in Bihar al-Anwar:

فلما توفي رسول الله ﷺ اشتغلت بدفنه والفراغ من شأنه، ثم آليت يميناً أني لا أرتدي إلا للصلاة وجمع القرآن

Falammā tawaffā Rasūlu Allāhi ṣallā Allāhu 'alayhi wa-ālihi ishtaghaltu bi-dafnihi wa'l-farāghi min shānihi, thumma ālaytu yamīnan annī lā artadī illā li'l-ṣalāti wa-jam'i al-Qur'ān.

"When the Messenger of Allah died, I busied myself with his burial and with settling his affairs. Then I swore an oath that I would not put on my cloak except for prayer, and for the gathering of the Quran."

- al-Majlisi (d. 1111 AH), Bihar al-Anwar, vol. 22, p. 330

Ali ﵇ gathered it in the order of revelation, a difference of arrangement from the standard codex. His extra material was commentary, marking which verses abrogate which (al-Askari, Riwayat al-Madrasatayn, vol. 3, p. 200).

Zayd's Gathering Under Abu Bakr

The same collection narrative that Sunnis transmit sits inside the Shia sources. Zayd ibn Thabit describes the gathering:

فتتبعت القرآن أجمعه من الرقاع والعسب واللخاف وصدور الرجال

Fatabbaʿtu al-Qur'āna ajmaʿuhu min al-riqāʿi wa'l-ʿusbi wa'l-lakhāfi wa-ṣudūri al-rijāl.

"So I pursued the Quran, gathering it from parchment scraps, palm-stalks, thin white stones, and the memories of men."

- al-Majlisi (d. 1111 AH), Bihar al-Anwar, vol. 89, p. 78
Uthman's Standardization and Ali's Endorsement

Al-Majlisi records Uthman's standardization in Bihar al-Anwar:

جمع عثمان الناس على قراءة زيد بن ثابت، وكتب المصاحف، فبعث بواحد منها إلى الكوفة وبواحد إلى البصرة وإلى كل من الشام ومكة واليمن والبحرين بواحد، وأمسك في المدينة مصحفاً كانوا يقولون له الإمام

Jamaʿa ʿUthmānu al-nāsa ʿalā qirā'ati Zayd b. Thābit, wa-kataba al-maṣāḥifa, fa-baʿatha bi-wāḥidin minhā ilā al-Kūfati wa-bi-wāḥidin ilā al-Baṣrati wa-ilā kullin min al-Shāmi wa-Makkata wa'l-Yamani wa'l-Baḥrayni bi-wāḥid, wa-amsaka fī al-Madīnati muṣḥafan kānū yaqūlūna lahu al-Imām.

"Uthman gathered the people upon the reading of Zayd ibn Thabit and wrote the codices. He sent one to Kufa, one to Basra, and one each to Syria, Mecca, Yemen, and Bahrain. He kept in Medina a codex they used to call the Imam."

- al-Majlisi (d. 1111 AH), Bihar al-Anwar, vol. 31, p. 211

Ali ﵇ endorsed the standardization:

لو لم يصنعه عثمان لصنعته

Law lam yaṣnaʿhu ʿUthmānu la-ṣnaʿtuh.

"Had Uthman not done it, I would have done it myself."

- al-Abyari (d. 1414 AH), al-Mawsu'a al-Qur'aniyya, vol. 3, p. 69
Conclusion

The Shia sources carry the same collection narrative as the Sunni sources: Ali's ﵇ oath, Zayd's gathering from scattered materials, Uthman's standardization, and Ali's ﵇ explicit endorsement. Each stage is documented in the Shia hadith literature without any charge of corruption against any participant.

The Imams on the Text's Unity

Introduction

Imam al-Baqir ﵇ was asked why hadith reports about the Quran differ. He placed the difference in the narrators, in the revealed text. Imam al-Sadiq ﵇ directed his followers to the received text.

Imam al-Baqir on Quran Unity

عن أبي جعفر عليه السلام قال: إن القرآن واحد نزل من عند واحد ولكن الاختلاف يجيئ من قبل الرواة

'An Abi Ja'far alayhi al-salam qala: Inna al-Qur'ana wahid nazala min 'inda wahid wa-lakin al-ikhtilaf yaji' min qabli al-ruwat.

"Imam al-Baqir ﵇ said: The Quran is one; it came down from One. But the variance comes from the transmitters."

- al-Kulayni (d. 329 AH), al-Kafi, vol. 2, p. 630
Imam al-Sadiq on the Received Text

سألت أبا عبد الله عليه السلام عن تنزيل القرآن قال: اقرؤوا كما علمتم

Sa'altu Aba 'Abd Allah alayhi al-salam 'an tanzili al-Qur'an qala: Iqra'u kama 'ullimtum.

"I asked Abu Abd Allah about the revelation of the Quran. He answered: 'Recite as you were taught.'"

- al-Kulayni (d. 329 AH), al-Kafi, vol. 2, p. 631

This instruction is unique to the Shia sources. Sunni hadith preserves the identical Prophetic wording in Musnad Abi Ya'la (vol. 8, p. 470): iqra'u kama 'ullimtum, "Recite as you were taught." Both traditions carry the same instruction.

Conclusion

The Imams ﵈ identified the text as one, received from One, and placed all variance in the transmitters. Their instruction was to recite what was taught, the text the community received. The variance the Imams ﵈ acknowledged concerns transmission of reports, not the integrity of the recited text.

Mass Memorization

Introduction

A single chain, however careful, is one thread. What stops a reading from drifting over centuries is mass transmission (tawatur). The text was carried by numbers so large in every generation that agreement on a change was impossible.

The Shia Position

Al-Amili stated the school's position in al-Intisar:

إنه نقل بالتواتر المفيد للعلم الذي حصل لنا من خلاله أن لا زيادة فيه ولا نقصان منه ولو حرفا واحدا وهذا هو رأي الشيعة والذي عليه المعتمد عندهم

Innahu nuqila bi'l-tawaturi al-mufidi li'l-'ilmi alladhi hasala lana min khilalihi anna la ziyadata fihi wa-la nuqsana minhu wa-law harfan wahidan, wa-hadha huwa ra'y al-shi'ati wa-alladhi 'alayhi al-mu'tamadu 'indahum.

"It was transmitted by mass transmission, which gives us certain knowledge that not one letter was added to it or dropped from it. This is the view of the Shia and the one they rely upon."

- al-Amili, al-Intisar, vol. 3, p. 314

The text is guarded three ways: written in the codices, preserved in the breasts, and recited on the tongues. If a page were lost, the memorizers held the text. If a memorizer erred, the pages and the crowd corrected him.

Conclusion

The Quran was never carried by a single thread. Mass transmission placed the full text in the memory and on the tongues of so many reciters in every generation that agreement on a change was impossible. Al-Amili stated the school's position as certain knowledge: not one letter was added or dropped. Three independent layers of preservation, the codices, the memorizers, and the reciters, each correct the other.

The manuscript record confirms what the chains of recital assert. The oldest surviving codices, dated by radiocarbon to the first century of Islam, carry the same consonant skeleton, and the shared spelling quirks across fourteen early manuscripts point to a single early written archetype. The 1924 Cairo colophon names the chain from Hafs through Asim and al-Sulami to four companions, including Ali ﵇, and from them to the Prophet ﷺ.

The Sanaa Palimpsest

Introduction

The one manuscript cited as proof of change is the Sanaa palimpsest (DAM 01-27.1). A palimpsest is a manuscript whose original text was erased and written over. The Sanaa palimpsest has two layers: an upper text (the visible layer) and a lower text (the erased layer beneath).

The Finding

The upper text is the standard Quran. The lower text is a non-standard early codex of the kind ascribed to individual Companions. Sadeghi and Goudarzi concluded that the differences between the upper and lower texts are at the level of morphemes, words, and phrases, and at the level of sentences or verses (Der Islam 87, 2012, pp. 23, 26).

The palimpsest is a physical snapshot of a Companion codex being retired in favour of the standard text. This is the standardization the sources report: Uthman gathered the people upon one reading and distributed the standard codices. The Sanaa palimpsest shows the process in action, with the standard text written over the Companion variant.

Conclusion

The Sanaa palimpsest, the one manuscript most often cited as evidence of change, in fact demonstrates the opposite. Its lower text is a Companion variant being retired in favor of the standard codex, with the canonical reading written over it. The physical object captures the standardization process the sources report.

From al-Saduq (d. 381 AH) to al-Khoei (d. 1413 AH), the school's leading scholars affirm complete Quran preservation. Every major marja' of the 20th and 21st century has rejected textual tahrif.

Al-Saduq (d. 381 AH)

Introduction

Al-Saduq (Ibn Babawayh) produced the first systematic creedal denial of textual tahrif. His al-I'tiqadat fixed the position every subsequent generation of Imami scholars would build upon.

The Creed

The full statement from al-I'tiqadat:

اعتقادنا أن القرآن الذي أنزله الله تعالى على نبيه محمد (﵌) هو ما بين الدفتين وهو ما في أيدي الناس ليس بأكثر من ذلك. ومن نسب إلينا أنا نقول أنه أكثر من ذلك فهو كاذب

I'tiqaduna anna al-Qur'ana alladhi anzalahu Allahu ta'ala 'ala nabiyyihi Muhammadin salla Allahu 'alayhi wa-alihi huwa ma bayna al-daffatayn wa-huwa ma fi aydi al-nasi laysa bi-akthara min dhalik. Wa-man nasaba ilayna anna naqulu annahu aktharu min dhalika fa-huwa kadhib.

"Our belief is that the Quran which Allah, exalted be He, sent down upon His Prophet Muhammad ﷺ is what is between the two covers, and it is what is in people's hands, nothing more. Whoever attributes to us that we say it is more than that is a liar."

- al-Saduq (d. 381 AH), al-I'tiqadat, pp. 92-93
Conclusion

By anchoring his creed in the physical mushaf the community already possessed, al-Saduq drew the school's first unambiguous line against textual tahrif. His choice to call the opposing claim a lie, rather than merely mistaken, signaled that the Quran's completeness was a boundary of faith. Every subsequent theologian in the school built upon this foundation, refining its arguments and affirming its conclusion.

Al-Mufid's Middle Position

Introduction

Shaykh al-Mufid (d. 413 AH) held a nuanced middle position between al-Saduq's absolute denial and the earlier scholars' openness to omission. His position represents the most careful theological formulation the school produced on this question.

The Position

From al-Masa'il al-Sarawiyya and Awa'il al-Maqalat:

ما بين الدفتين من القرآن أكثر ما أنزل الله، وما سوى ذلك فإنه عند ولي الشرع

Ma bayna al-daffatayn min al-Qur'an aktharu ma anzala Allahu, wa-ma siwa dhalik fa-innahu 'inda waliyyi al-shar'.

"What is between the two covers of the Quran is the majority of the revelation. The rest is with the guardian of the Shari'a."

- al-Mufid (d. 413 AH), al-Masa'il al-Sarawiyya, p. 78; Awa'il al-Maqalat, section 59

Al-Mufid affirmed that nothing was added to the Quran. He held that reason does not consider omission theoretically impossible, but resolved the question by arguing that any omissions affected Ali's ﵇ tafsir notes and commentary, the revealed text itself. His position is distinct from both al-Saduq (who denied omission entirely) and from those who affirmed textual loss. Al-Mufid's formulation acknowledges a gap between the complete revelation and the standard codex, and locates that gap in the commentary material, in the recited verses.

Conclusion

Al-Mufid represents the school's most theologically careful formulation. He denies addition, acknowledges the standard codex as the majority of revelation, and locates any remaining material in the commentary held by the Imams ﵈. His position preserves both the integrity of the recited text and the logical possibility that the Ahl al-Bayt ﵈ possessed additional explanatory material. Later scholars like al-Murtada and al-Tusi moved from this middle position to a full denial of omission.

Al-Murtada (d. 436 AH)

Introduction

Al-Sharif al-Murtada mounted the strongest classical denial of textual tahrif. He argued from three grounds: tawatur (mass transmission), rational necessity, and the rejection of solitary reports in matters of creed.

The Cities Argument

Al-Murtada placed certainty in the Quran's transmission on the level of certainty about the existence of famous cities:

إن العلم بصحة نقل القرآن كالعلم بالبلدان والحوادث العظام والكتب المشهورة وأشعار العرب

Inna al-'ilma bi-sihhati naqli al-Qur'ani ka'l-'ilmi bi'l-buldani wa'l-hawadithi al-'izami wa'l-kutubi al-mashhurati wa-ash'ari al-'arab.

"Knowledge of the correctness of the Quran's transmission is like knowledge of cities, great historical events, famous books, and the poetry of the Arabs."

- al-Murtada (d. 436 AH), Jawabat al-Masa'il al-Tarabulusiyyat

Ibn Hazm records that al-Murtada deemed infidels whoever maintained the belief in the distortion of the Quran. For al-Murtada, to deny the Quran's textual integrity was to deny the fulfillment of Allah's ﷻ promise.

Conclusion

Al-Murtada placed the Quran's textual certainty on the same epistemic footing as the reality of known cities and recorded events. No sane person doubts that Mecca exists or that great battles occurred, because the evidence is too widespread and too multiply attested to be fabricated. Al-Murtada argued the Quran's transmission carries this same self-evident force, and therefore no solitary narration can dent it.

Al-Tusi (d. 460 AH)

Introduction

Shaykh al-Tusi, known as Shaykh al-Ta'ifa (Leader of the School), ruled decisively in al-Tibyan. His position closed the classical phase of the debate: addition is unanimously false, and the sound position is that deletion is false as well.

The Ruling

From the introduction to al-Tibyan:

وأما الكلام في زيادته ونقصانه فمما لا يليق به، لأن الزيادة فيه مجمع على بطلانه، والنقصان منه فالظاهر من مذهب المسلمين خلافه، وهو الأليق بالصحيح من مذهبنا

Wa-amma al-kalamu fi ziyadatihi wa-nuqsanihi fa-mimma la yaliqu bihi, li-anna al-ziyadata fihi mujma'un 'ala butlanihi, wa'l-nuqsanu minhu fa'l-zahiru min madhhabi al-muslimina khilafuhu, wa-huwa al-aliqu bi'l-sahihi min madhabina.

"As for the question of addition to it and omission from it, it is something unbefitting. Addition to it is unanimously agreed to be false, and as for omission from it, the apparent position of the Muslims is contrary to it, and this is the more fitting of the sound positions of our school."

- al-Tusi (d. 460 AH), al-Tibyan, vol. 1, p. 3
Conclusion

Al-Tusi closed the classical phase of the debate with a dual ruling: addition is unanimously false, and the sound position of the school is no deletion either. His authority as Shaykh al-Ta'ifa meant this formulation carried the weight of the entire school, and every major theologian after him affirmed it. The reports indicating deletion yield no certainty, he wrote, and should be set aside.

Al-Khoei (d. 1413 AH)

Introduction

Ayatollah al-Khoei produced the definitive modern treatment in al-Bayan fi Tafsir al-Quran (pp. 195-257). He classified the narrations by chain, identified their sources, and dismissed the claim of textual corruption as a tale disconnected from evidence.

The Verdict

Al-Khoei's closing assessment of the tahrif narrations:

إن حديث تحريف القرآن حديث خرافة وخيال، لا يقول به إلا من ضعف عقله، أو من لم يتأمل في أطرافه حق التأمل

Inna haditha tahrifi al-Qur'ani hadithu khurafatin wa-khayal, la yaqulu bihi illa man da'ufa 'aqluhu, aw man lam yata'ammal fi atrafihi haqqa al-ta'ammul.

"The claim of the alteration of the Quran is a tale of fable and fantasy. None asserts it except one whose reason is weak, or one who has examined its aspects with proper reflection."

- al-Khoei (d. 1413 AH), al-Bayan fi Tafsir al-Quran, p. 278
Conclusion

Al-Khoei's treatment closed the modern chapter of the debate. By classifying the narrations by chain, tracing them to their sources, and testing each against reason and the mutawatir text, he showed that the tahrif claim is a tale disconnected from both rational proof and verified transmission. His verdict carries the authority of the 20th century's leading marja', and every contemporary marja' has affirmed the same position.

The Consensus Chain

Introduction

The following scholars affirm complete Quran preservation, spanning a millennium from the 4th century AH to the present:

The Chain of Affirmation

Al-Saduq (d. 381 AH): "Whoever asserts that we claim it is greater in length than that is a liar." (al-I'tiqadat)

Al-Mufid (d. 413 AH): "What is between the two covers is the majority of the revelation." Nothing was added. (al-Masa'il al-Sarawiyya)

Al-Murtada (d. 436 AH): "Our belief in the completeness of the Quran is as certain as our belief in famous cities." (Jawabat al-Masa'il al-Tarabulusiyyat)

Al-Tusi (d. 460 AH): "Complete consensus that no addition has happened; the sound position is no deletion either." (al-Tibyan)

Al-Tabarsi (d. 548 AH): "We the Shia are opposed to this belief" of reduction or addition. (Majma' al-Bayan)

Tabataba'i (d. 1412 AH): "The clearest proof that the Quran we have today is the same that was revealed." (al-Mizan)

Al-Khoei (d. 1413 AH): "Whoever claims tahrif opposes the mind and rationality." (al-Bayan)

Khomeini (d. 1409 AH): Condemned Nuri's Fasl al-Khitab; treated the existing Quran as completely authoritative in all rulings. (Kashf al-Asrar)

Al-Muzaffar (d. 1381 AH): "This Holy Book that we recite today is the same Holy Quran which was revealed to the Holy Prophet ﷺ. Anyone who claims it to be otherwise is either an evil-doer, a mere sophist, or is sadly mistaken." (Beliefs of the Shiite School)

Ma'rifat (d. 2007): "The miraculous nature of the Quran is evidence against tahrif." (Siyanat al-Quran)

Sistani (contemporary): "The Quran that exists in our hands is the same as that which was revealed upon Allah's ﷻ messenger ﷺ without addition or loss." (Ahdath al-Istiftaat al-Aqidiyya)

Conclusion

The consensus chain spans an unbroken millennium from al-Saduq through every major theologian to every contemporary marja'. No significant scholar in the school's mainstream has affirmed textual tahrif since the rationalist turn, and the weight of names across ten centuries makes the position effectively irreversible. This is the same certainty al-Murtada compared to belief in the existence of famous cities: too widely attested to be overturned.

The Imam's Instruction

Introduction

The binding instruction from Imam al-Sadiq ﵇ turned his followers away from private variants and toward the common codex. The instruction is preserved in both Shia and Sunni sources with identical wording.

The Shia Source

From al-Kafi:

اقرؤوا كما يقرأ الناس

Iqra'u kama yaqra'u al-nas.

"Recite as the people recite."

- al-Kulayni (d. 329 AH), al-Kafi, vol. 2, p. 633
The Sunni Parallel

The Sunni tradition preserves the identical Prophetic wording in Musnad Abi Ya'la:

اقرؤوا كما علمتم

Iqra'u kama 'ullimtum.

"Recite as you were taught."

- al-Mawsili (d. 307 AH), Musnad Abi Ya'la, vol. 8, p. 470

Both traditions carry the same instruction: the community's received text is the authoritative text. The Imam ﵇ bound every Shia to the common codex. The Prophet ﷺ bound every Muslim to what he taught. The instruction has held for fourteen centuries.

Conclusion

The binding instruction from the Imam ﵇ turned every Shia toward the common codex, and the identical Prophetic ﷺ wording preserved in the Sunni tradition shows the instruction was universal. Both traditions carry the same command: the community's received text is the authoritative text. The instruction has held for fourteen centuries because it was given at the source and preserved.

Glossary of Key Terms

Tahrif (تحريف): Bending something away from its proper place. In Quranic studies, distortion of the text or its meaning. The Imami school affirms tahrif of meaning (misinterpretation) and denies tahrif of text (addition, deletion, substitution of words).

Mushaf (مصحف): A bound book. A codex of pages between two covers. The word is generic and applies to any bound book. Its specialization as a synonym for the Quranic codex was a post-Uthmanic cultural development.

Tawatur (تواتر): Mass transmission. A report carried by so many independent narrators in every generation that coordinated fabrication becomes impossible. Tawatur produces yaqin (certainty), as opposed to probability.

Khabar al-wahid (خبر الواحد): A solitary report. Any narration that does not reach the threshold of tawatur. Even with a reliable chain, a solitary report produces only probability (zann), not certainty. It cannot establish matters of creed.

Mu'arada (المعارضة): Comparison against the Quran. The foundational Imami principle that every report must be tested against the Quran. If a report contradicts the Quran, it is rejected regardless of its chain.

Khilaf al-ammah (خلاف العامة): Opposing the general public (the Sunni majority). A Shia principle for resolving conflicting narrations: when two reports conflict, prefer the one that contradicts the Sunni position. Applies to jurisprudential disputes, not to foundational creed.

Marja' (مرجع): A source of emulation. The highest-ranking Shia jurist whose rulings followers adopt. Contemporary maraji' include Ayatollah Sistani and others.

Akhbar ahad (أخبار الآحاد): Plural of khabar al-wahid. Solitary reports.

Zann (ظن): Probability or conjecture. The epistemic value of a solitary report. Insufficient for establishing creedal matters, which require certainty (yaqin).

Yaqin (يقين): Certainty. The epistemic standard required for matters of belief (usul al-din). Only tawatur or self-evident truths produce yaqin.

Final Verdict

The Quran the community recites today is the complete text the Prophet ﷺ received. The Imams ﵈ are its unbroken chain. A minority of early narrators held that verses were dropped. Their reports remain in the books, never became the school's teaching, and its scholars reject them. The "scribal error" narrations are either weak in chain, interpretable as tafsir and ta'wil, or rejected outright by the principles of mu'arada and the superiority of mutawatir over akhbar ahad.
