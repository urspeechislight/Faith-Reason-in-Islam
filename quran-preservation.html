Quranic Integrity

The Preserved Quran

A Shia analysis of tahrif, the scribal error narrations, and the chain of transmission from the Prophet ﷺ through Ali ﵇ to the mushaf in your hands

The Argument Under Examination

Background

Early Shia hadith collections contain narrations that appear to suggest textual alteration (tahrif). The dominant scholarly position from the 4th century AH to today affirms the Quran is completely preserved. Two premises and a conclusion.

Premise 1: The Quran's Own Testimony

Allah attaches the preservation of the Quran to Himself. The promise is explicit, unconditional, and eternal.

﴿إِنَّا نَحْنُ نَزَّلْنَا الذِّكْرَ وَإِنَّا لَهُ لَحَافِظُونَ﴾

Innā naḥnu nazzalnā al-dhikra wa-innā lahu la-ḥāfiẓūn.

"Indeed, We sent down the Reminder, and indeed We are surely its preservers."

- Quran 15:9

Premise 2: The Imams' Instruction

Imam al-Sadiq ﵇ bound his followers to the received text. The instruction has never been revoked.

اقرؤوا كما يقرأ الناس

Iqra'ū kamā yaqra'u al-nās.

"Recite as the people recite."

- al-Kulayni (d. 329 AH), al-Kafi, vol. 2, p. 633

Conclusion: The Quran Between the Two Covers Is Complete

Affirmed by every major Shia scholar from al-Saduq (d. 381 AH) to al-Khoei (d. 1413 AH). The reports suggesting alteration remain in the books; they never became the school's teaching.

The Arabic word tahrif carries a range of meanings, from distortion of interpretation to textual corruption. The Imami scholarly tradition drew a sharp line between these senses and tracked the debate through five distinct historical phases.

What Tahrif Means

The Root

The root h-r-f means to bend something off its proper course. The classical dictionaries fix the primary sense as twisting a word's meaning (Ibn Faris, Maqayis al-Lughah; al-Raghib, al-Mufradat). The Quran itself uses the word for the Jews who twisted scripture's meaning, never for deleting its text.

The Seven Types

Ayatollah Ma'rifat (d. 2007) and al-Khoei classified tahrif into seven types:

  1. Tahrif of meaning: misinterpreting verses contrary to their intent. Universally affirmed by all Shia and Sunni scholars.
  2. Tahrif of position: rearranging verse or surah order. Denied; the order was set by Prophetic instruction.
  3. Tahrif of pronunciation: variant vocalizations. Denied; these are the recognized qira'at.
  4. Tahrif of variant phrasing: minor word substitutions. Denied.
  5. Tahrif by substitution: replacing one word with another. Contested, generally denied.
  6. Tahrif by addition: adding to the Quran. Universally rejected by all Shia scholars.
  7. Tahrif by omission: removing revealed verses. The core disputed category, denied by the dominant school since al-Saduq.
The Critical Binary

All seven types collapse into one binary: tahrif al-nass (textual corruption) versus tahrif al-ma'na (interpretive distortion). Contemporary Shia scholarship affirms only tahrif al-ma'na, that the Quran's meaning has been distorted by misinterpretation, while denying tahrif al-nass entirely.

Conclusion

Tahrif of meaning is universally affirmed; tahrif of text is denied by the dominant school since al-Saduq.

The Historical Debate

Five Phases

The debate evolved through five distinct phases across a thousand years:

Phase 1: Early Period (3rd-4th c. AH). Belief in some form of textual alteration was common among early Imami scholars. Al-Kulayni (d. 329 AH) included tahrif narrations in al-Kafi without refuting them. Ali ibn Ibrahim al-Qummi's Tafsir and al-Ayyashi's Tafsir follow the same pattern. Al-Sayyari's Kitab al-Qira'at is the earliest extant Shia work implying textual changes.

Phase 2: The Rationalist Turn (Late 4th-5th c. AH). Denial becomes dominant. Al-Saduq (d. 381 AH) produced the first systematic creedal denial. Al-Mufid (d. 413 AH) took a middle position: nothing was added, but reason does not consider omission impossible. Al-Murtada (d. 436 AH) argued the strongest classical denial. Al-Tusi (d. 460 AH) ruled that addition is unanimously false and the sound position is no deletion either.

Phase 3: Akhbari Revival (10th-12th c. AH). The Akhbari school accepted hadith at face value without rationalist filtering. Fayd al-Kashani (d. 1091 AH) compiled nine pages of tahrif narrations in Tafsir al-Safi, then immediately added that the correct view of the sect is against that. Al-Majlisi (d. 1110 AH) called some traditions mutawatir in one work and akhbar ahad in another.

Phase 4: Fasl al-Khitab (c. 1298 AH / 1881 CE). Mirza Husayn Nuri (d. 1320 AH) produced the most comprehensive pro-tahrif work ever written: over one thousand traditions across four hundred pages. The book caused immediate controversy. Scholars at the hawza of Samarra heavily criticized it. Khomeini reportedly called it a collection of jokes with no academic value.

Phase 5: 20th-21st Century. Rejection becomes near-universal. Every major modern marja' affirms complete Quran preservation. Tabataba'i, al-Khoei, Khomeini, Sistani, and Ma'rifat all reject textual tahrif decisively.

Conclusion

By the 20th century, rejection of textual tahrif became near-universal among Shia scholars.

Al-Saduq's Creed

Introduction

Al-Saduq (Ibn Babawayh, d. 381 AH) produced the first systematic creedal denial of textual tahrif in the Imami tradition. His al-I'tiqadat fixed the school's position for every subsequent generation of scholars.

The Creed

Al-Saduq wrote in his creedal treatise:

"Our belief concerning the Quran is that it is the Word of Allah ﷻ, and His revelation sent down through the trustworthy Spirit. It is what is between the two covers, nothing less. Whoever asserts that we claim it is greater in length than that is a liar."

- al-Saduq (d. 381 AH), al-I'tiqadat, pp. 92-93
Conclusion

Al-Saduq fixed the school's creedal position: the Quran between the two covers is the complete revelation.

Al-Murtada's Position

Introduction

Al-Sharif al-Murtada (d. 436 AH) mounted the strongest classical denial of textual tahrif. He argued from three grounds: tawatur (mass transmission), rational necessity, and the rejection of solitary reports (akhbar ahad) in matters of creed.

The Cities Argument

Al-Murtada placed certainty in the Quran's transmission on the level of certainty about the existence of famous cities:

"Our belief in the completeness of the Quran is as certain as our belief in famous cities, and in the things we witness and know with the senses. The report of the Quran is a report whose certainty cannot be exceeded by any other report."

- al-Murtada (d. 436 AH), Jawabat al-Masa'il al-Tarabulusiyyat

Ibn Hazm records that al-Murtada deemed infidels whoever maintained the belief in the distortion of the Quran. For al-Murtada, to deny the Quran's textual integrity was to deny the fulfillment of Allah's promise.

Conclusion

Certainty in the Quran's transmission equals certainty about the existence of famous cities.

Al-Khoei's Refutation

Introduction

Ayatollah al-Khoei (d. 1413 AH) produced the most comprehensive modern treatment of the tahrif question in al-Bayan fi Tafsir al-Quran. He classified the narrations by chain, identified their sources, and rejected the claim of textual corruption on rational and textual grounds.

The Classification

Al-Khoei traced roughly two-thirds of all tahrif narrations to al-Sayyari, whom he identified as corrupt in doctrine. Another quarter trace to Furat al-Kufi, absent from all early Shia rijal works. The remaining narrations, he argued, point to tahrif of meaning, not text:

"Whoever claims tahrif opposes the mind and rationality, and relies in his claim upon reports whose chains he does not examine and whose meanings he does not understand."

- al-Khoei (d. 1413 AH), al-Bayan fi Tafsir al-Quran, p. 220
Conclusion

To claim tahrif is to oppose rationality and rely on reports whose chains and meanings are unexamined.

The word mushaf is a generic Arabic term meaning a bound book or codex of pages between two covers. Its specialization as a synonym for the Quranic codex was a later cultural development driven by the dominance of the Uthmanic codices.

The Root and Pattern

Introduction

The root s-h-f carries the primary meaning of breadth, expanse, and flatness (Ibn Faris, Maqayis al-Lughah, III, 334). From this root comes sahifa (page, leaf, written sheet), suhuf (pages, scriptures), and the derived noun mushaf.

The Morphology

The word mushaf is the passive participle of the Form IV verb ashhafa, meaning to gather pages into a bound volume between covers. Ibn Manzhur's Lisan al-Arab (d. 711 AH) records the classical morphological analysis:

إنما سمي المصحف مصحفاً لأنه أُصْحِفَ، أي جُعِلَ جامعاً للصحف المكتوبة بين الدفتين. قال الفراء: يقال مُصْحَفٌ ومِصْحَفٌ كما يقال مُطْرَفٌ ومِطْرَفٌ؛ وقوله مُصْحَفٌ من أُصْحِفَ أي جُمِعَتْ فيه الصحف. استثقلت العرب الضمة في حروف فكسرت الميم، وأصلها الضمُّ.

Innamā summiya al-muṣḥaf muṣḥafan li-annahu uṣḥifa, ayy juʿila jāmiʿan li'l-ṣuḥuf al-maktūba bayna al-daffatayn. Qāla al-Farrā': yuqālu muṣḥafun wa-miṣḥafun kamā yuqālu muṭrafun wa-miṭrafun; wa-qawluhu muṣḥafun min uṣḥifa ayy jumiʿat fīhi al-ṣuḥuf. istathqalat al-ʿarab al-ḍammata fī ḥurūfin fa-kasarat al-mīm, wa-aṣluhā al-ḍamm.

"It is called mushaf because it was ushifa, meaning it was made a gathering for the written pages between the two covers. Al-Farra' said: one says mushaf and mishaf, just as one says mutraf and mitraf. Mushaf is from ushifa, meaning the suhuf were gathered in it. The Arabs found the damma heavy in these letters and so broke the mim with kasra; its original is the damma."

- Ibn Manzhur (d. 711 AH), Lisan al-Arab, s.v. ص ح ف

The earliest Arabic morphological analysis confirms that mushaf means a physical object: pages bound between covers. The definition is entirely generic; no mention of the Quran.

Conclusion

Mushaf is a passive participle meaning pages gathered and bound between covers.

Pre-Islamic Usage

Introduction

Before the Quran existed as a compiled text, mushaf was already an Arabic word meaning a bound book. The pre-Islamic poet Imru' al-Qays (d. circa 540 CE), author of the most famous of the Mu'allaqat and the most celebrated pre-Islamic poet, used the word to describe the books of Christian monks, decades before the Prophet's ﷺ birth.

The Evidence

Daniel Madigan, in The Qur'an's Self-Image (p. 90, n. 92), documents that the Ethiopic term mushaf (meaning both "book" and "scripture," from the verb sahafa "to write") had already been borrowed into Arabic in pre-Islamic times. Imru' al-Qays used it for the books of monks. The word cannot mean divine scripture exclusively.

Additional pre-Islamic attestations include Antarah ibn Shaddad (d. circa 608 CE), whose Diwan uses the plural sah'if for written pages, recorded by al-Qurtubi in his Tafsir (XI, 86). Noldeke notes that sahifa already appears in pre-Islamic poetry in the verses of al-Mutalammis, Labid, and Aws b. Hajar. The Quran itself (87:18-19) uses suhuf for the revelations given to Abraham and Moses, texts that existed centuries before the Quranic codex.

Conclusion

Mushaf was an Arabic word for bound books before the Quran existed as a compiled text.

The Poetry Evidence

Introduction

A famous line of classical poetry uses mushaf in its generic lexical sense and settles the question of whether the word can mean exclusively the Quran.

The Verse

A poet wandering lost through Baghdad compared himself to a book sitting unread in a heretic's home:

بغداد دارٌ لأهل المال طيبة / وللمفاليس دار الضنك والضيق / ظللت حيران أمشي في أزقتها / كأنني مصحف في بيت زنديق

Baghdādu dārun li-ahl al-māli ṭayyibatun, wa-lil-mafālīsi dār al-ḍanki wa'l-ḍayq. Ẓalaltu ḥayran amshī fī aziqqatihā, ka-annanī muṣḥafun fī bayti zindīq.

"Baghdad is a pleasant home for the wealthy; for the destitute, a house of hardship and constriction. I wandered lost, walking through its alleys, as though I were a mushaf in the house of a zindiq (heretic)."

- Ibn Butlan (d. 455 AH), Da'wat al-Atibba'; also Ibn Bassam (d. 542 AH), al-Dhakhirah

A zindiq is a heretic or free-thinker. A heretic would not keep a Quran in his house. The metaphor works because mushaf means simply a book, a book that sits unread and unwanted in a heretic's home. The poet uses the word in its generic lexical sense.

Conclusion

A heretic would not keep a Quran; the word mushaf in this verse means a book.

Mushaf of Fatimah

Introduction

The Imams kept writings the community did not have. The most famous is the Mushaf of Fatimah ﵍. Some have asked whether this was a parallel Quran. The Imam's ﵇ own words settle the question.

The Distinction

Imam al-Sadiq ﵇ explicitly distinguished the Mushaf of Fatimah ﵍ from the Quran:

مصحف فيه مثل قرآنكم هذا ثلاث مرات، والله ما فيه من قرآنكم حرف واحد... إنه لعلم وما هو بذاك

Muṣḥafun fīhi mithlu qur'ānikum hādhā thalātha marrāt, wa-Allāhi mā fīhi min qur'ānikum ḥarfun wāḥid... innahu la-ʿilmun wa-mā huwa bi-dhāk.

"A codex, in it the like of this Quran of yours three times over. And by God, in it there is not one single letter of your Quran. It is knowledge indeed, but it is not that."

- al-Kulayni (d. 329 AH), al-Kafi, vol. 1, p. 287

The Imam ﵇ defines the Mushaf of Fatimah ﵍ and empties it of Quran. It contains angelic communications, predictions of future events, and the names of future rulers. It is a book of private knowledge given to Fatimah ﵍ after her father's ﷺ death. The word mushaf means exactly what it says lexically: a bound book.

Conclusion

The Mushaf of Fatimah ﵍ contains knowledge, but not a single letter of the Quran.

The strongest narration cited by proponents of tahrif is the report where Imam al-Sadiq ﵇ calls the dual form in 5:95 a scribal error. Tested against the Imami school's own conditions for establishing doctrine, the narration fails.

The Hadith

Introduction

The report from al-Kafi vol. 8 records Hammad b. 'Uthman reciting Quran 5:95 in the presence of Imam al-Sadiq ﵇. The verse uses the dual form "dhawa adlin minkum" (two persons of justice from among you). The Imam corrects the dual to the singular "dhu adlin minkum" and attributes the difference to a scribal error.

The Report

The full chain and text, verified from the FTS5 corpus:

علي بن إبراهيم، عن أبيه، عن ابن أبي عمير، عن حماد بن عثمان قال: تلوت عند أبي عبد الله (﵇) «ذوا عدل منكم» فقال: «ذو عدل منكم، هذا مما أخطأت فيه الكتاب»

ʿAlī b. Ibrāhīm, ʿan abīhi, ʿan Ibn Abī ʿUmayr, ʿan Ḥammād b. ʿUthmān qāla: talawtu ʿinda Abī ʿAbd Allāh (ʿalayhi al-salām) "dhawā ʿadlin minkum," fa-qāla: "dhu ʿadlin minkum, hādhā mimmā akhṭa'at fīhi al-kuttab."

"'Ali b. Ibrahim reported from his father, from Ibn Abi 'Umayr, from Hammad b. 'Uthman, who said: I recited in the presence of Abu Abd Allah (al-Sadiq ﵇): 'Two just persons from among you.' He said: 'A person of justice from among you. This is from what the scribes erred in.'"

- al-Kulayni (d. 329 AH), al-Kafi, vol. 8, p. 229, Hadith 247

The chain runs through Ibn Abi 'Umayr, one of the most universally trusted narrators in the entire Shia tradition, and Hammad b. 'Uthman, who is generally reliable. This is a stronger chain than most tahrif narrations. The rejection must therefore rest on substantive grounds: the school's conditions for establishing creed, the principle of mu'arada, the later Imam rule, and the reinterpretable nature of the phrase "the scribes erred."

Conclusion

The chain is relatively strong; the narration's rejection rests on the school's substantive conditions for establishing doctrine.

Khabar Wahid Cannot Establish Creed

Introduction

A khabar al-wahid (solitary report) is any narration that does not reach the threshold of tawatur (mass transmission). Even if every narrator in its chain is perfectly trustworthy, a solitary report conveys only zann (probability), not yaqin (certainty). Each narrator, however honest, can mishear, forget, conflate, or paraphrase. Only a mutawatir chain, where the sheer number of independent transmitters makes coordinated error impossible, produces yaqin.

The Principle

This distinction is not a Shia innovation. Ibn al-Jawzi (d. 597 AH), a major Sunni authority, stated it explicitly and titled his third chapter accordingly:

إثبات أن خبر الواحد يفيد الظن ولا يفيد العلم عند السلف وأئمة المحدثين وأنه لا يبنى عليه أصول الاعتقاد

Ithbāt anna khabar al-wāḥid yufīdu al-ẓanna wa-lā yufīdu al-ʿilma ʿinda al-salaf wa-a'immat al-muḥaddithīn wa-annahu lā yubnā ʿalayhi uṣūl al-iʿtiqād.

"Establishing that solitary reports convey probability, not knowledge, according to the Salaf and the masters of hadith, and that foundations of belief are not built upon them."

- Ibn al-Jawzi (d. 597 AH), Daf' Shubah al-Tashbih, p. 27

The Imami school holds an even stricter position. The integrity of the Quran falls under Nubuwwah: it is the fulfillment of Allah's promise to His Messenger ﷺ that He would guard the revelation. A claim that this promise was violated, that human scribes introduced errors and those errors persisted for fourteen centuries, is a claim about whether Allah fulfilled His own word. For matters of aqidah, anything less than certainty is insufficient. Zann cannot override yaqin.

Conclusion

A solitary report cannot establish a creedal claim about the Quran's text; probability cannot override certainty.

Mu'arada: Contradiction with the Quran

Introduction

The Imami school possesses a principle that stands above all others in the science of hadith authentication: every report, no matter how strong its chain, must be measured against the Quran. The Imam who reportedly said "the scribes erred" is the same Imam who told his followers to reject anything that contradicts the Book of Allah.

The Rule

The foundational Shia principle of mu'arada, preserved in al-Kafi and verified from the FTS5 corpus:

إذا ورد عليكم حديثان مختلفان فاعرضوهما على كتاب الله، فما وافق كتاب الله فخذوه، وما خالف كتاب الله فردوه

Idhā warada ʿalaykum ḥadīthāni mukhtalifāni fa-aʿriḍūhumā ʿalā kitābi Allāh, fa-mā wāfaqa kitāba Allāhi fa-khudhūhu, wa-mā khālafa kitāba Allāhi fa-ruddūhu.

"When two differing reports come to you, present them to the Book of Allah. Accept that which agrees with the Book of Allah, and reject that which contradicts it."

- al-Kulayni (d. 329 AH), al-Kafi; verified from FTS5 corpus
The Three Verses

The claim that the written Quran contains an uncorrected scribal error directly contradicts three explicit, muhkam, mutawatir verses:

Quran 15:9: Allah attaches preservation to Himself. If scribes introduced an error that persisted for 1,400 years, the promise was broken.

Quran 41:42: "Falsehood cannot come to it from before it or from behind it." A scribal error is falsehood that entered the text from behind it.

Quran 75:16-18: "It is for Us to collect it and to promulgate it." Allah took personal responsibility for the collection. The scribal-error claim implies the collection was imperfect.

The Imam cannot contradict the Quran, because the Imam's own instruction was to reject anything that contradicts the Quran. If a report attributed to the Imam appears to contradict the Quran, the report must be reinterpreted or rejected. It cannot overturn the Quran.

Conclusion

The claim of scribal errors contradicts the Quran's own testimony of preservation in 15:9, 41:42, and 75:16-18.

Tahrif of Meaning, Not Text

Introduction

The phrase "the scribes erred" was a known idiom in early Islamic discourse. It did not necessarily mean the physical mushaf was corrupted. Ibn Abbas ﵁ used the identical phrase about three other verses, and the Sunni scholarly consensus is that he meant the scribes chose one valid reading over another.

Ibn Abbas on 24:27

Ibn Abbas used the same phrase about Quran 24:27, authenticated by al-Hakim on the conditions of Bukhari and Muslim:

عن ابن عباس، قال في قوله تعالى «حَتَّى تَسْتَأْنِسُوا وَتُسَلِّمُوا عَلَى أَهْلِهَا»: هي من خطأ الكاتب. وإنما هي: حتى تستأذنوا وتسلموا...

ʿAn Ibn ʿAbbās, qāla fī qawlihi taʿālā "ḥattā tastanisū wa-tusallimū ʿalā ahlihā": hiya min khaṭa' al-kātib. Wa-innamā hiya: ḥattā tasta'dhinū wa-tusallimū...

"Ibn Abbas said regarding the verse 'until you seek permission and greet': It is from the error of the scribe. It should be: until you seek authorization and greet."

- Ma'rifat (d. 2007), al-Tamhid fi Ulum al-Quran, vol. 1, p. 370; from FTS5 corpus
The Sunni Framework

Ibn Ashtah explained Ibn Abbas's framework in Kitab al-Masahif. The phrase "the scribes erred" meant choosing one valid reading, not introducing a genuine mistake:

أخطئوا أي في اختيار الأولى من الأحرف السبعة لجمع الناس عليه، لا أن الذي كتبوا من ذلك خطأ لا يجوز

Akhta'ū ayyan fī ikhtiyāri al-ūlā min al-aḥruf al-sabʿati li-jamʿi al-nāsi ʿalayhi, lā anna alladhī katabū min dhālika khaṭa'un lā yajūz.

"They erred, meaning in choosing the first of the seven ahruf to gather people upon, not that what they wrote is an error that is impermissible."

- Ibn Ashtah, Kitab al-Masahif; preserved in Abu Shahba (d. 1402 AH), al-Mudkhal, p. 475

Ibn Abbas's framework shows how this language was used in the early Islamic period. "The scribes erred" meant the community settled on a reading that obscures the deeper meaning. The same idiom applies to the Imam's ﵇ statement about 5:95: the community's reading of "two just persons" obscures the verse's true referent, the divinely appointed arbiters of justice.

Conclusion

"The scribes erred" was an early Islamic idiom for the community settling on a reading that obscures the deeper meaning.

The Sunni Cross-Pollination

Introduction

No Sunni source has any variant reading, error claim, or alternative interpretation for "dhawa adlin minkum" (5:95). All seven canonical readers, all ten canonical readers, and even the irregular readings read the verse identically. The claim of scribal error exists only in Shia sources.

The Scribal Error Motif

The phrase "the scribes erred" does appear in the Sunni corpus, attributed to Ibn Abbas about three other verses (24:27, 17:23, 24:35), authenticated by al-Hakim on the conditions of Bukhari and Muslim. The motif originated in the broader Islamic discourse and entered Shia collections through cross-pollination. The al-Kafi narration about 5:95 uses the exact same framework as the Ibn Abbas reports: attributing a variant reading to scribal error.

Khilaf al-Ammah

The Shia principle of khilaf al-ammah says: when two Imami narrations conflict, prefer the one that contradicts the dominant Sunni position, because the one agreeing was likely said under taqiyya. The dominant Sunni position is that the Quran text is correct and complete. The "scribal error" narration contradicts this position. However, the principle applies to fiqh (jurisprudential disputes), and the integrity of the Book of Allah is a foundational creed, not a fiqhi dispute. Accepting the scribal-error narration means agreeing with a Sunni-originated framing from Ibn Abbas's reports. The khilaf al-ammah principle exists to flag and reject exactly this kind of cross-pollination from the dominant school.

Conclusion

The scribal-error motif originated in Sunni discourse; accepting it means agreeing with Sunni-originated framing.

The Quran the Shia recite today traces by a named, documented chain from the Prophet ﷺ through Ali ﵇ and the Imams ﵈ to the printed mushaf. The reading-manuals count the links by name; the manuscripts confirm the skeleton.

The Upstream Chain

Introduction

The reading most of the Muslim world recites, Hafs from Asim, carries its own documented chain back to the Prophet ﷺ. The chain is recorded by name in the reading-manuals. Every link is a person who recited the whole book to the person before him.

The Chain

Al-Dhahabi (d. 748 AH), a master reciter, records his own chain in Ma'rifat al-Qurra' al-Kibar:

فإني قرأت القرآن كله على أبي القاسم سحنون المالكي... عن السامري، عن الأشناني، عن عبيد بن الصباح، عن حفص، عن عاصم، عن أبي عبد الرحمن السلمي، عن علي

Fa-innī qara'tu al-Qur'āna kullahu ʿalā Abī al-Qāsim Saḥnūn al-Mālikī... ʿan al-Sāmirī, ʿan al-Ashnānī, ʿan ʿUbayd b. al-Ṣabbāḥ, ʿan Ḥafṣ, ʿan ʿĀṣim, ʿan Abī ʿAbd al-Raḥmān al-Sulamī, ʿan ʿAlī.

"I recited the whole Quran to Abu al-Qasim Sahnun al-Maliki. The chain runs back through al-Samiri, from al-Ashnani, from Ubayd ibn al-Sabbah, from Hafs, from Asim, from Abu Abd al-Rahman al-Sulami, from Ali ﵇."

- al-Dhahabi (d. 748 AH), Ma'rifat al-Qurra' al-Kibar, vol. 1, p. 210

The chain runs up from Hafs through Asim and al-Sulami to Ali ibn Abi Talib ﵇. Al-Sulami read the whole Quran to four companions: Uthman, Ali ﵇, Zayd ibn Thabit, and Ubayy ibn Ka'b. The load-bearing link on the Shia reading is al-Sulami's recital to Ali ﵇.

Conclusion

The recited text traces by named transmitters through al-Sulami to Ali ﵇ and the Prophet ﷺ.

Ali Wrote Every Verse

Introduction

Ali ibn Abi Talib ﵇ confirmed that he wrote every verse of the Quran by direct dictation from the Prophet ﷺ, with full instruction on placement, interpretation, abrogation, and clarity.

The Testimony

From al-Kafi:

ما نزلت على رسول الله آية من القرآن إلا أقرأنيها وأملاها علي فكتبتها بخطي، وعلمني تأويلها وتفسيرها، وناسخها ومنسوخها، ومحكمها ومتشابهها

Mā nuzila ʿalā Rasūl Allāh āyatun min al-Qur'ān illā aqra'anīhā wa-amlāhā ʿalayya fa-katabtuhā bi-khaṭṭī, wa-ʿallamanī ta'wīlahā wa-tafsīrahā, wa-nāsikhahā wa-mansūkhahā, wa-muḥkamahā wa-mutashābihahā.

"No verse of the Quran came down upon the Messenger of God ﷺ without his reciting it to me and dictating it to me. I wrote it in my own hand. He taught me its interpretation and its explanation, its abrogating and its abrogated, its clear and its ambiguous."

- al-Kulayni (d. 329 AH), al-Kafi, vol. 1, p. 112

Ali's ﵇ additions to his personal codex were commentary: marking which verses abrogate which. The content of the reports agrees that the Imam gathered the Quran in a gathering that we today call commentary (al-Askari, Riwayat al-Madrasatayn, vol. 3, p. 200).

Conclusion

Ali ﵇ wrote the Quran in his own hand, with the Prophet's ﷺ direct instruction on placement and meaning.

Mass Memorization

Introduction

A single chain, however careful, is still one thread. What stops a reading from drifting over centuries is mass transmission (tawatur). The text was carried by numbers so large in every generation that agreement on a change was impossible.

The Shia Position

Al-Amili stated the school's position in al-Intisar:

إنه نقل بالتواتر المفيد للعلم الذي حصل لنا من خلاله أن لا زيادة فيه ولا نقصان منه ولو حرفا واحدا وهذا هو رأي الشيعة والذي عليه المعتمد عندهم

Innahu nuqila bi'l-tawāturi al-mufīdi li'l-ʿilmi alladhī ḥasala lanā min khilālihi anna lā ziyādata fīhi wa-lā nuqṣāna minhu wa-law ḥarfan wāḥidan, wa-hādhā huwa ra'y al-shīʿati wa-alladhī ʿalayhi al-muʿtamadu ʿindahum.

"It was transmitted by mass transmission, which gives us certain knowledge that not one letter was added to it or dropped from it. This is the view of the Shia and the one they rely upon."

- al-Amili, al-Intisar, vol. 3, p. 314

The text is guarded three ways: written in the codices, preserved in the breasts, and recited on the tongues. If a page were lost, the memorizers held the text. If a memorizer erred, the pages and the crowd corrected him.

Conclusion

Not one letter was added or dropped. The Shia school relies upon this as certain knowledge.

Conclusion

The oldest surviving codices carry the same consonant skeleton, traceable to a single early written archetype.

The Sanaa Palimpsest

Introduction

The one manuscript cited as proof of change is the Sanaa palimpsest (DAM 01-27.1). A palimpsest is a manuscript whose original text was erased and written over. The Sanaa palimpsest has two layers: an upper text (the visible layer) and a lower text (the erased layer beneath).

The Finding

The upper text is the standard Quran. The lower text is a non-standard early codex of the kind ascribed to individual Companions. Sadeghi and Goudarzi concluded that the differences between the upper and lower texts are at the level of morphemes, words, and phrases, and never at the level of sentences or verses (Der Islam 87, 2012, pp. 23, 26).

The palimpsest is a physical snapshot of a Companion codex being retired in favour of the standard text. This is exactly the standardization the sources report: Uthman gathered the people upon one reading and distributed the standard codices. The Sanaa palimpsest shows the process in action, with the standard text written over the Companion variant.

Conclusion

The Sanaa palimpsest shows standardization of the text, not corruption.

From al-Saduq (d. 381 AH) to al-Khoei (d. 1413 AH), the school's leading scholars affirm complete Quran preservation. Every major marja' of the 20th and 21st century has rejected textual tahrif.

Al-Saduq (d. 381 AH)

Introduction

Al-Saduq (Ibn Babawayh) produced the first systematic creedal denial of textual tahrif. His al-I'tiqadat fixed the position every subsequent generation of Imami scholars would build upon.

The Creed

The full statement from al-I'tiqadat:

"Our belief concerning the Quran is that it is the Word of Allah ﷻ, and His revelation sent down through the trustworthy Spirit. It is what is between the two covers, nothing less. Whoever asserts that we claim it is greater in length than that is a liar."

- al-Saduq (d. 381 AH), al-I'tiqadat, pp. 92-93
Conclusion

Whoever claims the Quran is longer than what is between the two covers is a liar.

Al-Tusi (d. 460 AH)

Introduction

Shaykh al-Tusi, known as Shaykh al-Ta'ifa (Leader of the School), ruled decisively in al-Tibyan. His position closed the classical phase of the debate: addition is unanimously false, and the sound position is that deletion is false as well.

The Ruling

From the introduction to al-Tibyan:

"There is complete consensus that no addition has happened in the Quran. As for deletion, the sound position is that nothing was deleted from it either. The reports indicating deletion yield no certainty and should be set aside."

- al-Tusi (d. 460 AH), al-Tibyan, vol. 1, p. 3
Conclusion

Addition is unanimously false; the sound position is no deletion either.

Al-Khoei (d. 1413 AH)

Introduction

Ayatollah al-Khoei produced the definitive modern treatment in al-Bayan fi Tafsir al-Quran (pp. 195-257). He classified the narrations by chain, identified their sources, and dismissed the claim of textual corruption as a tale disconnected from evidence.

The Verdict

Al-Khoei's closing assessment of the tahrif narrations:

"All of this is a tale of fable and fantasy, unsupported by rational proof or verified transmission. The Quran we hold in our hands is the complete, unaltered Word of Allah."

- al-Khoei (d. 1413 AH), al-Bayan fi Tafsir al-Quran, p. 278
Conclusion

Tahrif is a tale of fable and fantasy, unsupported by rational proof or verified transmission.

The Consensus Chain

Introduction

The following scholars affirm complete Quran preservation, spanning a millennium from the 4th century AH to the present:

The Chain of Affirmation

Al-Saduq (d. 381 AH): "Whoever asserts that we claim it is greater in length than that is a liar." (al-I'tiqadat)

Al-Mufid (d. 413 AH): "What is between the two covers is the majority of the revelation." Nothing was added. (al-Masa'il al-Sarawiyya)

Al-Murtada (d. 436 AH): "Our belief in the completeness of the Quran is as certain as our belief in famous cities." (Jawabat al-Masa'il al-Tarabulusiyyat)

Al-Tusi (d. 460 AH): "Complete consensus that no addition has happened; the sound position is no deletion either." (al-Tibyan)

Al-Tabarsi (d. 548 AH): "We the Shia are opposed to this belief" of reduction or addition. (Majma' al-Bayan)

Tabataba'i (d. 1412 AH): "The clearest proof that the Quran we have today is the same that was revealed." (al-Mizan)

Al-Khoei (d. 1413 AH): "Whoever claims tahrif opposes the mind and rationality." (al-Bayan)

Khomeini (d. 1409 AH): Condemned Nuri's Fasl al-Khitab; treated the existing Quran as completely authoritative in all rulings. (Kashf al-Asrar)

Al-Muzaffar (d. 1381 AH): "This Holy Book that we recite today is the same Holy Quran which was revealed to the Holy Prophet ﷺ. Anyone who claims it to be otherwise is either an evil-doer, a mere sophist, or is sadly mistaken." (Beliefs of the Shiite School)

Ma'rifat (d. 2007): "The miraculous nature of the Quran is evidence against tahrif." (Siyanat al-Quran)

Sistani (contemporary): "The Quran that exists in our hands is the same as that which was revealed upon Allah's messenger ﷺ without addition or loss." (Ahdath al-Istiftaat al-Aqidiyya)

Conclusion

The consensus spans a millennium, from al-Saduq to every contemporary marja'.

The Imam's Instruction

Introduction

The binding instruction from Imam al-Sadiq ﵇ turned his followers away from private variants and toward the common codex. The instruction is preserved in both Shia and Sunni sources with identical wording.

The Shia Source

From al-Kafi:

اقرؤوا كما يقرأ الناس

Iqra'ū kamā yaqra'u al-nās.

"Recite as the people recite."

- al-Kulayni (d. 329 AH), al-Kafi, vol. 2, p. 633
The Sunni Parallel

The Sunni tradition preserves the identical Prophetic wording in Musnad Abi Ya'la:

اقرؤوا كما علمتم

Iqra'ū kamā ʿullimtum.

"Recite as you were taught."

- al-Mawsili (d. 307 AH), Musnad Abi Ya'la, vol. 8, p. 470

Both traditions carry the same instruction: the community's received text is the authoritative text. The Imam ﵇ bound every Shia to the common codex. The Prophet ﷺ bound every Muslim to what he taught. The instruction has held for fourteen centuries.

Conclusion

Both Shia and Sunni traditions carry the same instruction: recite as the people recite, as you were taught.

Final Verdict

The Quran the community recites today is the complete text the Prophet ﷺ received. The Imams ﵈ are its unbroken chain. A minority of early narrators held that verses were dropped. Their reports remain in the books but never became the school's teaching.
