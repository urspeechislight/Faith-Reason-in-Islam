Historical Objection · Debate

The Murdered Critics the Chains Cannot Convict

The charge that the Prophet Muhammad ﷺ had three critical poets killed rests, in two of its three cases, on reports the attackers' own tradition grades weak or fabricated, and those two stories cannot hold their dates, while the third case, the one Bukhari carries, reads on its own words as an act of war against a treaty-breaking inciter. From Ibn Ishaq, Ibn Sa'd's Tabaqat, Bukhari, Ibn Ashur, al-Milani's Nafahat al-Azhar, al-Subhani's Dalil al-Murshidin, al-Qummi's Tafsir, al-Kafi, and Wasa'il al-Shi'a.

The Argument Under Examination

Background

The attack charges that the Prophet Muhammad ﷺ had three poets of Medina killed for opposing him, Ka'b ibn al-Ashraf, Abu Afak, and Asma bint Marwan, and that a true prophet could not have done this. The stories are real texts in real books, and no Muslim writer should pretend otherwise. The answer stands on the same tradition's own books, on the chains the graders themselves discard, on the dates the stories give themselves, and on the standard the Imams ﵈ set, where the Book judges every report. The case rests on four premises.

Premise 1

Two of the three killings in the charge, Abu Afak and Asma bint Marwan, survive in two books alone, Ibn Ishaq's Sira and Ibn Sa'd's Tabaqat. Ibn Ishaq's notice about Abu Afak carries no chain of transmission at all, and his notice about Asma runs through Abdullah ibn al-Harith from his unnamed father, the form the hadith scholars call mursal. The raid notices in the Tabaqat reach Ibn Sa'd through al-Waqidi, and the Sunni graders condemned al-Waqidi as abandoned and as a fabricator, among them Bukhari, Ahmad, Ibn Ma'in, and al-Nasa'i. A murder charge built on chains the attackers' own school discards cannot stand.

Premise 2

The two stories cannot hold their own dates. The Tabaqat fixes Asma's killing in Ramadan and Abu Afak's in the Shawwal that follows it, one month later. Ibn Ishaq's story makes Asma's poem an answer to the killing of Abu Afak, so his death must come first, and the Tabaqat's own months put hers first. The lament Abu Afak recited answers the execution of al-Harith ibn Suwayd ibn al-Samit, and the tradition's own authorities place that execution after Uhud, more than a year after Badr and later than both dates the Tabaqat gives. The story family contradicts itself at every joint.

Premise 3

The third case, Ka'b ibn al-Ashraf, is the strong one, and the school does not deny it, because Bukhari carries the report. The answer is in the reports' own words. Ka'b traveled to Mecca after Badr to stir Quraysh against Medina, recited laments over their dead, and aimed verses at Muslim women. His own wife, inside the story, tells him he is at war. The school's sources add his plot to kill the Prophet ﷺ under cover of hospitality and his work as the corrupt judge the hypocrites chose against the Prophet's tribunal. The killing was an act of war against a treaty-breaking inciter, and the ruse that reached him is a weapon the Imams ﵈ bind to war alone and forbid against every protected civilian.

Premise 4

The Imams ﵈ set the standing rule for every report. Present it to the Book of Allah. Take what agrees with the Book, and discard what conflicts with it. The Qur'an fixes that the Prophet ﷺ does not speak from desire, and a report that shows him ordering murder for wounded pride conflicts with the Book it must face. A lone report cannot override a Book the whole community transmits.

Conclusion

The murdered critics charge fails on its own sources. Abu Afak and Asma rest on chains the Sunni school itself discards, and their story family cannot hold its dates. Ka'b's case, on the words of its own reports, is a wartime act against an inciting belligerent. The verdict of murder for verse never reaches the bar the Imams ﵈ set, where the Book judges every report.

1. The Chains the Tradition Itself Discards

Sources: Ibn Ishaq, Sira, pp. 365 and 675-676; Ibn Sa'd, Tabaqat, vol. 2, pp. 30-31 and 37; Bukhari, vol. 5, no. 369

Chains: Abu Afak, no chain; Asma, Abdullah ibn al-Harith from an unnamed father; Ka'b, Thawr ibn Zayd from Ikrima from Ibn Abbas

Tabaqat stratum: the raid notices reach Ibn Sa'd through al-Waqidi

Claim: the two weaker cases stand on chains the Sunni school itself grades weak

What the Three Attacks Share

All three stories come from one shelf of books. The Abu Afak and Asma reports survive in Ibn Ishaq's Sira and Ibn Sa'd's Tabaqat, and in no canonical collection at all. Bukhari's Sahih and Muslim's Sahih left both stories out, and so did the rest of the canonical tier. The Ka'b story alone entered Bukhari, in a short form that carries the permit and the killing and none of the raid's scenery.

The chains the two books name do not improve the picture. Ibn Ishaq's Abu Afak notice carries no chain of transmission, the gap the scholars call mursal. His Asma notice runs through Abdullah ibn al-Harith, from his father, and the father goes unnamed, which is the same gap one step down. The fuller Ka'b narrative in Ibn Hisham hangs on one named path, Thawr ibn Zayd, from Ikrima, from Ibn Abbas, while Bukhari's short form carries none of it. The Tabaqat's raid notices reach Ibn Sa'd through al-Waqidi, his principal source for the expeditions, and al-Waqidi's standing is the next exhibit.

The Man Behind the Tabaqat Versions

The condemnation crosses the camps that matter. Some later voices praised al-Waqidi, and al-Milani records the praisers, al-Qushji and al-Taftazani, al-Dihlawi and al-Saharanfuri, and then he names the graders. Bukhari, Ahmad, Ibn Ma'in, Abu Hatim, al-Nasa'i, al-Daraqutni, Ibn Adi, Ibn al-Jawzi, Ibn al-Madini, Ibn Rahawayh, and al-Dhahabi stand on the condemning side. These are the standard authorities of the attackers' own school, the men whose dictionaries every Sunni grader consults.

The entry in al-Dhahabi's Mizan al-I'tidal, the standard dictionary of graded narrators, carries the verdicts in a row. Bukhari and Abu Hatim rule his material abandoned. Abu Hatim and al-Nasa'i say he fabricates hadith. Al-Daraqutni finds weakness in him. Ibn Adi says his hadith are not preserved and the fault is his. Ibn Rahawayh calls him a fabricator outright.

Al-Nasa'i drew the line hardest. His list of the four liars known for fabricating hadith, the list the whole tradition repeats, names al-Waqidi as the fabricator of Baghdad. The raid notices the attack quotes from the Tabaqat sit on this footing. Their base layer is a man the attackers' own authorities placed among the forgers.

The chain question alone sets both stories aside. Even a reader who waived the chains would still need the two stories to agree with each other. They do not.

2. A Story Family That Cannot Hold Its Dates

Sources: Ibn Ishaq, Sira, pp. 675-676; Ibn Sa'd, Tabaqat, vol. 2, pp. 30-31; al-Subhani, Dalil al-Murshidin, pp. 366-367; Ibn Ashur, al-Tahrir wa'l-Tanwir, vol. 1, p. 260

The chain of laments: al-Harith ibn Suwayd's execution, then Abu Afak's poem over it, then Asma's poem over Abu Afak

Tabaqat dates: Asma in Ramadan, the nineteenth month, Abu Afak in Shawwal, the twentieth

Claim: the story family inverts its own causal order and predates its own trigger

The Killing the Lament Answers

The attack's own text ties the two poems to one earlier event. Abu Afak, in Ibn Ishaq's words, showed his disaffection when the Prophet ﷺ killed al-Harith ibn Suwayd ibn al-Samit. The question is who al-Harith was and why he died, and the school's sources answer with one voice.

Al-Harith fought at Badr on the Muslim side. At Uhud he killed al-Mujadhdhar ibn Ziyad to settle a pre-Islamic blood debt, and the Prophet ﷺ ordered al-Harith killed in recompense, the plain blood-right of the murdered man. Ibn al-Athir says the authorities on the reports agree on this account. So the killing Abu Afak lamented was a lawful retaliation for murder, and it belongs to the weeks after Uhud, in the third year.

Ibn Ashur, reading the verses about the hypocrites, files both poets in that register. Abu Afak sits among the hypocrites and Asma among the hypocritical women, and the trigger for both is the execution of a murderer. On this reading the poems were acts of the party working against the city's new order, and the weakest stories in the attack carry the weakest company.

حارث بن سويد بن الصامت ، شهد بدراً لكنّه قتل المجذر بن زياد يوم أُحد لثأر جاهلي ، فأمر النبيّ بقتل الحارث بالمجذر .

"Al-Harith ibn Suwayd ibn al-Samit took part in Badr. He killed al-Mujadhdhar ibn Ziyad at Uhud to avenge a pre-Islamic feud. The Prophet ordered al-Harith to be killed in recompense for al-Mujadhdhar."

The Dates the Two Books Give

Now set the dates beside the story. The Tabaqat fixes Asma's killing in Ramadan, at the nineteenth month after the Hijra, and Abu Afak's in Shawwal, the twentieth month. Shawwal follows Ramadan, so on the Tabaqat's own numbers Asma dies first, and Abu Afak dies a month after the woman who mourned him. Ibn Ishaq's narrative runs the other way, and it has to, because Asma's poem is an answer to Abu Afak's killing. The lament cannot precede the man it laments. One of the two books is wrong about its own story, and the attack needs both of them.

The second fault is larger. Abu Afak's lament answers the execution of al-Harith ibn Suwayd, and that execution, on the agreed account, came after Uhud. Uhud belongs to the third year, more than a year after Badr. Ibn Ishaq places the Abu Afak and Asma material in the run of stories that follows Badr, in the second year. On the numbers the sources give, Abu Afak mourned an execution that had not happened yet. No choice of date repairs the family, because every choice breaks a different link.

The Conversion the Story Dates Wrong

The same passage carries a third fault. Ibn Ishaq says the killing of Asma was the first day Islam became powerful in Banu Khatma, and that before it the Muslims of the clan concealed their faith. The passage then names Muslims of Banu Khatma who already existed, and the first name is the killer himself. Umayr ibn Adi was with the Prophet ﷺ when the words were spoken, and the Tabaqat's own version has him back in Medina in time for the dawn prayer behind the Prophet ﷺ. A faith that became powerful for the first time that morning cannot already have a companion of the Messenger standing in its prayer line. And the men of the clan, on Ibn Ishaq's own telling, became Muslims because they saw the power of Islam, a claim that sits beside a list of three clan Muslims who existed before the killing. The conversion scene is the storyteller's ending, and the page's own names contradict it.

Two of the three cases now stand on discarded chains and broken dates. The third case is different, and it deserves the stronger answer, because its report entered Bukhari. The war frame is written inside the story itself.

3. The War the Ka'b Report Describes

Sources: Bukhari, vol. 5, no. 369; Ibn Hisham, Sira, p. 365; Ibn Sa'd, Tabaqat, vol. 2, p. 37; al-Qummi, Tafsir, vol. 2, p. 359; al-Majlisi, Bihar al-Anwar, vol. 9, p. 77

Acts the reports give Ka'b: the Mecca journey after Badr, the laments over the Quraysh dead, the verses against Muslim women, the pledge and loan parley

School frame: a treaty-breaking belligerent, dealt with under the law of war

Claim: the strongest case is a wartime act, and the embellishments around it belong to the weak stratum

What the Report Says Ka'b Did

Start with the honesty this case needs. Bukhari carries the report of the permit, and the school does not deny the event. A denial would fail, because the report is there. The answer stands on what the reports themselves say.

The attack's own quotations of Ibn Hisham fix the acts. After Badr, Ka'b went to Mecca, stayed with al-Muttalib, and began to inveigh against the Messenger of Allah, reciting laments over the Quraysh dead thrown into the pit after Badr. He composed verses of another kind about Muslim women. When the party came to him at night, his wife took hold of his bedsheet and said, in the story's own words, that he was at war, and that those who are at war do not go out at that hour. The story knows its own frame. A man who travels to the enemy capital to urge a renewal of the war, and whose own household names the hour a war hour, is a belligerent in the text of the report itself, whatever a modern reader does with that word.

The Plot the School's Sources Record

The school's own sources carry the episode inside a longer confrontation with Banu al-Nadir. The Prophet ﷺ came to Ka'b to borrow blood-money owed to a betrayed ally, and Ka'b resolved, under the greeting of hospitality, to kill him and then pursue his companions. Revelation disclosed the plan. The reply was an ultimatum sent through an envoy to the tribe as a body, leave the land or accept war, with the exit offered before any sword was drawn. Three marks separate this from a private murder. The plot was disclosed by revelation, outside human knowledge. The demand went to the collective that owned the covenant. And the door out stood open.

The Judge the Hypocrites Chose

A second strand fixes Ka'b's political office. The Qur'an's verse about those who seek judgment from the taghut, the false authority, came down over a corrupt tribunal. Ka'b heard cases for money, and the hypocrites of Medina knew where to buy a ruling, while the Jew of the story chose the Prophet's ﷺ tribunal for the reason the report gives, that he takes no bribe and does not rule unjustly. The Imams ﵈ read the verse forward from Ka'b to every judge who rules by other than the truth. The school's sources remember Ka'b as the enemy faction's magistrate and propagandist, a combatant by office, and the Sunni report of his incitement journey to Mecca draws the same portrait from the other side.

War Permits the Stratagem

The one element the attack reads as proof of guilt is the permit to deceive. The school's law of war carries the category in one line, and the Imam's ﵇ own use of the word draws its boundary. What he called a ruse he at once fenced away from the Muslim, the Jew, and the Christian under the polity's protection, over a single dirham of tax. The ruse is a weapon of war, aimed at a belligerent outside the covenant, and it never touches the protected. The Bukhari permit, say what you like, you are free in the matter, sits in exactly that class, a stratagem against an enemy combatant in an existing war. Every legal tradition of war recognizes the same permission.

The remaining details answer to the chain standard. Bukhari's report carries the permit, the party, and the killing. The beheading, the head carried back, and the praise over it belong to the Tabaqat's fuller scene, the raid-notice stratum built on al-Waqidi. The attack splices the two into one continuous narrative, and the splice hides the fact that the strong report and the weak embroidery answer to different standards. Only the strong one survives the graders of the first section.

So the strong case, read whole, is an act of war against an inciter whose own household called the hour a war hour. What remains is the standard that judges any report claiming the Prophet ﷺ ordered a wrong, and that standard belongs to the Imams ﵈ and to the Book.

4. The Book Judges Every Report

Rule: present every report to the Book of Allah, take what agrees, discard what conflicts

Sources: al-Hurr al-Amili, Wasa'il al-Shi'a, vol. 27, p. 112, and vol. 14, p. 356; al-Kulayni, al-Kafi, vol. 15, p. 832

The Book's portrait: the Prophet ﷺ does not speak from desire (Qur'an 53:3-4)

Claim: no lone report can convict what the transmitted Book contradicts

The Rule the Imams Set

Abu Ja'far ﵇, the fifth Imam, gave the House its procedure, and his chain runs through Abdullah ibn Bukayr. A report attributed to the House needs a witness in the Book before anyone acts on it, and an unwitnessed report is held back. The second transmission says the same thing harder. Present the report to the Book of God, take what agrees, and discard what conflicts. The rule names no exception for maghazi storytelling and none for reports hostile to the Prophet ﷺ. On the school's own procedure the Abu Afak and Asma stories fail at the first step. The Book the whole community transmits never shows the Prophet ﷺ answering an insult with a knife in the dark, and it forbids killing any soul Allah has forbidden except by right (Qur'an 17:33).

(33351) 18 - وعنه عن أحمد ، عن علي بن الحكم ، عن عبد الله بن بكير ، عن رجل ، عن أبي جعفر (﵇) - في حديث - قال : إذا جاءكم عنا حديث فوجدتم عليه شاهدا ، أو شاهدين من كتاب الله فخذوا به ، وإلا فقفوا عنده ، ثم ردوه إلينا ، حتى يستبين لكم .

"From Ahmad, from Ali ibn al-Hakam, from Abdullah ibn Bukayr, from a man, from Abu Ja'far, within a report. He said, if a report reaches you from us and you find one or two witnesses for it in the Book of God, act on it. Otherwise, hold back from it, and refer it back to us, until it becomes clear to you."

- Imam Muhammad al-Baqir ﵇ reported through Abdullah ibn Bukayr, al-Hurr al-Amili, Wasa'il al-Shi'a (Ahl al-Bayt ed.), vol. 27, p. 112, hadith 33351

(26100) 4 - روى عن النبي ﵌ والأئمة ﵈ انهم قالوا : إذا جاءكم عنا حديث فأعرضوه على كتاب الله فما وافق كتاب الله فخذوه وما خالفه فاطرحوه أو ردوه إلينا

"It is narrated from the Prophet and the Imams, peace be upon them, that they said, if a report reaches you from us, present it to the Book of God. What agrees with the Book of God, take it. What contradicts it, discard it, or refer it back to us."

- The present-to-the-Book rule, narrated from the Prophet and the Imams ﵈, al-Hurr al-Amili, Wasa'il al-Shi'a (al-Islamiyya ed.), vol. 14, p. 356, hadith 26100

The Portrait the Book Fixes

The Book's portrait of the Prophet's ﷺ speech is fixed in the verses of the Star. He does not speak from desire, and what he utters is revelation. The Imam's ﵇ reading, transmitted in al-Kafi, ties those words to the Prophet's ﷺ conduct itself, his preferring of the people of his house, so that even his partiality belongs to revelation and desire has no seat in him. An order to kill a mother of five for a poem is speech from wounded pride. The Book excludes it before any chain is counted.

Ismah, the school's doctrine that God shields His prophets from sin, stands behind that reading. A reader who rejects the doctrine still carries the whole case built above, because the chains and the dates fall on the attackers' own authorities. The doctrine is the school's account of why the report never fit in the first place.

أُقْسِمُ بِقَبْضِ (9) مُحَمَّدٍ إِذَا قُبِضَ « ما ضَلَّ صاحِبُكُمْ » بِتَفْضِيلِهِ (10) أَهْلَ بَيْتِهِ « وَما غَوى وَما يَنْطِقُ عَنِ الْهَوى » يَقُولُ : مَا يَتَكَلَّمُ بِفَضْلِ أَهْلِ بَيْتِهِ بِهَوَاهُ وَهُوَ قَوْلُ اللَّهِ عَزَّ وَجَلَّ : « إِنْ هُوَ إِلَّا وَحْيٌ يُوحى » (11) .

"He said, I swear by the taking of Muhammad when he is taken. 'Your companion has not strayed,' by his preferring the people of his house. 'Nor has he erred, nor does he speak from desire.' That is, he does not speak of the merit of his house by his own whim. And this is the word of God, exalted be He, that 'It is nothing but revelation revealed.'"

- The Imam's reading of Qur'an 53:3-4, al-Kulayni, al-Kafi (Dar al-Hadith ed.), vol. 15, p. 832

The Authority the Prophet Left

The Prophet ﷺ himself named the standing authority. The two weighty things, the Book and his House, are mass-transmitted through the Sunni and the Shia lines together, and al-Hurr al-Amili states that transmission in plain terms. A story that survives in one strand of the maghazi literature, on chains the graders discard, stands outside that channel. It can fill a book. It cannot convict a prophet.

9 - أقول : وقد تواتر بين العامة والخاصة عن النبي ﵌ أنه قال : إني تارك فيكم الثقلين ما إن تمسكتم بهما لن تضلوا : كتاب الله وعترتي أهل بيتي وأنهما لن يفترقا حتى يردا على الحوض

"I say, it has been mass-transmitted among both Sunni and Shia transmitters, on the authority of the Prophet. He said, I am leaving among you the two weighty things. As long as you hold to them both, you will never go astray. They are the Book of Allah, and my offspring, the people of my house. And the two will never separate until they return to me at the Pool."

- al-Hurr al-Amili, Wasa'il al-Shi'a (al-Islamiyya ed.), vol. 18, p. 19

Final Verdict

The charge that the Prophet Muhammad ﷺ killed three critics of Medina fails case by case, on the sources the attack itself quotes.

First, Abu Afak and Asma bint Marwan rest on chains the tradition discards. Neither story entered a canonical collection. Ibn Ishaq's notices are mursal, one without any chain and one through an unnamed father, and the Tabaqat's raid notices stand on al-Waqidi, whom Bukhari, Ahmad, Ibn Ma'in, al-Nasa'i, and al-Dhahabi condemned as abandoned and as a fabricator. The attackers' own school threw this evidence away centuries before the attack was written.

Second, the story family cannot hold its dates. The Tabaqat puts Asma's killing in Ramadan and Abu Afak's in the Shawwal after it, a month later, while Ibn Ishaq's story makes her poem an answer to his death. The execution Abu Afak lamented belongs, on the agreed account, after Uhud, more than a year after Badr, and later than both dates the Tabaqat gives. The story predates its own trigger. The conversion scene at its end names Muslims of the clan who already existed, the killer among them.

Third, Ka'b's case is a war on the report's own words. The one report the attack carries from Bukhari sits inside the sira's war with Quraysh and their allies in Medina. Ka'b traveled to Mecca after Badr to urge a renewal of the war, and his own wife told him the hour was a war hour. The school's sources add his plot to kill the Prophet ﷺ under hospitality and his office as the corrupt judge of the hypocrites. The ruse that reached him is a weapon of war the Imams ﵈ fence away from every protected person, and the beheading detail belongs to the Waqidi stratum, not to Bukhari.

Fourth, the Imams' ﵈ rule refers every report to the Book. Take what agrees with the Book, and discard what conflicts. The Book fixes that the Prophet ﷺ speaks only revelation, and it forbids killing except by right. A lone report cannot overturn what the whole community transmits.

The murdered critics charge is not established history in two of its cases, and it mistakes a war for a murder in the third. The chains cannot convict, and the tradition that preserved these stories never trusted them enough to try.
