Debate · The Prophet's Household in the Sunni Canon

Why Abu Bakr Struck Aisha Until She Bled

The most beloved ethics manual in Sunni Islam records the day Abu Bakr slapped his daughter, the Prophet's wife, until her mouth bled, because she told the Prophet Muhammad ﷺ to say nothing except the truth. From al-Ghazali's Ihya Ulum al-Din, Abu Talib al-Makki's Qut al-Qulub, and al-Khatib al-Baghdadi's Tarikh Baghdad.

The Argument Under Examination

Background

The popular Sunni portrait of the Prophet's ﷺ household is a portrait without friction. Abu Bakr stands in it as the gentle, soft-hearted friend. Aisha stands in it as the perfect believer, whose every word and act carried blessing. A reader who meets the arbitration reports for the first time meets a different house. In those reports Abu Bakr draws blood from his own daughter, the Prophet's ﷺ wife, inside the Prophet's ﷺ own home. This argument is addressed to readers who accept the Sunni hadith canon as a record of religious history, and it draws on that canon alone. It asks one question. What does the school's own library actually say happened between Abu Bakr and Aisha?

The Reports

The event enters the record on several routes, and four Sunni works carry it whole. Abu Talib al-Makki wrote it into Qut al-Qulub around 376 AH. Al-Ghazali lifted it from him into the Ihya, the most widely read book on the Muslim heart that Sunni Islam owns. Al-Khatib al-Baghdadi entered a longer arbitration version in Tarikh Baghdad with a full chain of transmitters. A fourth recension traced to Ibn Asakir adds a palm frond and a beating. In every version the trigger is the same. Aisha tells the Prophet ﷺ to speak only the truth, or to be fair, and her father reads her words as an accusation against the Prophet's ﷺ honesty.

The Chains

The chains are weak by the school's own grading, and the argument states that plainly. Al-Iraqi, the hadith scholar who checked the Ihya's reports, graded the slap report weak. Ibn al-Subki could find no chain at all for the report next to it, the one in which Aisha challenges the Prophet's ﷺ claim to prophethood to his face. The argument never rests on the chains being strong. It rests on what the school printed, kept, and taught for nine hundred years.

The Quran

The Quran had already said the harder thing. Surat al-Tahrim was revealed about two of the Prophet's ﷺ wives, Aisha and Hafsa, and Sahih al-Bukhari itself names them. The verse commands the two women to repent and says their hearts deviated. Ibn Abbas glossed the word as "sinned." Ibn Masud read it as "went astray." The lexicons fix the verb's meaning, the first exegetes weigh it with chains, and the modern renderings that soften it lose what the first readers heard.

Premise 1: The school's most respected books carry the event in print.

The slap report sits in Qut al-Qulub, the Ihya, Tarikh Baghdad, al-Tabarani's al-Awsat in a shortened recension, and the collections that drew on Ibn Asakir. These are Sunni Islam's own works, written by Sunni Islam's own scholars, kept in print for nine centuries while the school's own graders marked the chains weak. Al-Ghazali used the story as an example of the Prophet's ﷺ patience, with Abu Bakr as the father who lost his temper. Genre matters, and the Sufi manuals license weak stories for edification. The claim here is narrower. The school transmitted this story about its greatest generation without shame, and printed it in every century. And the genre license does not reach the whole record. Tarikh Baghdad is a history built on chains, kept to isnad discipline. Whatever the chains are worth, the texts are the school's own testimony about what was said in that house.

Premise 2: The chains are weak, and the argument does not need them to be strong.

Al-Iraqi graded the slap report weak. Several routes carry the same core event, from al-Tabarani, al-Khatib, and al-Daylami, and al-Khatib's chain names real transmitters of the first generations. The honest use of these reports is printed Sunni testimony, and nothing more. A hostile reader grants nothing to a weak chain, and an argument that hides the grading loses that reader at the first check.

Premise 3: The Quran's own words carry the same portrait.

Qur'an 66:4 addresses two wives by the feminine dual, commands them to repent, and states that their hearts deviated. The Sunni exegetes from Ibn Abbas onward glossed the verb as "turned away and sinned," and Ibn Masud's reading said "went astray" outright. The reports of the slap show conduct of this kind. Aisha's remark casts doubt on the Prophet's ﷺ truthfulness, her own father answers it as an offense, and the Quran commands repentance for hearts that swerved. The weak reports and the strong verse point the same way.

Conclusion: The friction is the school's own record, and the smooth portrait arrived late.

Premises 1 and 2 together mean the reports are genuine Sunni testimony carried in the school's most trusted manuals, honestly labeled as weakly chained. Premise 3 means the conduct the reports describe matches what the Quran states about the same two wives, whose verb the exegetes read from inclining to sinning. Therefore the disagreement between Shias and Sunnis over Aisha's conduct cannot be a disagreement about what the school's own books say. They carry one portrait, on weak chains and on strong. The disagreement is about whether the sources may be quoted, and the honest answer is that they may.

1. The Quarrel Before the Judge

The event enters the record as a family arbitration. The Prophet ﷺ and Aisha had a dispute, and they called in her father to judge between them. The report that follows is the shorter recension, and it comes from the book Sunni readers hold closest to the heart.

What the Ihya Records

Source: al-Ghazali, Ihya Ulum al-Din, vol. 4, p. 133, Book of Marriage, on anger

Speaker: Aisha, the Prophet Muhammad ﷺ, Abu Bakr

Dating: compiled 489-505 AH by Abu Hamid al-Ghazali (d. 505 AH)

Claim: Abu Bakr slapped Aisha until her mouth bled, because she told the Prophet ﷺ to speak only the truth

The Text of the Ihya

Al-Ghazali placed the event in his chapter on how a husband should bear with his wife. He offered it as praise for the Prophet's ﷺ patience. The report's content goes past what the chapter needed for its lesson.

وَجرى بينه وبين عائشة كلام ، حتى أدخلا بينهما أبا بكر ﵁ حكماً ، واستشهده .

فقال لها رسول الله ﵌» تكلَّمين أو أتكلَّم ؟ فقالت بل تكلم أنت ولا تقل الا حقا فلطمها أبو بكر حتى دمى فوها وقال ، يا عدية نفسها أو يقول غير الحق ،

فاستجارت برسول الله صلى الله عليه وسلم ، وقعدت خلف ظهره فقال له النبي صلى الله عليه وسلم لم ندعك لهذا ولا أردنا منك هذا

"A dispute arose between the Prophet ﷺ and Aisha. They brought Abu Bakr in as an arbitrator and witness."

"The Messenger of Allah asked her, 'Will you speak, or shall I speak?' She answered, 'You speak, and speak only the truth.' Abu Bakr slapped her until her mouth bled. He said, 'Enemy of yourself, would he speak anything except the truth?'"

"She sought protection with the Messenger of Allah and sat behind him. The Prophet said to Abu Bakr, 'We did not call you for this, and we did not want this from you.'"

- al-Ghazali, Ihya Ulum al-Din, vol. 4, p. 133

Every load-bearing element sits in those lines. Aisha's remark, "speak only the truth," quietly treats the Prophet's ﷺ words as open to doubt. Her father hears it that way. He strikes her hard enough to draw blood, and his rebuke explains the strike. "Enemy of yourself, would he speak anything except the truth?" The Prophet ﷺ shields her with his own back and corrects Abu Bakr, twice in one sentence. So the Ihya's own text divides the house into three parts. Aisha doubts, Abu Bakr rages, and the Prophet ﷺ protects. Al-Ghazali kept the story for its ending, the Prophet's ﷺ gentleness, and in doing so he kept the rest with it.

The Older Recension in Qut al-Qulub

Source: Abu Talib al-Makki, Qut al-Qulub, vol. 2, p. 421

Speaker: Aisha, the Prophet Muhammad ﷺ, Abu Bakr

Dating: compiled c. 376 AH by Abu Talib al-Makki (d. 386 AH), al-Ghazali's direct source

Claim: the same arbitration, with Abu Bakr's rebuke in full, "You and your father speak falsehood"

What the Older Text Adds

The Ihya's version is an abridgement. Al-Ghazali took his text from Abu Talib al-Makki, and the older recension keeps a sentence the Ihya drops, the sentence that shows what Abu Bakr said about his own daughter and himself.

وَجرى بينه وبين عائشة كلام حتى أدخل أبا بكر ﵁ بينهما حكما واستشهد فقال لها رسول الله ﵌ : تكلمين أو أتكلم قالت : بل تكلم أنت ولكن لا تقل إلَّا حقا ، فلطمها أبو بكر حتى دمي فوها وقال : أي عدوة نفسها ، أو يقول غير الحق ؟

بل أنت وأبوك تقولان الباطل ولا يقول رسول الله إلَّا حقّا .

نصرة لرسول الله وغضبا له حتى استجارت بالنبي وقعدت خلف ظهره فقال له النبي : لم ندعك لهذا ولم نرد هذا منك

"A dispute arose between the Prophet ﷺ and Aisha. He brought Abu Bakr in as an arbitrator and witness. The Messenger of Allah asked her, 'Will you speak, or shall I speak?' She answered, 'You speak, but speak only the truth.' Abu Bakr slapped her until her mouth bled. He said, 'Enemy of yourself, would he speak anything except the truth?'"

"'You and your father speak falsehood, while the Messenger of Allah speaks only the truth.'"

"He did it in defense of the Messenger of Allah, in anger for his sake, until she sought protection with the Prophet ﷺ and sat behind him. The Prophet said to Abu Bakr, 'We did not call you for this, and we did not want this from you.'"

- Abu Talib al-Makki, Qut al-Qulub, vol. 2, p. 421

The added sentence sharpens the scene the Ihya left smooth. Abu Bakr does not only call his daughter an enemy of herself. He swears that she and her father speak falsehood when the Prophet ﷺ speaks truth. The father in the sentence is Abu Bakr himself. The older text also names his motive directly, defense of the Prophet ﷺ and anger for his sake. The same page of Qut al-Qulub sets the rule that a husband may strike only in a way that breaks no bone and draws no blood. The anecdote printed four lines later draws blood. Whether the book noticed its own contradiction, the reader does.

Why He Struck Her

Source: Qut al-Qulub vol. 2, p. 421 with Ihya vol. 4, p. 133

Claim: the trigger in every recension is Aisha's doubt of the Prophet's ﷺ truthfulness

The Trigger

Both recensions agree on the cause. Aisha answered the Prophet's ﷺ offer to speak first with a condition, that he speak only the truth. The Arabic carries a restriction, la taqul illa haqqan, which tells the Prophet ﷺ to say nothing except the truth. Her father heard a wife telling Allah's Messenger ﷺ that he might otherwise lie. That is the exact reading his blow announces, because his rebuke answers the restriction word for word and asks whether the Prophet ﷺ would speak anything except the truth. So the oldest question of this debate, whether Aisha ever doubted the Prophet ﷺ, receives its answer from her own father's mouth in the school's own books. He struck her because he understood her words as doubt. What the next section adds is the rest of the event, in the fuller recensions and the palm frond. The bridge to the Quran comes in section 4, where the same doubt meets a verse that asks two wives for repentance over hearts that swerved.

2. The Blow in the Other Recensions

Two further recensions carry the event with names and detail the Ihya lacks. The first survives in al-Khatib al-Baghdadi's great history with a chain of transmitters. The second is traced to Ibn Asakir and is the harshest of all.

The Arbitration Before Abu Bakr

Source: al-Khatib al-Baghdadi, Tarikh Baghdad, its chain relayed in al-Milani, Tashid al-Mura'at, vol. 4, pp. 105-106

Speaker: Aisha, the Prophet Muhammad ﷺ, Abu Bakr

Chain: al-Khatib from Ibn Rizqah, from Abu al-Qasim ibn Dinari, from Ibn Abi al-Awwam, from his father, from Hafs ibn Umar, from Mubarak ibn Fadala, from Ubayd Allah ibn Umar, from al-Qasim ibn Muhammad, from Aisha

Claim: the blow draws blood from Aisha's nose and both nostrils, and the Prophet ﷺ washes it away himself

The Longest Safe Recension

Al-Milani of Qom preserved al-Khatib's text with its chain inside his documentation of the Muraja'at debate. The chain passes through al-Qasim ibn Muhammad, Aisha's own nephew and one of the seven great jurists of Medina. The report gives the arbitration in Aisha's own voice.

كان بيني وبين رسول اللّه كلام فقال : بمن ترضين أن يكون بيني وبينك ؟ أترضين بأبي عبيدة بن الجراح ؟ قلت : لا ، ذاك رجل ليّن يقضي لك عليَّ .

قال : أترضين بعمر بن الخطاب ؟ قلت : لا ، إني لأفرق من عمر ، فقال رسول اللّه : والشيطان يفرق منه .

فقال : أترضين بأبي بكر ؟ قلت : نعم ، فبعث إليه فجاء ، فقال رسول اللّه : إقض بيني وبين هذه ، قال : أنا يا رسول اللّه ؟ قال : نعم . فتكلم رسول اللّه . فقلت له : أقصد يا رسول اللّه .

قالت : فرفع أبو بكر يده فلطم وجهي لطمةً بدر منها أنفي ومنخراي دماً وقال : لا أُمّ لك ، فمن يقصد إذا لم يقصد رسول اللّه .

فقال : ما أردنا هذا ، وقام فَغَسل الدم عن وجهي وثوبي بيده

"Aisha said, 'A dispute arose between me and the Messenger of Allah. He asked, Whom do you accept as an arbitrator between us? Do you accept Abu Ubaydah ibn al-Jarrah? I said, No. He is a gentle man, and he would rule in your favor against me. He asked, Do you accept Umar ibn al-Khattab? I said, No. I fear Umar. The Messenger of Allah said, Even Satan fears him. He asked, Do you accept Abu Bakr? I said, Yes. He sent for him, and Abu Bakr came. The Messenger of Allah said, Judge between me and this woman. Abu Bakr asked, Me, Messenger of Allah? He said, Yes. The Messenger of Allah spoke. I said, Be fair, Messenger of Allah.'"

"'Abu Bakr raised his hand and struck my face. Blood flowed from my nose and nostrils. He said, You wretch, who would be treated fairly if the Messenger of Allah were not?' "

"'The Prophet said, We did not want this. He stood up and washed the blood from my face and clothing with his own hand.'"

- al-Khatib al-Baghdadi, Tarikh Baghdad, chain relayed in al-Milani, Tashid al-Mura'at, vol. 4, pp. 105-106

The details stack onto what the Ihya established. Aisha rejects two arbiters and names her father, the only one who would favor her. The Prophet ﷺ presents his case, and she interrupts him with aqsid, be fair. Her words again treat his fairness as something in doubt. The blow lands on her face, and blood runs from her nose and both nostrils. Abu Bakr's rebuke now names the offense. No one would deserve fair treatment if the Messenger of Allah did not. The ending belongs to the Prophet ﷺ alone. He says the arbitration never asked for this, and he washes her blood away with his own hands. Two men stand in the room, and the report offers the strike no defense.

The Palm Frond

Source: Ibn Asakir's recension, cited in al-Isami, Simt al-Nujum, p. 191, and al-Salihi, Sabil al-Huda, vol. 11, p. 173, Arabic text preserved in Y. al-Habib, al-Fahisha, pp. 488-489

Speaker: Aisha, the Prophet Muhammad ﷺ, Abu Bakr

Claim: Abu Bakr splits Aisha's nose with his hand and then beats her with a palm frond

The Harshest Recension

The fullest version is quoted from Ibn Asakir by two later Sunni compilers. In it the arbitration collapses into open violence, and the Prophet's ﷺ intervention is the only thing that ends it.

من ترضين بيني وبينك؟ أَترضين بعمر بن الخطاب؟ قالت: لا، عمر فظٌّ غليظ! قال: أَترضين بأبيك بيني وبينك؟ قالت: نعم. فبعث إليه رسول الله فقال: إن هذه من أمرها كذا ومن أمرها كذا. قالت: فقلتُ: اتَّق الله ولا تقلُّ إلا حقاً!

قالت: فرفع أبو بكر يده فرَّثَمَ أنفي! وقال: أنتِ لا أمَّ لكِ يا ابنه ام رومان تقولين الحق أنتِ وأبوكِ ولا يقوله ﵌؟! فابتدرني منخراي كأنها عزلاوان، فقال رسول الله ﵌: إنا لم ندعُكَ لهذا. قالت: ثم قام إلى جريدة في البيت وجعل يضربني بها! فوليتُ هاربةً منه، فلزقت برسول الله ﵌ فقال: أقسمتُ عليكَ لما خرجتَ فإنا لم ندعُكَ لهذا.

"The Messenger of Allah asked, 'Whom do you accept between me and you? Do you accept Umar ibn al-Khattab?' Aisha said, 'No. Umar is harsh and severe.' He asked, 'Do you accept your father between us?' Aisha said, 'Yes.' The Messenger of Allah sent for Abu Bakr and said, 'This woman of mine has done such and such.' Aisha said, 'I said, Fear Allah, and speak only the truth.'"

"'Abu Bakr raised his hand and split my nose open. He said, You wretch, daughter of Umm Ruman, do you and your father speak the truth while he does not? Blood rushed from both my nostrils like two waterspouts. The Messenger of Allah said, We did not call you for this. Then Abu Bakr went to a palm frond in the house and began striking me with it. I fled from him and clung to the Messenger of Allah. He said, I adjure you to leave. We did not call you for this.'"

- Ibn Asakir's recension, cited by al-Isami, Simt al-Nujum, p. 191, and al-Salihi, Sabil al-Huda, vol. 11, p. 173; text preserved in al-Fahisha, pp. 488-489

This recension keeps the same trigger, her demand that he fear Allah and speak only the truth, and answers it with the same rebuke, that she and her father speak truth while he does not. The escalation is new. The first blow splits her nose, and the blood runs like water from the spouts of a waterskin. The beating continues with a palm frond until she flees. Only then does the Prophet ﷺ adjure his father-in-law to leave the house. A report of this shape could never be graded strong. Its value is documentary. Sunni compilers of the ninth and eleventh centuries after the Hijra copied it from Ibn Asakir into works of praise, and it is still in print. The recensions differ in severity, and the section that follows asks the harder question. What are any of these reports worth, chain for chain?

What the Scholar Explained

Source: al-Zabidi, Takhrij Ahadith al-Ihya, vol. 2, p. 413

Claim: the Ihya's own commentator glosses the blood and the insult

The Commentary on the Text

Al-Zabidi, the hadith scholar who checked and annotated the Ihya's reports, explained the two phrases of section 1's text that a reader might soften. His glosses leave no room for reading the blow as light.

(حَتَّى دَمي فَمها) أَيْ خَرَجَ الدَّمُ مِنْ فَمها (وَقَالَ يا عَدِيَّةَ نَفْسِهَا) تَصْغِيرُ عَدُوَّةٍ

"'Until her mouth bled' means that blood came from her mouth. 'Enemy of herself' is the diminutive form of 'enemy.'"

- al-Zabidi, Takhrij Ahadith al-Ihya, vol. 2, p. 413

The school's own annotator fixes the meaning of both words. Blood left her mouth. The endearment-looking word adiyya is the diminutive of enemy, and Arabic uses that diminutive to sharpen an insult, exactly as the translations above render it. With the texts now standing in full, the question moves from what the books say to how they got there, and that is the chains.

3. The Chains of the Reports

A debate page that hides the gradings loses on contact with any reader who can open the books. The gradings are printed beside the reports themselves, in the takhrij literature the school wrote to check its own manuals.

What the Hadith Scholars Graded

Source: al-Iraqi's takhrij as printed in Takhrij Ahadith al-Ihya, vol. 2, p. 413

Grader: al-Iraqi (d. 806 AH), the Ihya's checker, with Ibn al-Subki (d. 771 AH)

Claim: the slap report is weak by isnad, and the adjacent report's chain could not be found

The Grading of the Slap Report

Al-Iraqi tracked the slap report to its two collections and gave his verdict in one line.

قَالَ الْعِرَاقِيُّ : رَوَاهُ الطَّبَرَانِيُّ فِي الْأَوْسَطِ وَالْخَطِيبُ فِي التَّارِيخِ مِنْ حَدِيثِ عَائِشَةَ بِسَنَدٍ ضَعِيفٍ

"Al-Iraqi said, al-Tabarani recorded the report in al-Awsat. Al-Khatib recorded it in al-Tarikh. Both took it from Aisha through a weak chain."

- al-Zabidi, Takhrij Ahadith al-Ihya, vol. 2, p. 413
The Grading Next to It

The report printed beside the slap in the Ihya is the one in which Aisha calls the Prophet's ﷺ prophecy a mere claim. Al-Iraqi's line on it, and Ibn al-Subki's, close that door as well.

وَقَالَ الْعِرَاقِيُّ : رَوَاهُ أَبُو يَعْلَى فِي مُسْنَدِهِ وَأَبُو الشَّيْخِ فِي كِتَابِ الْأَمْثَالِ مِنْ حَدِيثِ عَائِشَةَ بِسَنَدٍ ضَعِيفٍ . قال ابن السبكي : (6 / 310) لم أجد له إسناداً

"Al-Iraqi said, Abu Ya'la recorded it in his Musnad. Abu al-Shaykh recorded it in his book al-Amthal. Both took it from Aisha through a weak chain. Ibn al-Subki said, 'I found no chain for it.'"

- al-Zabidi, Takhrij Ahadith al-Ihya, vol. 2, p. 413

The school's own apparatus gives two weak verdicts and one missing chain. The slap report runs through al-Tabarani's al-Awsat and al-Khatib's history. The arbitration recension of section 2 carries al-Khatib's full isnad through al-Qasim ibn Muhammad, Aisha's nephew. Al-Daylami's collection, preserved in al-Suyuti's Kanz al-Ummal, adds a third route. The name inside that isnad a hostile reader will ask about first is Mubarak ibn Fadala, and the school's own biographer of transmitters answers.

مبارك بن فضالة مختلف فيه وكان يدلس قال ابن عدي أرجو أن تكون أحاديثه مستقيمة علق له البخاري مواضع

"Mubarak ibn Fudalah's reliability was disputed, and he practiced tadlis, concealing a narrator in a chain. Ibn Adi said, 'I hope his hadiths are sound.' Al-Bukhari cited him through suspended chains in several places."

- Ibn Hajar al-Asqalani, Muqaddimat Fath al-Bari, p. 467

Ibn Hajar's verdict is dispute plus tadlis, the practice of naming a teacher without naming how he was heard. Ibn Adi offers hope, and hope is not a grading. Bukhari's suspended citations do not carry the connected chain the arbitration report needs. So the transmitter record and al-Iraqi's grading agree, and the chain question closes on the school's own words. Al-Milani of Qom documented the routes in his verification of the Muraja'at. He notes that al-Tabarani's printed recension stops short of the arbitration scene, and that Ibn Adi truncated the report he cited. His observation is recorded here as fact, his conclusions are left out. The verdict on the chains is the school's verdict. Weak, several times over, and never discarded. The reports stayed in the manuals, were reprinted in every century, and were explained by the school's own annotators. That is their exact status, printed Sunni testimony on a weak chain, and nothing more. The verse that follows carries no chain at all, and needs none.

4. The Quran's Own Words

Contested words. sāghat (صَغَتْ) in Qur'an 66:4. Some circulated English renderings soften the verb to "inclined," as if the verse noted a small drift. The lexicons, the poets, and the first exegetes carry a heavier sense, to turn aside, to go astray, to sin.

Verse. Qur'an 66:4, revealed about Aisha and Hafsa.

Method. Lexicons first, then poetry, then the verse with its exegetes.

What the Lexicons Say

Source: Ibn Manzur, Lisan al-Arab, vol. 14, p. 461, and al-Azhari, Tahdhib al-Lughah, vol. 8, p. 148

Claim: the root ṣ-gh-w means to lean away, to turn aside

The Dictionaries

The classical lexicons enter the verb under the root ṣad-ghayn-waw. Ibn Manzur gives the core sense first.

صغا : صَغا إليه يَصْغى ويَصْغُو صَغْواً وصُغُوّاً وصَغاً : مال ، وكذلك صَغِيَ ، بالكسر ، يَصْغى صَغىً وصُغِيّاً . ابن سيده في معتلّ الياء : صَغى صَغْياً مالَ

"Ṣagha toward a thing, yaṣghī and yaṣghū, means he inclined. The form ṣaghiya with kasra has the same meaning. Ibn Sayyidah, in his section on verbs ending in yā', says ṣaghiya means he inclined."

- Ibn Manzur, Lisan al-Arab, vol. 14, p. 461

Ibn al-Sikkit, the third-century language master, fixes the same sense in Lisan al-Arab's own citation.

وقال ابن السكيت : صَغَيْت إلى الشيء أَصْغى صُغِيّاً إذا مِلت ، وصَغَوْت أَصْغُو صُغُوّاً

"Ibn al-Sikkit said, 'I say ṣaghaytu toward a thing when I incline toward it. I also say ṣaghawtu, meaning that I inclined.'"

- Ibn Manzur, Lisan al-Arab, vol. 14, p. 461

Al-Azhari's dictionary carries the entry with the same core and one addition that shows where the word leans.

وقال الليث : صغا إلى كذا يصغا : إذا مال ، وأصغيتُ إليه سَمْعي ، والإصغاءُ : الاستماع ، وصَغَتِ النُّجوم : إذا مالت للغروب

"Al-Layth said, ṣagha toward a thing means he inclined. I say, I turned my hearing toward him. Isghā' means listening. The stars ṣaghat when they inclined toward setting."

- al-Azhari, Tahdhib al-Lughah, vol. 8, p. 148
The Poets Show the Word in Use

A dictionary defines, and a poet proves the definition lived. Al-Azhari cites the great pre-Islamic poet al-Asha, using the word's adjective for the turned eye of a she-camel.

وقال الأعشى يصف ناقةً : ترى عينَها صَغْواءَ في جَنْبِ مُوقِهَا * تُراقِبُ كَفِّي والقطيع المُحرَّما

tarā ʿaynahā ṣaghwā'a fī janbi mūqihā / turāqibu kaffī wa-l-qaṭīʿa l-muḥarramā

"Al-Asha, describing a she-camel, said, 'You see her eye turned sideways within its socket, watching my hands and the forbidden herd.'"

- al-Azhari, Tahdhib al-Lughah, vol. 8, p. 148
The Quran's Own Use of the Root

The Quran uses the same root in a verse every exegete glosses the same way, and al-Tabari explains its grammar there himself.

يقول : ولتميل إليه قلوب الذين لا يؤمنون بالآخرة . وهو من صغوت تصغي وتصغو

"He says, so that the hearts of those who do not believe in the Hereafter may lean toward it. The verb comes from ṣaghta, yaṣghī and yaṣghū."

- al-Tabari, Jami al-Bayan, vol. 8, p. 10

So the lexicons fix the meaning before the verse of Tahrim is even opened. The root never names a small affectionate lean. It names a turning away, of an eye, of the setting stars, of hearts that follow what they should refuse. The dictionaries fix the turning. The exegetes, quoted below, add the sin. What remains is to hear what the first readers said when the verb fell on two wives of the Prophet ﷺ.

The Verse That Names the Two Hearts

Verse: Qur'an 66:4

Occasion: Surat al-Tahrim, revealed about Aisha and Hafsa, as Sahih al-Bukhari itself records

Claim: the verse commands repentance and states the hearts deviated

The Verse

إِن تَتُوبَآ إِلَى ٱللَّهِ فَقَدْ صَغَتْ قُلُوبُكُمَا ۖ وَإِن تَظَٰهَرَا عَلَيْهِ فَإِنَّ ٱللَّهَ هُوَ مَوْلَىٰهُ وَجِبْرِيلُ وَصَٰلِحُ ٱلْمُؤْمِنِينَ ۖ وَٱلْمَلَٰٓئِكَةُ بَعْدَ ذَٰلِكَ ظَهِيرٌ

In tatūbā ilā llāhi fa-qad ṣāghat qulūbukumā. Wa-in taẓāharā ʿalayhi fa-inna llāha huwa mawlāhu wa-Jibrīlu wa-ṣāliḥu l-mu'minīna wa-l-malā'ikatu baʿda dhālika ẓahīr.

"If you two repent to Allah, your hearts have already deviated. And if you two support each other against him, then Allah is his protector, and Gabriel, and the righteous of the believers. And after that, the angels are his supporters."

- Qur'an 66:4, Uthmani text

The verse addresses two women in the feminine dual and tells them to repent. Repentance is asked only where wrongdoing sits. The two women are named in Sahih al-Bukhari itself, in a report where Ibn Abbas asks Umar directly.

فسكبت على يديه من الإداوة فتوضأ فقلت يا أمير المؤمنين من المرأتان من أزواج النبي ﵌ اللتان قال لهما ان تتوبا إلى الله فقال وا عجبي لك يا ابن عباس عائشة وحفصة ثم استقبل عمر الحديث يسوقه

"I poured water from the skin onto his hands, and he performed ablution. Ibn Abbas asked, 'Commander of the Believers, who were the two wives of the Prophet whom Allah told to repent?'"

"Umar replied, 'How surprising, Ibn Abbas. They were Aisha and Hafsa.' Umar then continued his account."

- Sahih al-Bukhari, vol. 3, p. 104

The school's own sahih closes the identification. Hafsa stands in the verse beside Aisha, and the arbitration reports concern Aisha alone. Al-Milani notes that a parallel report exists for Hafsa as well, in Tashid al-Mura'at, vol. 4, p. 106. The verse's judgment, in any case, covers both women it names. The verb of the hearts is the contested word, and the translation above renders the turning as deviated, the sense the lexicons carry. The first exegetes added the weight of sin, with chains.

What the First Exegetes Said

Source: al-Tabari, Jami al-Bayan, vol. 28, p. 205, with Ibn al-Jawzi and al-Suyuti

Speakers: Ibn Abbas, Qatada, Mujahid, al-Zajjaj

Claim: the verb means inclined to sin, turned away, went astray

Al-Tabari's Own Reading

Al-Tabari opens his commentary on the verse with his own gloss.

يقول تعالى ذكره : إن تتوبا إلى الله أيتها المرأتان فقد مالت قلوبكما إلى محبة ما كرهه رسول الله من اجتنابه جاريته ، وتحريمها على نفسه

"If you two women repent to Allah, your hearts have inclined to love what the Messenger disliked. That was his keeping away from his servant girl, and his forbidding her to himself."

- al-Tabari, Jami al-Bayan, vol. 28, p. 205
The Chains of the Gloss

Then he relays the first generation's own words, with isnads.

حدثني محمد بن سعد ، قال : ثني أبي ، قال : ثني عمي ، قال : ثني أبي ، عن أبيه ، عن ابن عباس ، قوله : إن تتوبا إلى الله فقد صغت قلوبكما يقول : زاغت قلوبكما ، يقول : قد أثمت قلوبكما .

"Muhammad ibn Sa'd reported it through his father, his uncle, his father, and his grandfather, from Ibn Abbas. Ibn Abbas said, 'Your hearts have turned away' means 'your hearts have sinned.'"

- al-Tabari, Jami al-Bayan, vol. 28, p. 205

حدثنا بشر ، قال : ثنا يزيد ، قال : ثنا سعيد ، عن قتادة فقد صغت قلوبكما : أي مالت قلوبكما .

"Bishr reported from Yazid, from Sa'id, from Qatada, 'Your hearts have inclined' means your hearts inclined away."

- al-Tabari, Jami al-Bayan, vol. 28, p. 205
The Reading That Settles the Weight

Mujahid's report preserves the moment the early community measured the word and found it heavy.

حدثنا ابن حميد ، قال : ثنا يحيى بن واضح ، قال : ثنا محمد بن طلحة ، عن زبيد ، عن مجاهد قال : كنا نرى أن قوله : فقد صغت قلوبكما شئ هين ، حتى سمعت قراءة ابن مسعود : إن تتوبا إلى الله فقد صغت قلوبكما .

"Ibn Humayd reported from Yahya ibn Wadih, from Muhammad ibn Talha, from Zubayd, from Mujahid, 'We thought that the words, your hearts have inclined, were a small matter, until I heard Ibn Mas'ud recite, If you both repent to Allah, your hearts have inclined.'"

- al-Tabari, Jami al-Bayan, vol. 28, p. 205

Ibn al-Jawzi's manual collects the same three voices, adds the grammarian al-Zajjaj, and records Ibn Mas'ud's reading in its received form, with the verb zāghat, went astray.

قال ابن عباس : زاغت ، وأثمت . قال الزجاج : عدلت ، وزاغت عن الحق . قال مجاهد : كنا نرى قوله ﷿ : " فقد صغت قلوبكما " شيئا هينا حتى وجدناه في قراءة ابن مسعود : فقد زاغت قلوبكما .

"Ibn Abbas said, 'They turned away and sinned.' Al-Zajjaj said, 'They turned aside and swerved from the truth.' Mujahid said, 'We thought that the words, your hearts have inclined, were a small matter. Then we found Ibn Mas'ud's reading, Your hearts have turned away.'"

- Ibn al-Jawzi, Zad al-Masir, vol. 8, p. 54

Al-Suyuti's compilation of exegetical reports gathers the glosses under the verse.

أخرج ابن جرير وابن مردويه عن ابن عباس في قوله فقد صغت قلوبكما قال مالت وأثمت . وأخرج ابن المنذر عن ابن عباس صغت قال مالت . وأخرج عبد بن حميد عن قتادة في قوله صغت قال مالت .

"Ibn Jarir and Ibn Mardawayh reported from Ibn Abbas about the words, your hearts have inclined. He said, 'They inclined and sinned.' Ibn al-Mundhir reported from Ibn Abbas that ṣaghat means they inclined. Abd ibn Humayd reported from Qatada that ṣaghat means they inclined."

- al-Suyuti, al-Durr al-Manthur, vol. 6, p. 241

The record is closed on every side. Ibn Abbas reads sin, Qatada reads inclination away, al-Zajjaj reads a swerve from truth, and Ibn Mas'ud's Quran reading replaces the gentle verb with went astray. Mujahid's own report is the earliest witness that some hearers did try the light reading, and the tradition corrected them in the same breath. A modern English rendering that stops at "inclined" gives the reader only the half Mujahid abandoned. This is the verse the arbitration reports now stand beside. Al-Tabari ties the verse's occasion to the affair of the servant girl, so the swerving there was loving what the Prophet ﷺ disliked. The arbitration reports show another swerving, a doubt of his truthfulness answered by her father's hand. The fit is close, and the page claims no more. What the two records share is the direction, wives of the Prophet ﷺ in conduct that needed repentance.

5. Why the Weak Report Still Matters

The reports travel on weak chains and the verse does not. A fair question follows. If the chains are weak, why quote the reports at all? Three answers stand in the sources, and each one closes a live route of escape.

The Doctrine of Companion Righteousness

Source: Ihya vol. 4, p. 133 with Takhrij al-Ihya, vol. 2, p. 413 and pp. 413-414

Claim: the canonical books themselves carry the friction the doctrine smooths away

The Friction Inside the Canon

The doctrine of adalat al-sahaba holds that every companion lived in righteousness and needs no examination. The friction inside the Prophet's ﷺ own house is therefore a problem for the doctrine. The canon's own pages keep that friction in print. Beside the slap report, the Ihya prints another line of Aisha's, on a chain al-Iraqi traced to Abu Yala and Abu al-Shaykh.

وقالت له مرة في كلام غضبت عنده ، أنت الذي تزعم انك نبي الله فتبسم رسول الله صلى الله عليه وسلم واحتمل ذلك حلما وكرما

"Aisha said to him once, during a conversation in which she was angry, 'You are the one who claims to be a prophet of Allah.' The Prophet smiled and bore it with patience and generosity."

- al-Ghazali, Ihya Ulum al-Din, vol. 4, p. 133

And on the same page sits the report that Bukhari and Muslim both carry, the one al-Iraqi graded as agreed upon.

وكان يقول لها : إنّى لأعرف غضبك من رضاك . قالت وكيف تعرفه ؟ قال إذا رضيت قلت لا وإله محمّد ، وإذا غضبت قلت لا وإله إبراهيم . قالت صدقت انما أهجر اسمك

"The Prophet would tell Aisha, 'I know when you are angry with me and when you are pleased with me.' Aisha asked, 'How do you know?' The Prophet replied, 'When you are pleased, you say, No, by the God of Muhammad. When you are angry, you say, No, by the God of Abraham.' Aisha said, 'You are right. I only avoid saying your name.'"

- al-Ghazali, Ihya Ulum al-Din, vol. 4, p. 133, a report al-Iraqi grades as carried by Bukhari and Muslim

The reports run in one direction. The weakest report has her doubting his prophecy to his face. The agreed-upon report has her withholding his name in anger, and the Prophet ﷺ reading her mood from it. Between them lies the slap, provoked by a doubt of his truthfulness. A doctrine that rejects the agreed-upon report has abandoned the canon. A doctrine that accepts it has accepted that Aisha's conduct, on the school's own record, ranged from anger to open accusation. The weak reports extend the same direction further, and the chain section has already priced how far they may be trusted.

The Slander Affair and a Charge Answered

Source: Sahih al-Bukhari, vol. 4, p. 375

Claim: the sahih canon's record of Abu Bakr during the slander affair contains no violence

What the Sahih Records

The arbitration event is sometimes confused with the slander affair of Surat al-Nur, the ifk. Keep the two events separate. In the sahih account of the ifk, Abu Bakr's one recorded act is an oath about money, and the Quran itself corrected it.

وَاللَّهِ لَا أُنْفِقُ عَلَى مِسْطَحٍ شَيْئًا أَبَدًا بَعْدَ مَا قَالَ لِعَائِشَةَ ، فَأَنْزَلَ اللَّهُ تَعَالَى : « وَلا يَأْتَلِ أُولُوا الْفَضْلِ مِنْكُمْ وَالسَّعَةِ » إِلَى قَوْلِهِ « غَفُورٌ رَحِيمٌ » ، فَقَالَ أَبُو بَكْرٍ : بَلَى ، وَاللَّهِ إِنِّي لَأُحِبُّ أَنْ يَغْفِرَ اللَّهُ لِي ، فَرَجَعَ إِلَى مِسْطَحٍ الَّذِي كَانَ يُجْرِي عَلَيْهِ

"By Allah, I will never spend anything on Mistah again after what he said about Aisha. Then Allah revealed, 'Let not those of you with bounty and wealth swear,' up to the words, 'Forgiving, Merciful.' Abu Bakr said, 'Yes, by Allah, I love that Allah should forgive me.' He resumed the support he had given Mistah."

- Sahih al-Bukhari, vol. 4, p. 375

The separation matters for honesty. The ifk material is of the strongest grade and shows no violence from anyone. The slap reports are weak and belong to a different event, an arbitration whose date no source gives. A reader who blends the two events strengthens the weak reports with credit they have not earned. The two events stay separate. What remains after the separation is the argument as it stands, in its exact weight. Three objections still come at it, and they are next.

The Strongest Counterarguments

Claim: the two serious objections, weak chains and the Prophet's ﷺ own response, both answered inside the sources

The Objection From the Chains

The strongest objection is the one the premises already granted. The chains are weak, so the event may never have happened. That objection is granted in full, and the conclusion stands anyway, because the argument never needed the event to be proven. It needed two things only. First, that the school's most respected manuals print the event, teach it, and annotate it, which Premise 1 established. Second, that the conduct the event describes matches the Quran's own words about the same two wives, which Premise 3 established. A report may be weak in chain and still be genuine testimony of what the school transmitted and believed about its heroes. That is the report's role here.

The Objection From the Prophet's Gentleness

The second objection reads the same texts the other way. The Prophet ﷺ tells Abu Bakr, we did not call you for this, shields Aisha with his back, and washes the blood away himself. Should that prove the household ideal after all? The Prophet's ﷺ gentleness answers Abu Bakr's violence, and it is aimed at Abu Bakr. It does not vindicate her conduct. The reports matter for another reason. Aisha doubted, in words her own father read as an accusation, and the Quran commands two wives to repent for hearts that deviated. The Prophet's ﷺ conduct in the reports is the standard against which the other two stand judged. The surah's next verses offer the two wives a way back, and the claim above never concerned their final standing with Allah. It concerns the conduct the verse records on the way there, hearts that swerved and needed the way back.

The Charge of Insult

The last route of escape is the charge itself. When Shia writers quote this material, the reply is often that Shias insult Aisha and invent charges against her. The record assembled here answers with its sources. The slap is in Qut al-Qulub, the Ihya, Tarikh Baghdad, and al-Tabarani. The line, you are the one who claims to be a prophet, is in the Ihya beside it. The command to repent is in the Quran, and the glosses of sin are from Ibn Abbas, Qatada, and the grammarian al-Zajjaj, relayed by al-Tabari, Ibn al-Jawzi, and al-Suyuti. Every hard word in the record was written by a Sunni author before any Shia author quoted him. The dispute is with the school's own library, and the library does not negotiate.

Glossary of Key Terms

Isnad: the chain of named transmitters that carries a report from the compiler back to an eyewitness. Muslim scholars judge a report's strength largely by the chain's transmitters.

Da'if (weak): the grading given to a report whose chain has a break, an unknown transmitter, or a transmitter judged unreliable. A weak report is rejected as legal proof and may still be quoted as testimony.

Adalat al-Sahaba: the Sunni doctrine that every companion of the Prophet ﷺ was righteous and accepts no examination of a companion's conduct.

Takhrij: the discipline of tracing a report to its collections and grading its chain. Al-Iraqi's takhrij of the Ihya is the classic example for that book.

Tafsir: Quran commentary. Al-Tabari's Jami al-Bayan (d. 310 AH) is the earliest complete Sunni tafsir that has reached us.

Ifk: the slander affair of Surat al-Nur, in which Aisha was falsely accused and Qur'an 24:11-26 cleared her. It is a separate event from the arbitration described above.

Diminutive (tasghir): an Arabic word pattern that shrinks a word. On names of reproach it sharpens the insult, as in adiyyah nafsiha, little-enemy of herself.

Final Verdict

The reports of the slap travel on weak chains, and the premises granted that openly. Their weight comes from two other places. They were printed, taught, and annotated inside the books Sunni Islam treasures most, from Qut al-Qulub to the Ihya to the history of Baghdad. And the conduct they describe matches words of the Quran that no chain questions. Two hearts deviated. Two women were commanded to repent. The first exegetes read the verb as sin, and the first Quran reciters read it as going astray. The friction in the Prophet's ﷺ household is the canon's own content, from Surat al-Tahrim to the agreed-upon report of the avoided name. The smooth portrait of a house without conflict arrived late, and it survives only by leaving the school's own books unopened. The books answer for themselves.
